IBM 1958 An-niuil Report 
To the Stockholders: 
In 1958, IBM's gross income from domestic operations amounted to $1,171,788,199, 
an increase of $171,356,602 over the previous year. 
Earnings were $126,191,858, a $36,900,269 increase over 1957. 
Earnings per share were $10.65, based on 11,849,023 shares outstanding at 
the end of the year. Earnings in 1957 were $7.54 per share on the 11,841,197 shares 
outstanding December 31, 1957, adjusted for the January 28, 1958 stock dividend. 
As previously reported, under the consent decree entered into with the ' 
Department of Justice on January 25, 1956, the Corporation is required to offer 
for sale, as well as for lease, new punched card accounting and data processing 
machines. It was also required, until July 25, 1958, to permit customers to 
purchase punched card accounting and data processing machines previously under 
lease to them. For the year 1958, the net income after taxes includes $15,636,122 
derived from the outright sale to customers of machines previously under lease 
to them, compared with $3,927,000 for the year 1957. In view of this, the earnings for 
1958 are not directly comparable with the year 1957. Since these leased machines 
have been sold, net income from rentals will not be received from them in the future. 
Congress enacted a new tax law, effective January 1, 1959, which favorably affects 
IBM's customers in that it limits the m a x i m u m amount of manufacturers' excise 
tax payable on a leased machine to that which would be paid on the outright purchase 
of the machine. However, this applies only in cases where the manufacturer also 
offers for sale the same kinds of machines it offers for lease. Therefore, in order to 
afford customers the full advantage of the new tax law, the Corporation announced, 
effective January i, 1959, that customers may again purchase the machines which 
they have on lease. 
Cash dividends of $30,764,992 were paid during the year, an increase of 
$5,357,818 over the previous year. 
The IBM World Trade Corporation, a wholly-owned subsidiary, and its foreign 
subsidiaries, which carry on IBM's business outside the United States, had a gross income 
of $245,721,021 in 1958, an increase of $43,558,968 over the previous year. 
Net income was $34,014,739, a $6,808,884 increase over 1957. Financial results of 
operations outside the United States are not included with the parent company's except 
to the extent of cash dividends received from IBM World Trade Corporation, 
which amounted to $8,000,000 in 1958, an increase of $2,000,000 over 1957. 
The lessening of business activity in the United States during 1958 had a marked 
effect upon net incoming orders for the Corporation's major line of p r o d u c t s -
punched card and electronic data processing machines and systems. N e t incoming 
orders for the full year 1958 for this type of equipment were only 5 3 % of

those received in 1957. However, because the Company had a substantial backlog of 
unfilled orders at the beginning of 1958, it was able to sustain a normal level of 
production. 
IBM derives a substantial portion of its revenues from rentals paid for equipment 
and service. Thus, a large part of current revenues comes from equipment ordered 
and installed in prior years. As a result, the reduced rate of incoming orders during the 
year did not materially affect the financial results in 1958. However, if the reduced 
rate of incoming orders continues, it will affect the rate of growth of the Company. 
To meet the downturn in orders, a number of actions were taken: 
For the small business market, the Company produced Series 50 punched card 
accounting machines. This low-cost equipment is designed to bring office 
mechanization within reach of many more businesses. For the growing intermediate 
computer market, IBM introduced the transistorized 7070 data processing system. 
For the large customer, the Company announced the most powerful computer yet 
marketed commercially by it, the transistorized 7090. Engineering work was 
accelerated on a new IBM electric typewriter, permitting it to be introduced in 
January, 1959. The Company developed data processing equipment for banks which 
can process paper checks of various sizes, "reading" Arabic numerals printed on 
the checks in magnetic ink. Previous IBM systems could handle only standard 
punched card checks. This equipment, announced early in 1959, opens a new area of 
record keeping to IBM systems. 
In 1958, the Company not only introduced more new products than in the previous 
year, but also sharply increased its sales promotion efforts. Stringent cost control 
programs were maintained without disrupting planned research and development 
expenditures, or other long-range programs. 
The Company is encountering increasingly strong competition from established 
competitors and new entrants into the expanding office equipment market. The key to 
the Corporation's progress will continue to be the knowledge, spirit and 
resourcefulness of its more than 86,000 employees, at home and abroad. Their 
response to the difficult and challenging conditions of last year was gratifying. 
We gratefully acknowledge their contributions.

Data Processing Division: 
Throughout industry, IBM punched card and electronic data processing systems con-
tinue to handle a widening flow of business information. Applications range from 
payroll accounting to the mathematical exploration of underground oil resources. In 
banking, transportation, insurance, merchandising, communications, public utilities, 
manufacturing and other industries, IBM machines and systems provide the business 
data necessary for more effective management control. 
In scientific areas, IBM data processing systems are playing an increasingly larger 
role. The four-ton Atlas space missile placed in orbit by the U. S. Air Force was designed 
with the aid of an IBM 704 computer. This satellite was tracked by another IBM 704 
at the Vanguard Computing Center in Washington, D. C. And at Cape Canaveral, 
Florida, still another IBM 704 monitored trajectories during the firing of the Atlas and 
other ballistic missiles. 
As in commerce and industry, the use of IBM data processing systems by federal, 
state and local government agencies continued to become more diversified during 1958. 
IBM systems are at work on tasks ranging from social security record control to voting 
registration. 
At the Brussels World's Fair, an I B M 305 R A M A C performed as an electronic refer-
ence volume by answering questions on world history from 4 B. C. to the present in ten 
languages, including Russian. 
In 1958 the division announced a number of new products: 
• A high capacity, high speed computer, the IBM 7090. The fully transistorized 7090 
is the most powerful data processing system marketed commercially by IBM. It will 
perform complex business and scientific computations five times faster than its imme-
diate predecessor. For example, it is capable of making 210,000 additions or sub-
tractions a second. 
• An intermediate data processing system, the IBM 7070. Like the large-scale 7090, 
the 7070 utilizes recent advances in transistor technology to achieve increased speed, 


===== PAGE 16 =====
capacity and logical ability. It can be used for business or scientific applications. 
• Basic accounting machines, the IBM Series 50. This product line includes a wide 
range of IBM punched card accounting machines designed to meet the needs of smaller 
enterprises. 
During the year a new IBM banking system was developed. This equipment processes 
ordinary paper checks of various sizes by "reading" numbers printed on the checks in 
special magnetic ink. These numbers, which meet the specifications of the American 
Bankers Association, can also be read visually. IBM's check handling equipment is 
connected with standard data processing equipment to make up a completely auto-
mated system for performing demand deposit accounting. 
Electric Typewriter Division: 
The year marked the 25th anniversary of the IBM electric typewriter, and during this 
anniversary year the division shipped its one-millionth machine. From the new type-
writer plant, at Lexington, Kentucky, also came the first production models of the 
6 3 2 Electronic Typing Calculator, a compact, easy-to-operate office machine that com-
bines typing and calculating in one unit. 
In addition, the Lexington plant tooled for the introduction of a newly engineered 
and dramatically restyled IBM electric typewriter for shipment early in 1959. Advanced 
technical features on the new machine include a faster, quiet gliding carriage, and a 
buoyant keyboard for personalized touch typewriting. There is a Standard Model, and 
an Executive Model with proportional spacing which produces letters having the 
appearance of fine printing. 
Supplies Division: 
Punched cards, magnetic tapes and other supplies used in IBM data processing systems 
are designed and produced by this division. These materials are basic documents in 
many areas of business, science, government and education. The punched card has 
become a part of almost everyone's daily activity, for example, utility bills, credit cards, 
motor vehicle registrations, checks and magazine subscription notices. 
Under the terms of the consent decree entered into with the Department of Justice 
on January 25, 1956, the Corporation is required to make available to competitors the 
card manufacturing machines which it pioneered. Many competitors are now using 
these machines. 
To improve further its service to customers, the division has constructed additional 
manufacturing facilities. During 1958, a new card plant, serving the Southwest, was 
dedicated at Sherman, Tex. The division opened another plant at Dayton, N . J.; broke 
ground for another at Concord, Mass.; and acquired land at Campbell, Calif, for relo-
cation of its present facility in San Jose, Calif.

Military Products Division: 
In a period of advancing military technology, IBM continues to serve the U. S. Govern-
ment through development and production of electronic equipment for defense. 
Shipments of electronic computers for use in the SAGE continental air warning sys-
tem continued in 1958 from the division's Kingston, N. Y, plant. They are the largest 
computers in the world today. Electronic bombing and navigation systems for inter-
continental bombers of the U. S. Air Force were produced throughout the year at the 
Owego, N . Y. plant. 
In addition to these major production contracts, the division undertook a number 
of new study and development contracts for the armed forces. These contracts include 
the planned use of computers in bombing and navigation systems for exotic-fueled 
bombers; the correlation of world-wide air battle information and intelligence; the 
simulation of battle situations for the training of field commanders; the processing 
of battle information for unit commanders in the field; and warning systems against 
ballistic missile attack. 
Special Engineering Products Division: 
This division engineers, produces and markets special data processing systems and 
related equipment to meet specialized customer requirements which cannot be handled 
by regular IBM products. It also develops auxiliary devices which make it possible for 
customers to apply standard IBM systems to specific situations. 
The Service Bureau Corporation: 
The Service Bureau Corporation, a wholly-owned, independently-operated subsidiary 
of IBM, consists of a nationwide network of 80 offices offering data processing services 
to customers on a contract basis. 
Its customers in 1958 included small proprietorships and the largest U. S. corpora-
tions. For them. The Service Bureau Corporation produced individual reports covering 
such diverse activities as payroll, sales accounting, inventory control, cost distribution, 
statistical analyses, operations research, and scientific computing. 
Sixteen of the SBC branch offices have electronic data processing equipment in addi-
tion to punched card accounting systems. In addition, the SBC Data Processing Center 
in New York offers customers assistance for the most advanced business and scientific 
applications on a large scale IBM 704 data processing system. In 1958, this center 
worked on such projects as the design of a guided missile ship, the design of inertial 
guidance systems for missiles, a network analysis for a municipal water system, hurri-
cane prediction, and the development of rocket engines.

Stock Option Plan: 
A Stock Option Plan was approved by stockholders at the 1956 annual meeting, 
authorizing a total of 50,000 shares for restricted stock options to be granted to officers 
and other key employees. Under the provisions of the Plan, stock dividends and split-ups 
proportionately increase the number of shares subject to the Plan. The 100% stock split-
up effected May 7, 1957 and the 2^/2% stock dividend paid January 28, 1958 have re-
sulted, therefore, in a total of 102,500 shares being authorized for stock option purposes. 
Of the total 102,500 shares authorized, options were granted for 64,396.65 shares 
through December 1957, and for 10,818 shares during 1958, making a total of 
75,214.65 shares optioned and leaving a balance at the close of the year of 27,285.35 
shares available for future options. At December 31, 1958, fifty officers and other 
key employees held options at prices, adjusted for the stock split-up and stock divi-
dend, ranging from $211.71 per share to $443.00 per share. 
Under the terms of the Plan, the option price is 100% of the market price on the 
date of grant of the option, adjusted for any subsequent stock dividends or stock split-
ups. Options are exercisable in eight annual installments beginning two years after 
their grant. In 1958, 12,242.07 shares became purchasable. Of this total, options have 
been exercised on 3,830 shares, and $813,188 was paid to the Corporation and credited 
to the capital stock account. 
IBM Employees 1958 Stock Purchase Plan: 
The IBM Employees 1958 Stock Purchase Plan, adopted by stockholders on April 29, 
1958, was declared by the Board of Directors to be effective for the first plan year 
starting July i, 1958. All regular employees not under the Stock Option Plan who 
have completed one year's continuous service are eligible to purchase IBM stock through 
payroll deductions not exceeding 10% of the employee's regular rate of pay, at a price 
15 % below the market price at the time the shares are bought. 
Of the 325,000 authorized shares of IBM stock reserved for the plan, 3,996 shares 
had been issued for $1,459,777 through December 31, 1958. This amount has been 
credited to the capital stock account.

Employee Benefits: 
At December 31, 1958, there were 61,159 employees of the Company and its domestic 
operating subsidiary in the United States engaged in more than 600 occupational cate-
gories. During 1958 IBM became one of the first major U. S. corporations to place all 
regular hourly-rated employees on a salary basis. 
Over the years, the Company has sought to compensate its employees properly and 
to maintain benefit programs for the security of employees and their families. In 1958, 
the total expenditure for payroll and employee benefit programs was $455,823,998 
in the United States. 
Following is a listing of major IBM employee benefits, all of which are paid for 
entirely by the Company: 
Group life insurance starts at $1,000 
and increases to $3,000 at the end of one 
year. It is then increased $3,000 a year, to $15,000 after five years. After ten years of 
continuous service the amount is $20,000, increasing to $25,000 after 25 years. 
The Company provides eight paid holidays each year. An employee with six months' 
to one year's service receives one week's vacation with full pay; after one year and up 
to ten years, two weeks' vacation; after ten years' service and up to twenty-five years', 
three weeks' vacation; and after twenty-five years' service, four weeks' vacation. 
Under the Sickness and Accident Pay Plan, employees absent because of illness 
receive regular salary for six months. Thereafter, each case is considered individually. 
The Family Hospitalization Plan covers employees, their wives or husbands and 
their unmarried children under nineteen years. For hospital room charges up to $20 
per day, they receive 7 5 % or up to $ i o per day, whichever is greater. Coverage is for 
70 days. For other hospital charges up to $300 they receive 75 % or up to $100, which-
ever is greater. 
The Family Major Medical Plan covers regular employees, their wives or husbands, 
and their unmarried children under nineteen years. Benefits are provided up to a 
maximum of $10,000 for medical expenses not covered under the Hospitalization 
Plan. In any calendar year, the Company pays 75% of such expenses in excess of $300. 
Totally and permanently disabled employees with ten or more years of continuous 
service receive an income for life. In addition to their sickness and accident pay bene-
fits, they receive full pay for two years followed by a monthly income for life, and are 
eligible for continued hospitalization, major medical and group life insurance benefits. 
Employees retiring after 10 or more years of service receive a retirement income 
equal to the greater of an amount computed exclusively upon length of service or an 
amount based on length of service and earnings. The minimum retirement income for 
an employee retiring at age 65 ranges from $110 per month for 10 years of continuous 
service to $275 per month for 45 years of continuous service, including Social Security, 
with increased retirement income up to a ceiling of $25,000 a year, depending on 
remuneration. Retired employees also receive life insurance, hospitalization and major 
medical benefits.
