To the Stockholders: 
The year 1971 was one of transition for IBM, 
with the phasing down of volume System/360 
data processing equipment production and 
the start of full-scale shipments of the new IBM 
System/370 products to customers around 
the world. 
IBM worldwide gross income and earnings 
grew moderately to record levels in 1971 as 
a result of a continuation of satisfactory in-
creases in gross income and earnings from oper-
ations outside the United States, and a modest 
increase in domestic gross income, with domes-
tic net earnings slightly higher than the prior 
year. 
Consolidated gross income from worldwide 
operations for the year 1971 amounted to 
$8,273,603,369, an increase of $769,643,679 
over 1970. Earnings were $1,078,846,907, 
an increase of $61,325,835 over the previous 
year. 
Earnings amounted to $9.38 per share on 
115,049,434 shares, the average number of 
shares outstanding during the year. This com-
pares with earnings of $8.92 per share on 
114,095,398 shares, the average number out-
standing in 1970. 
Operations outside the United States, in-
cluded in consolidated results, showed gross in-
come of $3,409,235,812, an increase of 
$476,001,728 over 1970. Earnings from these 
operations were $568,924,704, an increase of 
$56,404,386 over the year before. 
During the year we experienced a high rate 
of discontinuances, particularly in the United 
States, of data processing equipment formerly 
installed on a rental basis. Nevertheless, IBM's 
consolidated gross income from rentals and 
service in 1971 increased 11.2% over the previ-
ous year. 
While installations of new data processing 
equipment were lower in 1971 than in the 
previous year, a relatively high level of installa-
tions was achieved in the final quarter of 1971, 
with an increased proportion being on an out-
right purchase basis. This high level of instal-
lations is due principally to the build-up in pro-
duction of IBM's new System/370 product line. 
One of the most important things we did in 
1971 was to invest in improved performance 
for the future. While we were delivering new 
systems in volume, we continued to improve 
our product line to oifer both large and small 
customers more value per dollar of investment. 
For instance, we announced new models of 
System/370, increased the performance and 
versatility of System/3, and added a new 
SELECTRIC Typewriter with outstanding con-
venience features. 
At the same time, we strengthened the 
problem-solving skills of our marketing organ-
ization. For example, 4,400 IBM marketing 
representatives went back to IBM school to study 
new ways to help customers use our equip-
ment more effectively. During a specially de-
signed one-week course, they studied key 
management issues that face business executives 
today, such as financial management and busi-
ness organization. As a result, our marketing 
representatives now have a deeper insight into 
customer executives' problems and objectives. 
We also realigned our field organization to 
back up each marketing representative with 
more in-depth application knowledge. He and 
his customers always could call on the addi-
tional expertise of IBM industry specialists-
individuals with extensive experience in the 
type of business they serve, as well as thorough 
knowledge of computers and their use in that 
business. Now, substantially more of these 
specialists are available to help solve especially 
difficult customer problems. 
While attending to these primary concerns, 
we did not ignore another important corpo-
rate responsibility: responsibility to the national 
interest. IBM continued in 1971 to work on 
significant social problems where we felt the 
Company could make a useful contribution. Our 
efforts took several forms: financial aid to 
charitable and educational organizations, loans 
of IBM people and equipment for the training 
of disadvantaged people, support to minor-
ity businesses, the granting of leaves of ab-
sence or time off from work to IBM employees 
who wish to work on social problems. Addi-
tional details about these programs can be found 
on pages 18,19 and 20 of this report. 
As stockholders know, on June 29 Mr. 
Thomas J. Watson, Jr., relinquished his respon-
sibility as Chairman of the Board. We owe a 
tremendous debt of gratitude to Tom for 
the inspired leadership and complete dedication 
he gave to this Company during 15 years as 
Chief Executive Officer. All of us who have 
worked closely with him know that the growth 
and success of this Company is, in a very large 
measure, the direct result of the foresight, 
creativity and sound business management of 
Tom Watson, Jr. We are pleased that he con-
tinues as a member of the Board, the Corporate 
Office, the Management Review Committee 
and Chairman of the Executive Committee. 
Many skills will be needed to take full ad-
vantage of your Company's potential, and we 
believe we have them in abundance. The people 
of IBM are among the most talented and inno-
vative to be found anywhere. We feel confident 
that their continued ingenuity and dedication 
in all areas of the business will move the Com-
pany forward and assure our continued success.

Progress in products 
IBM is dedicated to offering its customers the 
very best products, programming and services 
that modern science and engineering can devise. 
During the last year, the Company continued 
to improve its products, to expand their capabil-
ities, and to make them easier to use and more 
productive. Here are some of the Company's 
major achievements during 1971. 
t 
New for manufacturers 
The IBM 2798 guidance display unit, for use 
in a manufacturing environment, provides easy 
man-machine dialog. Under computer control, 
the display unit asks for specific information 
and gives the operator step-by-step instructions 
on how to enter the data. The display screen 
then gives him directions that have been pre-
programmed for a given task. 
t 
Horizontal tape drive 
A new IBM 3410 magnetic tape system, 
shown in the foreground, brings advanced tape 
features at reduced cost to users of System/370 
and System/360; and it extends tape storage 
capacity to System/3 users for the first time. Its 
compact design and built-in control circuitry 
save space. 
t 
New S E L E C T R I C I I Typewriter 
The new I B M S E L E C T R I C II Typewriter, an-
nounced September 8, enables the user to quickly 
change the number of characters typed per 
inch. The user can produce reports and corre-
spondence in a lo-pitch type style and do other 
documents in a space-saving 12-pitch style. 
The I B M Tech III Ribbon on the S E L E C T R I C ii, 
in a new, easily changed snap-in/snap-out 
cartridge, lasts approximately 12 times longer 
than conventional ribbons. 
More power for System /3 7 o 
Model 195 became part of the System/370 
family on June 28. Its huge memory and fast 
circuits, combined with the most advanced 
basic operating programs, make it the most 
powerful computer IBM has ever made. 
t 
New display system 
The IBM 3270 information display system 
accommodates a wide range of applications 
—from high-volume data entry to the inquiry-
response flexibility needed in a management in-
formation system. Its screen displays twice 
the information of previous units.

System/y shipments begin 
On September 23—two months ahead of 
schedule—the first sensor-based IBM System/7 
began working for a customer. Before that, 
in July, IBM had announced important improve-
ments in the product. One was the addition 
of a disk module which greatly expands storage 
capacity and extends the range of jobs 
System/7 can perform. The other was a special-
order custom console which permits users to 
display the status of a process, convey instruc-
tions to the computer or make inquiries. 
New programming support 
A new set of basic operating programs, 
called the Time Sharing Option, became avail-
able in June. It adds a fully integrated time-
sharing capability to the IBM Operating System 
... provides remote terminal users with direct, 
immediate access to the central computer... and 
allows the computer to accommodate more 
users and handle more jobs concurrently. 
Throughout the year, IBM also announced 
significant new applications programming 
support tailored to specific customer problems. 
More than 125 such programs were announced 
during 1971. 
New data input devices 
Three new products were announced on 
MarchS.The typewriter-like IBM 3735 program-
mable buffered terminal accepts data and stores 
it temporarily. Later, it automatically feeds 
this data to a distant computer. This terminal 
can be programmed to guide the operator 
through the information-entering process. In 
addition, the new IBM 3505 card reader and 
IBM 3525 card punch bring added versatility 
and speed to the handling of 8o-column 
punched cards. 
Improved data communications 
Effective use of teleprocessing and remote 
terminals depends on fast, reliable communica-
tions between computer and terminals. Two 
new IBM devices, called modems, convert digital 
computer signals to analog signals suitable 
for transmission over private or leased tele-
phone lines. They provide backup, half-speed 
operation if a line cannot handle full speed. 
New aid for doctors and patients 
For insurance companies, health-care plans, 
government agencies and private firms, IBM's 
new Computer-aided Medical Examination 
System can save time in administering medical 
examinations. Using a light pen and an IBM 
optical image unit connected to an IBM com-
puter, each patient can record his own symp-
toms. The system records his answers, plus test 
results, compares them to established norms, 
then prints out a summary to guide a physi-
cian's further examination and diagnosis. 
New communicating typewriter 
The IBM Mag Card SELECTRIC Typewriter, 
announced in 1969, was given a new com-
municating capability in 1971. This develop-
ment, which can be installed on any Mag 
Card SELECTRIC Typewriter, now lets a cus-
tomer use it not only as a typewriter, but also as 
a device to transmit information to similar 
machines over telephone lines, or as a device for 
remote entry or retrieval of data in a computer. 
New system for stockbrokers 
The IBM 3670 brokerage communications 
system was announced on September 21. 
Linked by modern communication facilities, 
thousands of desk-top terminals can be 
combined with a centralized IBM System/370 
or System/360 to totally integrate a brokerage 
firm's branch office operations. A broker can 
then use his individual terminal to display in-
formation about customer portfolios, sta-
tistics on securities, analysts' opinions, and cur-
rent market conditions. He can also initiate 
buy or sell orders which the system checks and 
transmits to the floor of the exchange. The 
same terminal also provides confirmation to the 
broker that the order has been executed. 
New System/370 Model 1 3 5 
In 1971, System/370's advanced technology 
was extended to medium-size and smaller users 
of information systems. The new Model 135 
brings powerful communications and large data-
handling capabilities to smaller organizations 
that need to connect a central computer to many 
remote locations. 
System/370 shipments begin 
Just seven months after announcement, and 
ahead of schedule, System/370 customer ship-
ments began. First to be installed was a Model 
155 in early February. In April, a Model 165 
was installed and, in July, the first Model 145 
began operating for a customer. By year end, 
more than 1,300 System/370's had been 
shipped. The photo on the facing page shows a 
System/370 being tested before shipment.

IBM products at work 
'World Peace Through World Trade" was a goal of Mr. Thomas J. Watson, Sr., and he began promoting world trade 
when he joined the Company in its early years. Today, I B M ' S products contribute to individual well being and the quality 
of Hfe of people in more than i oo different countries around the globe. 
These benefits may result from the use of IBM products to improve economic conditions, health, living conditions, or com-
fort, or just to handle the masses of data on which our society functions. A few examples are described in the following pages. 
Computer benefits hotel guests. 
At its i,ooo-room hotel in Madrid, Spain, 
one of the largest hotel organizations in Europe 
uses an IBM System/3 computer to assure 
guests efficient service, whether they are travel-
ing alone or as part of a convention or tour 
group. The computer helps improve handling of 
reservations, billing and administrative func-
tions, as well as management of a large inven-
tory of supplies. 
Computer protects composers' rights. 
Two organizations in The Netherlands pro-
tect the performing and reproduction rights 
to musical compositions. They keep track of the 
use of about 1,000,000 musical compositions 
and then coordinate this information among co-
operating countries. They use an IBM com-
puter to keep accurate records and compensate 
each artist in proper proportion to the use of 
his creative work. 
More efficient food 
production. 
In Denmark, The Danish Farmers' Data 
Center uses IBM computers and teleprocessing 
to help improve the management of farms and 
food processing and distribution operations. 
The goal of the Data Center is to combine future 
farm production and cost estimates, expected 
sales, probable prices and other factors in 
complex mathematical models that help guide 
decision making. 
Building 
a stronger nation. 
In Colombia, South America, an IBM infor-
mation system is used to plan for the future 
and guide the nation's economic development. 
A computer and remote terminals help with 
the analysis of statistics on electric power pro-
duction, industrial and agricultural output, 
commerce and housing.

Computers help cut manufacturing costs. 
A manufacturer of large cast, forged and 
machined steel products uses an IBM 2790 data 
communications system to help reduce pro-
duction lead times, control and monitor produc-
tion costs, and improve customer service. 
With this system, job status information is re-
corded in small data-entry devices located in 
numerous shop areas. This data is routed 
through communications facilities to a central 
computer data base. Processed data is sent on 
a timely basis to the manufacturing floor at 
each plant, and to marketing, financial and ad-
ministrative management. 
A notebook you can talk to. 
IBM's portable dictating unit goes along 
when this daily newspaper editor goes after a 
story. He records on-the-spot news, quickly 
and easily, on the reusable magnetic belt in the 
unit. IBM Magnetic Tape SELECTRIC Type-
writers and a Magnetic Tape SELECTRIC Com-
poser are used to prepare copy for reproduction. 
Quick delivery and accurate billing. 
At a meat processing company in northern 
Italy, 2,000 orders a day are received, processed 
and shipped with the help of IBM computers. 
Billing used to have to wait for the results 
of weighing each order (every one varies). Now 
an IBM process-control computer, tied to 
electronic scales, automatically calculates the 
proper billing and has the invoice ready for each 
order when it's loaded on the delivery truck. 
Helping youth decide. 
In Flint, Michigan, high school students can 
use an IBM experimental computer system 
to examine career paths. A student can examine 
any of 400 vocations and 6,000 related courses 
of study on the screen of an IBM optical image 
unit. The student then can instruct the system to 
print out details on selected careers for later 
discussions with a guidance counselor and parents. 
Computers in medicine. 
In research on cancer, Sloan-Kettering 
Memorial Hospital uses IBM computers to help 
doctors record and analyze medical data. Else-
where, at research institutes, medical centers and 
hospitals, computers help doctors diagnose 
heart disease, reduce the time required to exam-
ine patients, and improve the efficiency of 
providing quality medical care.

People make the difference 
The key to I B M ' S success lies in maintaining technological leadership and the best possible service to its customers. 
Throughout the Company, IBM people are responding eagerly to this dual challenge. For example: research scientists are 
exploring the nature of matter in a search for better ways to control and use electricity; manufacturing engineers are 
seeking new ways to increase product reliability; marketing representatives and systems engineers help customers handle 
more work with their IBM systems; systems programmers are improving further the productivity and ease of use of 
our equipment; branch office administrators are busy coordinating Companywide activities that contribute to successful 
installation and performance. 
The people pictured in this section exemplify the desire and ability of iBMers everywhere to create new and improved 
IBM products and to help customers make better use of them. 
Here's a good example of IBM's extra con-
cern for customer problems: It was 3:30 p.m., 
Saturday, when a phone rang in an empty 
office at the Greencastle, Indiana, plant of IBM's 
Information Records Division. Press operator 
R. D. Ross (left) answered and learned that a 
customer in St. Louis needed 10,000 forms by 
Monday morning. Ross teamed up with 
Larry Corbin to expedite weekend production 
of the forms. Monday morning at 7 : 0 0 a.m. 
the forms were ready: printed, cut and packaged 
for shipment, and delivered to the customer 
that same day.

Social responsibility-and what IBM is doing to help 
I B M has tried to act on the belief that a profitmaking corporation has a responsibility to the society in which it works. 
Therefore, the Company has regularly been a major corporate contributor to education, hospitals, youth groups and com-
munity organizations. In recent years we have emphasized aid to minority groups such as blacks, Indians, Mexican-
Americans, Puerto Ricans. 
Our efforts begin within the Company. As an equal opportunity employer, we hire, promote and compensate each employee 
on the basis of his or her ability to help the Company, regardless of color, race, religion, sex, age or national origin. This, we 
believe, makes IBM a stronger company. In 1 9 7 1 , our total employment in the United States included 12,000 members of 
minority groups, including about 9,000 blacks. In the last five years the number of black and other minority managers 
increased over four times to a total of over 600. During that same period, we tripled the number of female managers to more 
than 450 and doubled the number of female professionals to more than 4,000. 
I B M support to educational organizations includes help for students from minority groups. We have supported the 
United Negro College Fund since it was formed in the mid-1940's. In 1968, IBM became the first corporate sponsor in the 
New York Urban League's Street Academy program to prepare young people for entrance into college preparatory 
schools and colleges. Of the 2 54 graduates of the IBM-sponsored Academy, 1 7 3 have gone on to college. 
We also support organizations that provide job training to members of minority groups. For example, in the Watts 
area of Los Angeles, IBM, with the Urban League and the Bank of America Foundation, has run a data processing training 
center for the past three years. We will support a data processing training center in Harlem, in cooperation with the 
Opportunities Industrialization Center, to begin operation in the spring of 197 2. In addition, the Company makes its 
personnel and equipment available to other selected organizations for job-training and management programs. 
Since 1968, IBM has operated a successful manufacturing operation of its own in the Bedford-Stuy vesant section of Brooklyn, 
New York, a predominantly black neighborhood. Of the total work force of 400, about 300 are local residents. We also 
help minority-owned companies, often with business advice and assistance from IBM people. Over 200 minority manufac-
turing and service firms now are regular suppliers to IBM. We also try to help build the financial resources of minority 
communities by means of deposits in minority-owned banks. 
Several years ago IBM began to grant time off from work or leaves of absence to employees who wanted to devote part or 
all of their time to pressing social problems. During 1 9 7 1 , over 100 IBM employees were on such leaves, most with pay. 
Many were helping minority groups, others were working in government or in civic organizations. 
Concern for the Company's social responsibility begins, as it must, at the highest levels of IBM management. A corporate 
committee, which includes a member of the Board of Directors, continually monitors IBM performance in this area. 
Other corporate staff groups are assigned to specific activities such as pollution control and equal opportunity employment. 
Such areas will continue to receive high priority, consistent with IBM'S responsibilities to customers, stockholders 
and employees.

Litigation 
In January 1969, the Department of Justice 
filed a civil antitrust complaint against IBM 
under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, 
charging the Company with monopolizing 
commerce in "general purpose digital com-
puters" in the United States. The government 
requests that IBM be required to stop certain 
practices which it claims lead to a monopoly, 
and asks the court to give such other relief by 
way of divorcement, divestiture and reorganiza-
tion as it may deem necessary to dissipate 
the effects of the practices charged. 
In December 1968, Control Data Cor-
poration instituted a lawsuit against IBM alleg-
ing violations of the Federal antitrust laws. 
In September 1970, Greyhound Computer 
Corporation, which had earlier filed a lawsuit in 
connection with IBM's 1969 announcement 
changing the way it charges for and supports its 
data processing equipment, amended its suit 
to include charges of violations of the Federal 
antitrust laws. In January 1972, The Telex 
Corporation instituted a lawsuit against IBM 
alleging violations of the Federal antitrust 
laws. These complaints seek injunctive relief 
and damages to be fixed by the court. 
In April 1970, Xerox Corporation filed a 
suit against IBM charging patent infringement 
and misuse of confidential information in 
connection with the IBM Copier. 
IBM denies the charges in all of the foregoing 
cases and is engaged in preparing its defenses 
against them. 
In May 1971, the Motor Replacement Com-
pany, Inc. suit, which sought damages arising 
primarily from IBM's 1969 announcement, was 
dismissed pursuant to settlement on a basis 
which the Company considers favorable.
