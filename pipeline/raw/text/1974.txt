High inflation rates experienced througii-
out the world during 1974 affected every 
segment of IBM's business. Nevertheless, 
worldwide gross income and earnings 
reached record levels, with a significant 
portion of the earnings increase flowing 
from the record amount of data processing 
equipment purchased outright by our 
customers. Gross income from domestic 
and overseas operations showed good in-
creases. Domestic earnings were also 
well ahead, while earnings from overseas 
operations were only modestly higher 
than 1973. 
Consolidated gross income from 
worldwide operations for 1974 amounted 
to $12,675,291,832, an increase of 
$1,682,049,879 over 1973. Net earnings were 
$1,837,639,361, an increase of $262,172,306 
over the previous year. 
Earnings amounted to $12.47 per share 
on 147,400,733 shares, the average num-
ber of shares outstanding during the year.

The amount of data processing equip-

lower in the fourth quarter of 1974 than 
the record level attained in the comparable 
1973 quarter. Although outright purchases 
of data processing equipment for the full 
year 1974 were the highest in our history, the 
previously reported lower level of pur-
chase continued during the fourth quarter. 
Should this trend continue, income com-
parisons for reporting periods in 1975 would 
be significantly affected. 
Worsening economic conditions 
throughoutthe world were reflected in lower 
incoming orders in the latter part of the 
year. This lower order rate, combined with 
record shipments and installations for 1974, 
resulted in a backlog at year-end some-
what below that at the end of 1973. 
High inflation rates in all major coun-
tries are causing severe cost and expense 
pressures throughout the business. The 
Company has counteracted some of the ef-
fects of such pressures through price 
increases and improvements in productivity 
and is continuing its emphasis on improv-
ing operating efficiencies. 
The Company implemented in 1974 a 
special arrangement under which employees 
with 25 years' service may leave vol-
untarily with special compensation for a 
period of up to four years. In a further effort 
to reduce manpower resources, new hiring 
will be limited to filling needs for skills 
which cannot be filled from within. 
Increasingly, the Company is using its 
own products and investing in other tools 
that raise productivity. Many other com-
panies are doing the same and it is their 
growing use of IBM products to help 
them control costs that helped us, in spite 
of the economic downturn, to demon-
strate continued sales strength during 1974. 
A number of new products were intro-
duced in 1974 and played an important 
part in the year's activities. They are de-
scribed elsewhere in this report. 
We were very pleased that on January 
24,1975, the appeals court overturned the 
lower court's decision in the Telex suit and 
ruled that IBM had not violated the anti-
trust laws. 
Other litigation developments included 
the appeal of the Greyhound suit, and we 
are awaiting the result. 
In the Department of Justice case, the 
Department's brief filed in October 1974 
reaffirmed its position that it is necessary 
to break up IBM to restore competitive 
conditions. The trial has been scheduled to 
begin on February 18,1975. IBM contin-
ues to believe that there is no correlation be-
tween bigness and badness, or between 
success and monopoly. In its brief filed on 
January 15,1975, IBM characterized the 
lawsuit as an attempt to change the whole 
thrust and purpose of the Sherman Act so 
that it fetters rather than encourages com-
petition. We reaffirm our belief in the 
integrity of the Company and its people; 
that IBM's business position has been 
achieved ethically; and that our legal posi-
tion is sound. 
In July, IBM petitioned the Federal Com-
munications Commission to become a 55 
percent partner, along with COMSAT General 
Corporation, which would be a 45 percent 
partner, in CML Satellite Corporation. CML 
has been developing plans for a domes-
tic satellite system to relay voice, image and 
data communications. A number of or-
ganizations opposed the petition, arguing 
that the joint venture might have an anti-
competitive impact on the satellite commu-
nications business. In reply, IBM stated 
that approval of the petition would promote 
FCC policies by improving domestic sat-
ellite communications services and increas-
ing rather than decreasing competition. 
On January 23,1975 the FCC, while turning 
down the petition as proposed, said IBM 
and COMSAT General should not be disqual-
ified as domestic satellite applicants 
and also said it would entertain applica-
tions from IBM and COMSAT General 
under alternative arrangements. IBM will 
study the options offered to determine 
whether any of them offers a viable busi-
ness opportunity for the Company. 
Before a Congressional subcommittee, 
in advertisements and elsewhere, IBM made 
significant position statements last year in 
another area of public interest: privacy and 
data security. Privacy principles that the 
Company endorsed in 1974 are consistent 
with the privacy bill that became law in 
January 1975. Data security —the safeguard-
ing of data stored in a computer— is a 
technical problem, and will be, as the Com-
pany stated in 1974, one of the basic de-
sign criteria for its data processing products. 
IBM's achievements in a difficult year re-
flected the individual efforts of the men 
and women who develop, manufacture, mar-
ket and service our products around the 
world. Their talent, creativity and dedication 
remain our best assurance that IBM is still 
the company of the future. We appreciate 
and gratefully acknowledge their outstand-
ing efforts.

IBM's first automatic digital computer was 
completed in 1944; its first large-scale system 
was introduced in 1952. In the beginning, 
computers were used primarily by engineers 
and scientists in industry, government and 
universities. Then a number of developments 
added new flexibility to the machines. It be-
came easier to get information into and out of 
them. Their speed and versatility improved. 
Programming methods were simplified. As a 
result, it became possible to use computers 
in increasing numbers of business applications. 
The third-generation systems of the mid-
1960s introduced lower throughput costs and 
unprecedented flexibility, with such periph-
eral equipment as graphic display units and 
optical scanners. More importantly, stand-
ard architecture allowed customers to move 
from smaller to more powerful models with 
little or no change in programs or procedures. 
The faster, more powerful System/370, intro-
duced in 1970, has further reduced throughput 
costs. It provides a wide variety of advanced 
functional capabilities, particularly for com-
puter operation via remote terminals. With these 
and IBM's smaller and specialized systems, 
more and more users in business, government, 
research and professions are finding com-
puter usage feasible for their needs. Today, 
IBM's systems are being put to work by cus-
tomers in 129 countries around the world.

For a 12-month period starting November 
1973, IBM's major U.S. plant, laboratory and 
headquarters locations saved 21,150,000 
equivalent gallons of oil — enough to serve 
approximately 18,000 homes in the North-
east for a year, and 413,400,000 KWH of elec-
tricity—enough to serve 45,000 homes per 
year. The energy savings represent approxi-
mately $12,400,000, and reductions of 
31.3 percent for fuel and 22.3 percent for 
electricity from pre-conservation levels 
of consumption. These surprising results 
were achieved with outstanding employee 
cooperation in a program of changing op-
erating modes of building systems, turning 
down thermostats and switching off un-
needed equipment and lights. Now, IBM's 
energy conservation efforts will continue 
with two new phases added to the cur-
rent program. The first new goal is to re-
duce energy consumption 30 to 40 percent 
in new buildings by revising design criteria 
and using computer analysis and simulation 
techniques to balance good architec-
ture with efficiency. The second is to con-
tinue installation of System/7 computers at 
major locations to monitor and control 
power use. Similar energy management pro-
grams are now in operation in certain IBM 
and customer locations. Based on experi-
ence from these initial installations, a given 
location will save an estimated additional 
10 to 12 percent as the System/7 computer 
becomes operational.

Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles of Consolidation: The con-
solidated financial statements include the 
accounts of International Business Machines 
Corporation and its domestic and foreign 
subsidiary companies. 
Translation of Foreign Currency Amounts: 
Foreign assets and liabilities are translated 
to U.S. dollars at approximate year-end 
exchange rates, except that plant, rental 
machines and other property and long-term 
debt are translated at approximate rates 
prevailing when acquired or incurred. 
Income and expense items are translated at 
average rates of exchange prevailing during 
the year, except depreciation which is cal-
culated at the approximate rates prevailing 
when the properties were acquired. Ex-
change adjustments are included in earn-
ings currently. 
Gross Income: Gross income is recog-
nized from sales when the product is shipped 
or in certain cases upon customer accept-
ance, from rentals in the month in which they 
accrue, and from services over the contrac-
tual period or as the services are performed. 
Depreciation: With minor exceptions, 
depreciation of domestic properties is com-
puted using the sum of the years-digits 
method. Depreciation of foreign properties 
is computed using either accelerated 
methods or the straight-line method. 
Retirement Plans: Current service costs 
are accrued currently. Prior service costs 
resulting from improvements in the plans are 
amortized generally over 10 years, except 
that certain foreign subsidiaries use longer 
periods not exceeding 25 years. 
Expenses: Marketing expenses and 
development and engineering expenses are 
charged against income as they are incurred. 
Income Taxes: Income tax expense is 
based on reported earnings before income 
taxes and thus includes the effects of 
timing differences between reported and 
taxable earnings which arise because 
certain transactions are included in taxable 
earnings in other years. Investment tax 
credits are deferred and amortized as a re-
duction of income tax expense over the 
average useful life of the applicable classes 
of property.

Litigation 
In January 1969, the Department of 
Justice filed a civil antitrust complaint 
against IBM under Section 2 of the Sher-
man Antitrust Act, charging the Company 
with monopolizing commerce in general 
purpose digital computers in the United 
States. 
in October 1974, the Department of 
Justice filed its pre-trial brief and on 
November 1,1974 filed a motion to amend 
its complaint to add allegations of at-
tempted monopolization of trade in various 
plug compatible peripheral devices which 
attach to IBM computer systems. Over 
IBM's opposition the Court granted the mo-
tion in January 1975, thereby considerably 
broadening the issues in the case. 
The government continues to seek 
divestiture relief, requesting that IBM be 
reorganized into several independent 
and competing organizations and that IBM 
be enjoined from continuing its alleged 
monopolistic practices. A trial date origi-
nally scheduled for October 1974 was 
postponed at the government's request 
and a new trial date of February 18,1975 
has been scheduled. 
In April 1970, Xerox Corporation filed 
a lawsuit against IBM charging patent in-
fringement and misuse of confidential 
information in connection with the IBM 
Copier I. In August 1973, Xerox filed a sup-
plemental lawsuit concerning the IBM 
Copier II. In July 1974, the United States 
District Court for the Southern District of 
New York consolidated the two suits. 
Xerox seeks injunctive relief and damages 
in an unspecified amount. 
In January and March 1972, the Telex 
Corporation instituted two lawsuits against 
IBM alleging violations of the federal anti-
trust laws and claiming damages, which 
after trebling aggregated $1,084 million. 
These resulted in an amended judgment in 
November 1973 in which Telex was awarded 
trebled damages of $259.5 million and cer-
tain injunctive relief. IBM's appeal of this 
decision was argued on May 14,1974 in the 
United States Circuit Court of Appeals for 
the Tenth Circuit, which issued its ruling on 
January 24,1975. The Court of Appeals 
found that IBM's actions constituted valid 
competitive practice and were neither 
predatory nor otherwise violative of the 
antitrust laws and reversed the lower 
court's decision with directions to enter 
judgment in favor of IBM. With respect 
to IBM's counterclaim. Telex was found by 
the lower court to have infringed IBM 
copyrights and misappropriated IBM trade 
secrets. Its judgment on the counterclaim 
awarded IBM damages of $21.9 million, re-
quired Telex to return IBM confidential 
documents and placed certain restraints on 
Telex's use of former IBM employees. The 
Court of Appeals confirmed the counterclaim 
judgment in favor of IBM but reduced the 
award of damages to $18.5 million. Further 
appeal by Telex to the United States 
Supreme Court is a possibility. 
Following the Telex judgment in the 
lower court, seven separate suits were insti-
tuted against IBM alleging federal anti-
trust law violations similar to those litigated 
in the Telex case. The plaintiffs, California 
Computer Products, lnc.,Transamerica Com-
puter Company, Hudson General Corpo-
ration, Memorex Corporation, Marshall

Industries, Forro Precision, Inc. and DPF 
Incorporated, seek damages, which after 
trebling aggregate $3,873 million (of which 
$3,150 million is sought by Memorex 
Corporation and its subsidiaries alone), and 
in some cases, injunctive relief. These 
cases were consolidated in the United States

Additional suits alleging Federal anti-

In July 1972, the trial of the Greyhound 
Computer Corporation lawsuit, filed in 
October 1969 against IBM, resulted in a 
directed verdict in favor of IBM. Greyhound 
had sought an unspecified amount of 
damages and injunctive relief for alleged 
violations of the federal antitrust laws and 
in connection with IBM's 1969 announce-

Greyhound has appealed the decision to the 
United States Circuit Court of Appeals for 
the Ninth Circuit. The appeal was argued on 
April 10,1974 and IBM is currently awaiting

In October 1974, Eaton Allen Corpora-

against IBM in the United States District 
Court for the Eastern District of New York.

cases and is engaged in preparing its

Stock Purchase Plan 
Under the IBM Employees 1971 Stock 
Purchase Plan, employees on the annual 
offering date, each July 1, who are not 
participants in a Stock Option Plan, may 
purchase IBM's capital stock one share 
at a time through payroll deductions not ex-
ceeding 10% of their compensation. The 
price an employee pays for a share of stock 
is equal to 85% of the market price on the 
annual offering date or on the date he has 
accumulated enough money to buy the 
share—whichever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 1,539,464 shares in 
1974, for which $266 million was paid to 
IBM and credited to the capital stock account. 
At December 31,1974,1,058,002 shares 
were reserved for sale under the 1971 Plan. 
Stock Option Plans 
In April 1971, stockholders approved the 
continuation for a five-year period of a Stock 
Option Plan for officers and other key em-
ployees. Under this Plan, as underthe 1966 
Plan, options may be granted for the pur-
chase of IBM's capital stock at not less than 
100% of the market price on the day the 
option is granted. 
Qualified options granted under both 
Plans have a maximum duration of five years 
and shares become purchasable in four 
annual installments commencing one year 
after the date of grant. 
The 1971 Plan also provides that non-
qualified options with a maximum duration 
of ten years may be granted alone or 
simultaneously with and for the same num-
ber of shares as qualified options. Non-
qualified options granted through December 
31,1972 were granted simultaneously 
with qualified options but purchases can not 
be made until five years and one day after 
the date of grant, and the number of shares 
purchasable is reduced on a one-for-one 
basis as shares are purchased under the re-
lated qualified option. Beginning in 1973, 
nonqualified options were granted alone and 
shares become purchasable in four annual 
installments commencing one year after the 
date of grant. 
In accordance with the Plans, installments 
are cumulative and the number of shares 
that may be purchased and the price per 
share are adjusted for stock dividends and 
splits effected after the option is granted.

Management's Discussion and Analysis 
of the Summary of Operations 
Total gross income reached record levels, 
increasing 15.3% in both 1973 and 1974, 
reflecting good growth in all major areas of 
the business. Gross income from sales 
increased 17.1% and 27.0% in 1973 and 
1974, respectively, and gross income 
from rentals and services increased 14.5% 
in 1973 and 10.1% in 1974. In each of 
these years, sales increased at a higher rate 
than rentals and services, due primarily to 
record levels of outright purchases of data 
processing equipment. 
Total cost of sales, rentals and services 
increased 11.6% in 1973 and 13.3% in 1974. 
Cost of sales increased 7.5% and 14.9% 
in 1973 and 1974 and cost of rentals and serv-
ices increased 13.4% in 1973 and 12.7% 
in 1974. Each of these increases was lower 
than the comparable increase in gross 
income except for the increase in cost of 
rentals and services in 1974. This increase 
was at a greater rate due to increased 
costs resulting from the pressures of infla-
tion as well as changes in the mix of 
products and services which have different 
levels of cost. 
Selling, development and engineering, 
and general and administrative expenses 
increased 16.3% in 1973 and 18.2% in 1974. 
These increases exceeded those in total 
gross income mainly as a result of the effect 
of worldwide inflation. 
Interest on debt increased 23.8% in 1973 
due to higher interest rates and decreased 
by 28.8% in 1974 as a result of a reduction in 
debt which was due primarily to the repay-
ment of over $300 million of debt previously 
incurred to comply with regulations of 
the Office of Foreign Direct Investments 
of the U. S. Department of Commerce. 
These regulations expired in early 1974. 
Other income, principally interest on 
marketable and other securities, increased 
41.2% in 1973 and 26.2% in 1974. The 
increases in both years resulted from higher 
interest rates and a larger average port-
folio during each of these years. 
U.S. Federal and foreign income taxes 
were 46.5% of earnings before such taxes in 
both 1973 and 1974. 
As a result of the factors noted above, 
net earnings increased 23.2% in 1973 
and 16.6% in 1974. 
As previously reported, outright 
purchases of data processing equipment 
for the full year 1974 were the highest 
in IBM's history, but were lower in the third 
and fourth quarters than in the earlier 
quarters of the year. Outright purchases, 
unlike rentals, increase income in the 
current period at the expense of future pe-
riods. Because of this variable effect of 
purchase activity on IBM's revenue flow, 
quarter-to-quarter comparisons can be-
come significantly distorted.

Organization 
To conduct its business tiiroughout the 
world, IBIVl is organized into the following 
divisions and wholly owned subsidiaries: 
Divisions 
Advanced Systems Development: identi-
fies and establishes worldwide market 
and service requirements for advanced 
systems; coordinates worldwide mar-
ketplace requirements for IBM's larger 
information-handling systems; and 
develops products to enhance user pro-
ductivity. 
Data Processing: markets within the United 
States IBM's information-handling 
systems, equipment, computer program-
ming, systems engineering, education, 
custom contracts and other related 
services to customers who require larger 
systems. 
Federal Systems: concentrates on advanced 
technology and systems for the ground-
based, seaborne, airborne and space-
borne information-handling and control 
needs of the U.S. Government. 
Field Engineering: provides maintenance 
and related services for the Company's 
information-handling systems and 
equipment, and support for program-
ming systems marketed in the United 
States by the Data Processing Division. 
General Products: worldwide development 
and United States manufacturing re-
sponsibility for computer tape units, disk 
files, mass storage systems, and printers. 
General Systems: worldwide development 
and United States manufacturing, market-
ing and service responsibility for smaller 
information-handling systems and as-
sociated peripheral equipment, including 
system-related programming support. 
Information Records: within the United 
States, markets disk packs and data 
modules, manufactures and markets 
supplies used in information-handling 
systems. 
Office Products: worldwide develop-
ment and United States manufacturing, 
marketing and service responsibility 
for electric typewriters, magnetic media 
typewriters, input processing equip-
ment, direct-impression composing 
products, copying equipment, and 
related supplies. 
Real Estate and Construction: manages the 
selection and acquisition of sites, the 
design and construction of buildings, 
and the purchase or lease of facilities. 
Research: brings scientific understanding 
to bear on areas of Company interest 
through basic research and the de-
velopment of technologies of potential 
long-range importance. 
System Development: worldwide respon-
sibility for systems definition, archi-
tecture and systems management of 
IBM's larger computer product lines and 
for associated systems programming; 
worldwide development and United 
States manufacturing responsibility for 
communications products including 
multiplexor systems, controllers, dis-
plays, terminal printers, and data entry 
and recognition devices. 
System Products: worldwide develop-
ment and United States manufacturing 
responsibility for IBM's larger central 
processor products, including main 
storage and logic technologies, packag-
ing technology, channels and cables; 
and standardized functional subassem-
blies used in the products of other 
IBM organizations; development and 
implementation of advanced design and 
manufacturing support systems. 
Subsidiaries 
IBM World Trade Corporation: provides 
support to its subsidiaries, IBM World 
Trade Americas/Far East Corporation 
and IBM World Trade Europe/Middle 
East/Africa Corporation, which con-
duct IBM's business outside the United 
States in their respective areas. 
Science Research Associates, Inc.: world-
wide development, production and 
marketing responsibility for textbooks, 
educational kits, learning systems, 
guidance products and a wide range of 
testing materials and services for 
elementary and secondary schools, 
colleges and industry.
