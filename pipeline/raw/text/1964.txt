To the Stockholders: 
During 1964, IBM made many significant ad-
vances in serving the rapidly growing information-
handling requirements of the worldwide market. 
The most far reaching of these advances was the 
introduction of the IBM System/360 which repre-
sented the most important new product announce-
ment in the Company's history. Since the introduc-
tion of this new computing system less than a year 
ago, customer reaction has been excellent, and a rec-
ord number of orders have been received from all 
over the world. 
The number of countries in which IBM does busi-
ness passed the hundred mark last year. This mile-
stone in our worldwide operations is highlighted in 
a special pictorial report beginning on page 18. The 
Company's operations outside the United States have 
become an increasingly significant part of our total 
business. Because of this, financial results have been 
reported on a worldwide consolidated basis since 
January i, 1964. Figures for prior periods have been 
restated accordingly and included in this report is a 
10-year comparative statement of operations on a 
worldwide basis. 
Consolidated gross income amounted to $3,239,-
359,581, an increase of $376,626,854,or 13.2% com-
pared with 1963. Gross income from domestic oper-
ations increased 11.1%, with regular products in-
creasing 13.9%, and space and defense products 
gross income showing a 25.6% decrease compared 
with 1963. Gross income from foreign operations 
showed an increase of 18.5%. 
Earnings were 1431,159,766, a $66,904,788 in-
crease over the prior year. 
Earnings per share were $12.30, based on the 
35,048,259 shares outstanding at the end of the 
year. This compares with earnings of $10.44 per 
share based on 34,902,278 shares outstanding on 
December 31,1963, adjusted for the 25% stock split 
effected May 5,1964. 
IBM's business comes primarily from the rental of 
its data processing machines. Outright sales of such 
equipment to our customers have the effect of realiz-
ing income currently, which, on a rental basis, would 
be realized in future periods. Stockholders were ad-
vised that IBM experienced an unusual concentration 
of outright sales of data processing equipment, pre-
viously under lease, in the first quarter of 1964. The 
proportion of outright sales for the last nine months 
of the year was substantially less than for the first 
three months, and was approximately the same as 
for the corresponding period in 1963.

During 1964, the Company continued its partici-
pation in the nation's space and defense programs. 
IBM is developing an advanced information-handling 
and control system which will be used to help the 
National Aeronautics and Space Administration con-
trol the two-man Gemini flights around the earth, 
and the three-man Apollo missions which are de-
signed to land a man on the moon. 
IBM delivered the first flight models of the on-
board computer system which will help guide the 
Gemini spacecraft into an orbital rendezvous with 
another space vehicle and then back to a predeter-
mined landing area on earth. IBM also is developing 
the guidance computer systems, as well as assem-
bling and testing the entire on-board instrumenta-
tion units, for the giant Saturn V launch vehicles 
which will be used in the Apollo program. 
Early in the year, IBM acquired Science Research 
Associates, Inc., a publisher of instructional materi-
als and achievement, aptitude, and intelligence tests. 
SRA was one of the first companies of its kind to pio-
neer the development of modern learning systems, 
and will help IBM explore solutions to educational 
problems in the years ahead. 
IBM's research and development laboratories re-
ported a number of advances during the year which 
could be important to the creation of future IBM 
products and services. IBM scientists, for example, 
recorded significant progress in the development of 
advanced integrated circuits which promise to be 
smaller and faster than the high-speed circuits now 
being used. Other advances involved the use of a 
laser beam for transmitting information, optical 
scanning devices for reading handwritten data, new 
methods for helping hospitals analyze patient infor-
mation, and experimental learning techniques in 
student instruction. 
The Company's greatest asset has always been its 
employees. The development of System/360, for ex-
ample, was the result of an outstanding creative ef-
fort by men and women working at IBM facilities here 
and abroad. Throughout the business, IBM people last 
year broadened their knowledge and took on demand-
ing new assignments so that the Company could con-
tinue to provide the marketplace with a wide range 
of information-handling products and services. 
Our industry is dynamic and highly competitive 
and the year ahead will make unprecedented de-
mands upon every part of IBM's worldwide organ-
ization. The introduction of System/360 cuts across 
every area of our business concerned with serving 
customers' data processing needs. As a result, heavy in-
troductory expenses will be involved i n providing cus-
tomers with the support they need in planning, pro-
gramming, and installing this new line of equipment. 
We have accelerated System/360 production to 
handle the growing number of customer orders, and 
installation of these new computers will begin with-
in the next few months. By the end of the year, we 
will be delivering more data processing equipment 
to our customers than ever before in IBM history.

IBM Serves Growing Customer Needs Around the World 
On April 7,1964, the Company introduced to cus-
tomers in this country and abroad a new generation 
of data processing equipment known as IBM Sys-
tem/ 360. This system is the first of its kind to utiHze 
new microelectronic circuits, enabling IBM to com-
plete the most significant design change it has ever 
made in the basic architecture of its computers. 
System/360 marks a new era in the development 
of computers. It makes available a single system 
whose computing capability ranges upward from 
IBM's smallest data processing system to an ultra-
high performance model which will be several times 
more powerful than any previous IBM computer. It 
offers more than two dozen different combinations 
of processing speed and memory capacity, and a 
wide range of peripheral units for entering, storing, 
and retrieving data. This permits customers to tailor 
their System/360 installations precisely to their data 
processing requirements, and, as their needs grow, 
expand to another point within the system's per-
formance range. 
System/360's single system concept means that, 
with minor exceptions, programs used to instruct 
one System/360 configuration also can be used by 
larger configurations with input-output devices of 
the same type and equivalent or larger memory ca-
pacity. Thus customers can expand their computing 
power without going to the expense of changing 
computer systems and rewriting their programs. The 
result is more computer productivity at lower cost. 
System/360 represents an extraordinary advance 
in memory capacity, which ranges from 4,000 to 
more than 16 million characters of information. The 
system also can use auxiliary storage devices capable 
of holding additional billions of characters of data. 
This increased capacity reduces the number of steps 
the system must take to solve a problem, enabling it 
to provide solutions at a greater speed. 
In designing System/360, IBM attempted to pro-
duce a line of computing equipment which would 
offer its customers maximum versatility in solving 
their data processing problems. The extent of this 
versatility can be seen in these examples involving 
some of System/360's most advanced features: 
System/360's multiple programming capability 
enables it to handle several jobs simultaneously. A 
bank could use the system to perform trust account-
ing at the main office while, at the same time, proc-
essing a stream of other transactions—such as sav-
ings deposits and loan payments—being fed in from 
branch office locations. 
System/360's powerful Tele-processing capabilities 
permit it to handle data from a great many remote 
points located nearby or thousands of miles away. 
A manufacturer could use the system to process data 
directly from many points on the factory floor so 
that management could have up-to-the-minute re-
ports on the status of production operations. 
System/360's immense memory significantly ex-
pands the amount of information a computer can 
store. An aerospace company, for instance, could 
store in the system's memory all the data needed to 
simulate the orbital flight of a satellite still under 
design. The memory could be enlarged still further 
to handle the billions of characters of data necessary 
to simulate a mission to the moon. 
System/360's large number of input-output de-
vices allow customers to select those units best suited 
to the needs of their individual businesses. The sys-
tem's audio response unit, for example, replies to 
telephone inquiries to the computer with prerecorded 
voice answers. A retailer could use this device to 
check a customer's credit rating while he's making 
his purchase. An insurance company could use an-
other advanced terminal to display in seconds any 
one of thousands of complete policy records stored 
in the system's large direct access files. 
A special information-handling system, based on 
System/360, was ordered by the Federal Aviation 
Agency for use in both an experimental model of a 
new air traffic control system and at the first opera-
tional control center in Jacksonville, Florida. The 
IBM equipment will calculate aircraft flight times 
over predetermined check points and provide flight 
control personnel with flight plan information on 
aircraft flying within the traffic control areas. 
Last year the Company also introduced the IBM 
1800 data acquisition and control system. This sys-
tem can record, measure, and analyze information as 
it is generated during a production operation, and 
then use the data to regulate the process automati-
cally. One of its uses is to help increase the efficiency 
of production operations in the steel, automobile, 
chemical, petroleum, vitility, and other industries. 
One of the most dramatic advances IBM has made 
in displaying information stored in a computer is 
embodied in the new IBM graphic data processing 
system. The system can electronically scan graphic 
information recorded on microfilm, store it inside a 
System/360 computer, and display it on a screen in 
the form of diagrams, drawings, graphs or manage-
ment reports. Graphic displays, such as engineering

New IBM Tele-processing Systems 
The use of IBM Tele-processing networks contin-
ued to grow last year as an increasing number of cus-
tomers were using these communications systems to 
transmit information between their computing cen-
ters and widely separated locations. 
Last year a large Tele-processing network used by 
American Airlines for handling passenger reserva-
tions became fully operational. The system, known 
as SABRE, was linked to New York City and other 
major cities during 1964, and now serves American 
Airlines' passengers from coast to coast. Progress also 
continued on the installation of two other airline 
reservation networks. Pan American World Airways 
began operating its PANAMAC system which eventu-
ally will connect 114 cities on six continents to a 
computing center in New York City. Delta Air Lines' 
computer-based reservations system also became op-
erational in several cities served by that company and

is expected to link Delta's complete reservation net-
work late in 1965. 
An advanced Tele-processing system also helped 
provide up-to-the-minute scoring coverage of the 
winter Olympic competition in Innsbruck, Austria, 
as well as the largest Olympic games in history held 
in Tokyo last fall. The system used in Tokyo covered 
nearly 6,000 athletes competing in 4,000 separate 
events in sites as far as 94 miles from the Japanese 
capital. Seconds after receiving the official results of 
an event, the system reported the winner at Tele-
processing stations throughout the Olympic com-
plex and determined whether an Olympic or world's 
record had been set. The system then followed with 
a complete report of the final scores and statistics for 
all the competitors in the event. 
During the year, a number of new Tele-processing 
devices were introduced, including the IBM 1092 
programmed keyboard which can transmit many dif-
ferent kinds of data into a computer for immediate 
processing. This flexibility is achieved by placing 
interchangeable plastic overlays on the unit's key-
board, with each overlay alerting the computer to a 
different set of facts. An automobile parts supplier 
using the unit, for example, could first report to his 
company's computer center his daily volume of tire 
sales by model, size, and type. He could then change 
overlays and subsequently record the sale of other 
items, such as batteries, oil, and accessory equipment. 
To help firms which supply a steady stream of 
items to branch locations from a central warehouse, 
the Company has introduced the IBM 1094 line en-
try terminal. Any of the firm's branch locations can 
use this device to transmit orders directly to the 
warehouse in seconds, reducing order-to-shipment 
time and providing faster service to their customers. 
Many firms today are using Tele-processing equip-
ment to transmit information between their com-
puting centers and terminals located a distance away. 
IBM last year introduced the IBM 1026 transmission 
control unit which permits smaller customers to ac-
cept data automatically from remote terminals, feed 
it into a computer, and then transmit the processed 
data back to the sender seconds later. Previously, 
these advanced Tele-processing capabilities were pos-
sible only with more elaborate systems. Another new 
device, the IBM 7711 data communications unit, en-
ables a computer at one location to "talk" to a com-
puter at another location. This unit can transmit 
data between computers at very high speeds, and is 
designed to help customers distribute the processing 
of large quantities of information among two or 
more systems. 
New Computer Programs Introduced 
Information-handling systems can help solve a 
broad range of highly complex problems with ex-
traordinary speed. Yet before they undertake even 
the simplest assignment they must be given precise, 
step-by-step programs of instruction telling them 
exactly what to do. In order to provide customers 
with the finest programming support available, the 
Company continued to develop advanced methods 
for applying IBM systems to new problem-solving 
assignments. 
Particularly significant was the development of 
very advanced programming techniques for use with 
System/360. Known as Operating System/360, these 
techniques include the supervisory programs which 
enable the computer to schedule its own operations 
automatically. This pennits the system to process a 
continuous flow of assignments without interrup-
tion and in the most efficient manner, as well as han-
dle many different types of jobs at the same time. 
The result is that the computer is able to handle 
complex problems with greatly increased speed. 
Operating System/360 also enables customers to 
select from many programming functions stored in-
side a computer those best suited to carrying out a 
particular assignment. This gives customers the same 
flexibility in selecting the proper machine language 
and other operating instructions that they have in 
choosing units of equipment needed to do the job. 
As part of the system, a new computer language 
is being developed which offers customers for the 
first time a single language for stating both business 
and scientific problems. 
The Company announced programming tech-
niques to help customers achieve an economical and 
orderly transition from their present computers to 
System/360. Virtually all of the instruction pro-
grams used with 15 previously announced IBM com-
puting systems can be utilized on appropriate mod-
els of System/360. This means that customers can 
continue working with most of their existing pro-
grams, while developing new programs specifically 
for use with System/360. 
IBM introduced several other programming sys-
tems of value to customers. One system, known as 
QUIKTRAN, enables an IBM system to handle up to 
40 different problem-solving tasks concurrently from

Typewriters and Dictation Equipment 
The Company sold more electric typewriters and 
IBM EXECUTARY dictation equipment during 1964 
than in any other year in its history. 
Last year, the Company introduced the new IBM 
Magnetic Tape SELECTRIC Typewriter which stores 
typing work and retypes the material automatically 
at speeds up to 180 words per minute. As a secretary 
types her material, the machine records it on a small 
reel of magnetic tape which can be used to operate 
the typewriter mechanism on subsequent retyping. 
Revisions can be made without manually retyping 
the unchanged text, since the machine automatically 
respaces, and repositions new words and sentences. 
The Company also recorded a breakthrough in the 
development of a new keyboard and typing element 
for the SELECTRIC which can print simplified Japa-
nese letters in the lower case and English letters in 
the upper case. This is the first time a single type-
writer has been able to print both Japanese and Eng-
lish letters. 
Specialized Industrial Products 
During 1964, IBM entered a completely new mar-
ket when it began introducing a number of advanced 
industrial products which other manufacturers can 
utilize in their operations. Several new devices an-
nounced last year are of particular value in the pro-
duction of electronic equipment. These devices can 
automatically insert tiny resistors on printed circuit 
cards, and test miniature components such as diodes, 
resistors, and capacitors for quality and reliability. 
Another new product was an industrial camera with 
a special lens that can record up to 1,200 highly 
miniaturized circuit elements on a single film ex-
posure. This enables engineers to study the perform-
ance of a great many different circuit patterns and 
designs which would be important to the develop-
ment of advanced electronic circuitry. 
Space and Defense 
Throughout the year, IBM information systems 
made a number of contributions to our country's 
space and defense programs. 
IBM systems specialists used the experience gained 
in tracking the orbital flights of Mercury astronauts 
to continue the development of a still more advanced 
information-handling and control system. This 
ground-based system will be located at the National 
Aeronautics and Space Administration's Manned 
Spacecraft Center in Houston, Texas, and will help 
NASA control the upcoming two-man Gemini flights 
around the earth and, later, the three-man Apollo 
flights which are planned ultimately to land a man 
on the moon. The system will process a continuous 
flow of data from other tracking stations around the 
world and from the spacecraft itself — supplying 
NASA flight controllers at the center with the vital 


===== PAGE 13 =====
up-to-the-second information they need to direct the 
mission. 
IBM specialists are working with NASA to find 
new ways to use this system in training astronauts 
and the hundreds of technical personnel who will 
support future manned space missions. By simulat-
ing space flights, scientists can test both the system 
and the people against thousands of possible situa-
tions that could occur during actual missions in space. 
Last year, IBM delivered the first flight models of 
the on-board computer system which Gemini astro-
nauts will use during their space missions. This com-
pact computer will help guide the spacecraft from 
its point of lift-off into an orbital rendezvous with 
another space vehicle and then back to a predeter-
mined landing area. 
The Company is developing the on-board guid-
ance computer systems for the giant Saturn V launch 
vehicles, one of which will be used to send an Apollo 
spacecraft to the moon. NASA also selected IBM to 
assemble and test the Saturn V's entire on-board in-
strumentation unit—a 2 2-foot ring located at the 
top of the vehicle's third stage. It will contain some 
6o different operating systems, including the IBM 
guidance computer. The work will be done at a new 
IBM facility located near NASA's Marshall Space 
Flight Center in Huntsville, Alabama. 
During 1964, an IBM system went into orbit for 
the first time guiding a Saturn I rocket—an early ver-
sion of the Saturn V moon vehicle—in a successful 
test flight. 
In the area of defense, IBM maintained its delivery 
of computers for the all-inertial Achiever guidance 
system which directs the TITAN II intercontinental 
ballistic missile to its target. The Company is now 
modifying this computer to handle still more ad-
vanced missions. IBM computers also guided the 
more powerful TITAN III launch vehicle in its suc-
cessful first test. 
An experimental IBM computer, made with thin 
metallic films instead of conventional electronic 
components, was turned over to the Navy during the 
year for tests aboard combat aircraft. The computer 
is smaller than a brief case, weighs only 26 pounds, 
and is designed to solve complex weapons firing 
problems during a mission. 
The Company continued to develop special sys-
tems to help military commands collect and analyze 
intelligence information from all over the world, 
and to process the data needed to plan and control 
military operations. The Defense Communications 
Agency has installed a special computer-operated in-
formation system at its centers in Virginia, Colorado, 
Hawaii, and France to warn military commanders of 
trouble anywhere along the 19-million-mile defense 
communications network, so that vital military mes-
sages can be kept moving. 
SBC's Expanding Customer Services 
A growing number of small and medium-size busi-
nesses whose information-handling requirements 
can best be served on a regular weekly, monthly or 
job contract basis are turning to The Service Bureau 
Corporation, a wholly owned, independently oper-
ated subsidiary of IBM. 
SBC offices in 71 cities are meeting this demand 
through the use of the most advanced IBM data proc-
essing equipment available and an increasing array 
of problem-solving services. 
SBC introduced several important new computer 
programs last year. One program helps company 
management select the most effective of several pos-
sible courses of action, such as in the scheduling of 
complex production operations. A second program 
permits civil engineers to determine automatically 
the stress requirements for a building under design 
—eliminating the time-consuming manual calcula-
tions that previously were needed to do the job. 
SBC announced still another program which per-
mits automobile dealers to apply advanced data 
processing methods to their accounting operations, 
and enables them to produce up-to-date financial re-
ports on such things as new car purchases, parts and 
accessory sales, and automobile repairs.

Since 1958, when stockholders first approved an

Under the IBM Employees 1961 Stock Purchase

is equal to 85% of the market price on the day he 
begins saving toward a share, or on the day he has 
accumulated enough money to buy the share — 
whichever price is lower.

Plan, including the 125,000 shares authorized by 
stockholders in April. Numbers of shares reflect the
