The requirements of our customers for 
greater productivity to counteract continuing 
inflationary pressures resulted in anotfier 
strong year for IBM, witfi record levels in gross 
income and net earnings. 
Worldwide net earnings for the year ended 
December 31, 1977, amounted to $2,719 mil-
lion, compared with $2,398 million forthesame 
period last year. The net earnings equaled 
$18.30 per share on 148.6 million shares, the 
average number of shares outstanding dur-
ing the period. This compared with $15.94 per 
share on 150.4 million shares, the average 
number outstanding in 1976. 
Consolidated gross income amounted to 
$18,133 million, compared with $16,304 
million last year. 
Net earnings for the quarter ended De-
cember 31 , 1977, amounted to $797 million, or 
$5.38 per share. This compared with $674 
million, or $4.47 per share, for the same pe-
riod last year. Gross income amounted to 
$5,038 million, compared with $4,519 million 
forthe same 1976 period. 
During the latter part of 1977, the value of 
the U.S. dollar weakened in relation to many 
of the major currencies of the world, resulting 
in a foreign currency exchange gain of $64 
million in the fourth quarter. This more than 
offset earlier exchange losses, thereby re-
flecting a net gain for the year of $28 million, 
which consists principally of unrealized 
gains from the translation of foreign currency 
assets and liabilities. 
Operations outside the United States, in-
cluded in consolidated results, are being 
reported in accordance with the Financial Ac-
counting Standards Board's Statement of 
Financial Accounting Standards No. 14, which 
required a change in reporting the net earn-
ings of these operations. Gross income for 
1977 was $9,125 million, compared with $8,154 
million in 1976. Net earnings were $1,228 
million, compared with $1,057 million in 1976, 
which has been restated on a comparable 
basis. The change does not affect consoli-
dated worldwide net earnings. 
High worldwide levels of outright pur-
chases of data processing equipment contin-
ued throughout 1977, resulting in a record 
level fortheyear, withthefourth quarter higher 
than any previous quarter. This activity was 
the major factor in both the year's and the 
fourth quarter's record gross income and 
net earnings. Outright purchases of data 
processing equipment are expected to con-
tinue at high levels. However, it is unlikely 
that the strong rate of increase experienced in 
1977 will continue in 1978. If it does not, 
period-to-period earnings comparisons will 
not be as favorable as they were in 1977. 
Consolidated gross income from rentals 
and services increased 6.7% over 1976. The 
relatively low increase in 1977 continues to 
reflect the heavy purchase activity which took 
place during the past two years. 
Incoming orders were substantially 
ahead of 1976. This contributed to a year-end 
backlog well in excess of last year's, even 
though 1977 shipments exceeded the high 
levels achieved during the previous year. 
World Information Demand 
The growth in the volume of our business 
reflects the fact that information is increas-
ingly being put to work for people. Information 
technology is spreading from offices to 
factories, from the research laboratory to the 
local store. All of IBM's products in some 
way are involved in the handling of informa-
tion—gathering, storing, processing and 
distributing it. We plan to continue to expand 
the capabilities of our products so that 
customers can use them to do more things 
for more people around the world. 
For example, we introduced new products 
in all areas of our business in 1977. 
IBM System/34 is a low-cost computer 
with multiple workstations that both small and 
large businesses can use to distribute their 
data processing throughout the organization. 
The new IBM Office System 6 provides 
high-level capabilities for text editing and for 
processing administrative records. A new 
magnetic card typewriter prints more than 
three times faster than the widely used IBM 
magnetic card "Selectric" typewriter. 
We announced three powerful new central 
processors in 1977-the IBM 3031, IBM 3032 
and IBM 3033—for intermediate and large-
system users having sizable data base and 
data communications requirements. We im-
proved the information displays and the 
data communications products used with 
many of our computers. 
To safeguard the privacy of information 
stored and transmitted by our computer cus-
tomers, we introduced new products to 
encrypt the information so as to make it 
meaningless to unauthorized persons. 
Additional products for future growth are 
being developed in our laboratories. We 
continue to decrease the size as well as the 
cost of product components. That provides 
better performance at lower prices and, in turn, 
makes possible many new applications that 
enable us to expand the market for IBM 
products. 
Growth in Operations 
We are also taking other steps to keep 
pace with the expanding opportunities before 
us. In 1977, we established a r>ew General

requirements of both our office products and 
smaller computers. This will help us stay 
competitive and be more responsive to cus-

Tax and Trade Issues 
The current proposal to repeal the present 
system of U. S. taxation of foreign earnings 
would put U.S. corporations operating over-
seas at a serious disadvantage by raising 
their tax costs significantly relative to foreign 
competitors. Today, no foreign government 
imposes such tax handicaps on its own com-
panies competing abroad. In fact, most for-
eign governments help those companies in a 
variety of ways, even including direct sub-
sidies. Restrictive U.S. tax measures not only 
make American companies less competi-
tive, they also reduce U.S. jobs that depend 
on exports. 
IBM and many other businesses have a 
great deal riding on the success of the current 
negotiations under the auspices of the Gen-
eral Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) 
that would reduce both tariff and non-tariff 
trade barriers. Some 40 countries have duty 
rates on data processing equipment at least 
twice as high as those of the U.S.—a disparity 
that we hope the GATT negotiations will 
reduce. 
Changes in Operations Overseas 
In November, 1977, after repeated efforts 
to reach an understanding with the Indian 
government, which had required us to give up 
60% ownership of our business there, we 
felt we had no choice but to change our mode 
of operations. 
We believe that in a high-technology, 
rapidly developing industry such as ours, the 
necessary worldwide coordination of re-
search, development, manufacturing, sales 
and service can best be carried out with full 
ownership of subsidiaries. In discussions 
with the Indian government over a long period, 
we believed that a mutually acceptable 
resolution would be found. Unfortunately, this 
did not come to pass, and we have accord-
ingly phased out our Bombay plant and will 
phase out our marketing and maintenance 
operations in India by mid-1978. 
Subject to the approval of the Indian gov-
ernment, we will maintain a limited presence 
in India by establishing a liaison office where 
we can receive authorized requests for our 
products and services. 
We were able to reach an agreement in 
principle with Indonesia in December, 1977, 
that will enable us to continue offering our 
products and services. An Indonesian law 
specified that foreign companies must turn 
their trading activities over to Indonesians 
by the end of 1977. Our agreement included 
the appointment of an agent to trade on 
our behalf, as well as reorganization of our 
operation so as to maintain a presence there. 
We look forward to continued business ac-
tivity in Indonesia. 
The Lawsuits 
The U.S. Justice Department's antitrust 
suit against IBM went to trial in May, 1975, and 
so far only the government has presented 
its case. We expect to start our defense by 
mid-1978 and will need considerable time to 
present our side. 
In February, 1977, the Federal District 
Court hearing antitrust charges brought 
against IBM by California Computer Products, 
Inc., dismissed the action at the close of 
Calcomp's presentation of its case. The court 
ordered a directed verdict in favor of IBM. 
Calcomp is appealing that decision. 
The U.S. Court of Appeals has granted 
Greyhound Computer Corporation's petition 
fora new trial. In July, 1972, Greyhound's 
antitrust charges against IBM were dismissed 
before IBM had presented its defense. 
Trial of the antitrust suit by Memorex Cor-
poration commenced in January, 1978. 
We are convinced that we have achieved 
our business position through fair and ethical 
competition and that our legal position is 
sound in each case. Further details on these 
and other lawsuits are contained in the finan-
cial section of this rSport. 
Repurchase of IBM Stock

All of the shares purchased in this way have

environment over the long term. We are

At one time, customers used IBM products for only a few information-handling tasks—such as inventory and 
payroll or scientific study—out of the public sight. 
Today, information technology in some form is visible almost everywhere. It is touching people's lives 
directly. It provides ease of buying through terminals in department stores, faster checkout at supermarket 
counters, or 24-hour banking atsidewalkteller machines. 
Moreover, the information being handled is taking many forms besides the letters and numbers still 
processed today in most applications. More and more, the information is encompassing a broader spectrum of 
timely data. The strength of a human pulse monitored by computer may be critically important information 
forthe doctor working againsttime. Pressure fluctuations in a natural gas line can be vital information in assuring 
heat and power for homes and industry. Amid the growing complexities of modern life, information in its 
multiple forms is becoming a fundamental resource for societies around the world. 
Whatever those forms, machine systems are needed to collect the information and to store, process, 
retrieve and communicate it quickly and accurately to the end user. Providing such systems is IBM's business. 
IBM's many products, from computers to copiers, are assisting customers in all parts of the world to process 
information in manufacturing, banking, distribution, health care, science and engineering, government, enter-
tainment and many other aspects of daily living. 
Shown on the following pages are some of the ways IBM customers are putting information to work, as 
well as ways IBM people and new products are helping to make that work more efficient and productive.

New products to widen the uses of informa-
tion technology were announced in 1977 in all 
principal areas of the business. Among 
the many new products or enhancements of 
existing products were the following: 
More Powerful Processors 
The new IBM 3033 processor, IBM's most 
powerful, offers major performance improve-
ments for users of large computers. Internal 
performance is generally 1.6 to 1.8 times that 
of the fastest comparable System/370— 
the Model 168-3. Logic circuits have twice the 
density of those in the Model 168-3. That 
compactness helps reduce the internal cycle 
time to only 58 billionths of a second, com-
pared to 80 billionths in the Model 168-3. With 
up to 8 million characters of high-speed 
main storage, the IBM 3033 processor is de-
signed for customers with sizable data base 
and data communications requirements. 
Two additional high-performance proc-
essors-the IBM 3031 and IBM 3032-are for 
those users of intermediate and large sys-
tems who need greater speed and capacity, 
but not the power of the IBM 3033. 
All three new processors are compatible 
members of the System/370 family of 
computers. 
Small Systems for Business 
A new low-cost computer with multiple 
workstations provides business information 
when and where needed. The IBM System/34 
brings daily operating information and com-
puter processing to many locations in a busi-
ness. Cost to the user is well below that of 
other kinds of IBM systems that offer inter-
action with the computer from multiple 
workstations. 
System/34 permits linking the central 
computer to as many as 64 workstations at 
distant sites. 
Introduced in January, 1978, was an 
economical computer, the IBM 5110, designed 
to reduce paperwork and improve produc-
tivity in business operations. The IBM 5110 
can store as many as 4.8 million charac-
ters of information on diskettes and is capable 
of performing a wide variety of applications 
in both large and small businesses.

Faster Mass Transactions 
The processing of bill payments received 
by utilities, banks, insurance companies 
and other businesses is reduced to a single, 
faster operation with the new IBM 3762/3790 
terminal system. 
When a bill stub and payment check are 
inserted into the operator station, the system 
reads the bill stub optically and compares 
the amount with the value of the check keyed 
in by the operator. If the two match, the 
system codes, endorses and imprints the 
check for further processing. If the bill and 
check amount do not match, the terminal 
locks, and a light signals the operator to take 
appropriate action. 
The IBM 3895 deposit processing system, 
a new machine for use by commercial banks, 
optically reads, then inscribes and bal-
ances large volumes of checks and their ac-
companying deposit slips. Each new IBM 3895 
system can proof more than 100,000 deposit 
documents daily. 
Protection of Information 
New IBM data security products help safe-
guard private information stored and trans-
mitted by computer systems. Two new devices 
- t h e IBM 3845 and 3846 data encryption 
units—can operate with a variety of computers 
and terminals. Also introduced was the IBM 
cryptographic subsystem, a combination 
of programs and equipment for use with most 
System/370 computers and data process-
ing networks. 
The new products are based on an ad-
vanced cryptographic technique. They make 
information appear meaningless by en-
ciphering it. The user enciphers and deciphers 
information by using variable, secret num-
bers called "keys." 
Improved Displays 
For general use in many industries are 
new displays, printers and control devices for 
the IBM 3270 information display system. 
Compatible with current 3270 units, the new 
products can be used with IBM System/370, 
System/360, and System/3 computers. 
One new terminal, for example, can show up 
to 3,440 characters on its screen—79% 
more characters than the previous model. 
In addition, an improved graphics display 
system, the IBM 3250, enables designers, 
engineers and draftsmen to use a light pen 
to modify drawings on a screen. When a de-
sign is completed, it can be stored in a 
System/370 computer for further use, for ex-
ample, by manufacturing personnel in de-
veloping tooling for the production process. 
Versatile Blood Separator 
An improved version of the IBM blood cell 
separator was announced in 1977. Capable 
of receiving blood directly from donors or pa-
tients, the new machine separates blood 
into such major components as red cells, white 
cells, plasma and platelets. Concentrates 
of those components are used in transfusions 
to control acute infections, plasma abnor-
malities, and other disorders. The separator 
has many applications in hospitals, blood 
banks and laboratories.

Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles of Consolidation: The con-
solidated financial statements include the ac-
counts of International Business Machines 
Corporation'and its U.S. and non-U.S. sub-
sidiary companies. 
Translation of Non-U.S. Currency Amounts: 
Non-U.S. assets and liabilities are translated 
to U.S. dollars at year-end exchange rates, 
except that inventories and plant, rental ma-
chines and other property are translated at 
approximate rates prevailing when acquired. 
Income and expense items are translated 
at average rates of exchange prevailing dur-
ing the year, except that inventories charged 
to cost of sales and depreciation are trans-
lated at historical rates. Exchange gains and 
losses are included in earnings currently. 
Gross Income: Gross income is recognized 
Non-U.S. Operations 
from sales when the product is shipped or 
in certain cases upon customer acceptance, 
from rentals in the month in which they 
accrue, and from services over the contrac-
tual period or as the services are per-
formed. Rental plans include maintenance 
service and contain discontinuance and 
purchase option provisions. Rental terms are 
predominantly monthly or for a two-year 
period, with some covering periods up to five 
years. 
Depreciation: With minor exceptions, 
depreciation of U.S. properties is computed 
using the sum of the years-digits method. 
Depreciation of non-U.S. properties is com-
puted using either accelerated methods 
or the straight-line method. 
Retirement Plans: Current service costs are 
accrued currently. Prior service costs re-
sulting from improvements in the plans are 
amortized generally over 10 years. How-
ever, certain non-U.S. subsidiaries use longer 
periods not exceeding 20 years. 
Expenses: Marketing expenses and 
development and engineering expenses are 
charged against income as they are incurred. 
Income Taxes: Income tax expense is 
based on reported earnings before income 
taxes. It thus includes the effects of timing 
differences between reported and taxable 
earnings that arise because certain trans-
actions are included in taxable earnings in 
other years. Investment tax credits are de-
ferred and amortized as a reduction of income 
tax expense over the average useful life of 
the applicable classes of property.

Litigation 
In January, 1969, the Department of Jus-
tice filed a civil antitrust complaint against 
IBM under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust 
Act, charging the company with monop-
olizing commerce in general purpose digital 
computers in the United States. Trial of 
the case began in May, 1975, and is currently 
in progress. 
The government continues to seek 
divestiture relief, requesting that IBM be re-
organized into several independent and 
competing organizations and that IBM be en-
joined from continuing its alleged monop-
olistic practices. 
In August, 1977, the United States Court 
of Appeals for the Ninth Circuit granted 
Greyhound Computer Corporation's petition 
for a new trial on all but one of its antitrust 
claims against IBM. The 1972 trial had resulted 
in a directed verdict in favor of IBM. Grey-
hound's suit, filed in 1969, had sought an un-
specified amount of damages and injunc-
tive relief. 
Six private lawsuits instituted against IBM 
in 1973 and 1974, alleging Federal anti-
trust law violations, remain in progress. The 
plaintiffs, California Computer Products, 
Inc., Transamerica Computer Company, 
Hudson General Corporation, Memorex 
Corporation, Forro Precision, Inc. and DPP 
Incorporated, seek damages which, after 
trebling, aggregate $4,203 million (of which 
$3,150 million is sought in the complaints 
filed by Memorex Corporation and its subsid-
iaries alone) and, in some cases, injunctive 
relief. Three of these lawsuits are in the pre-
trial discovery stage. Trial of the Memorex 
Corporation case is currently in progress. 
The two cases which have been tried 
thus far have resulted in the dismissal of the 
antitrust claims against IBM. In the Cali-
fornia Computer Products case, in February, 
1977, at the close of the plaintiff's case, the 
District Court entered an order directing a ver-
dict in favor of IBM and dismissing the 
action. California Computer Products, which 
is appealing the decision, claims damages 
which, after trebling, aggregate $306 million. 
In the Forro Precision case, $36 million in 
damages, after trebling, were sought. After 
trial, the antitrust charges against IBM were 
dismissed, and non-antitrust damage verdicts 
of $2.74 million for Forro and $.26 million for 
IBM were entered. Both parties are appealing. 
In 1970, 1973 and 1977, Xerox Corpora-
tion filed lawsuits against IBM charging 
patent infringement and misuse of confiden-
tial information in connection with IBM copiers. 
In 1975, IBM filed an action against Xerox 
for infringement of an IBM patent relating to 
office copiers, and Xerox counterclaimed 
for breach of contract relating to a printing 
subsystem and further copier patent in-
fringement. These lawsuits are in the pre-
trial stage. Xerox seeks injunctive relief 
and damages in an unspecified amount. 
Patent infringement actions between Xerox 
and IBM are also pending in Canada. The 
first of these to reach trial resulted in a finding 
that IBM had infringed five of Xerox's Cana-
dian patents, of which three had expired. IBM 
Canada, which continues normal marketing 
activities under a license received from Xerox 
after the trial, has appealed the trial court 
judgment. 
IBM has denied the charges in all of these 
cases and is vigorously defending each action.

to $9,125 million, an 11.9% increase. 
Gross income from sales increased 19.0% 
in 1977, principally from record worldwide 
outright purchases of data processing equip-
ment. Such purchase activity in the U.S. con-
tinued to be higher than that of non-U.S. 
operations, however, the latter has experi-

in other product areas increased at more 
moderate rates. 
Gross income from rentals and services 
increased 6.7% in 1977. The relatively low 
1977 increase continues to reflect the heavy 
worldwide purchase activity of data proc-
essing equipment in the past two years. While 
data processing rentals and services com-

products rentals and services reflected a 
higher rate of increase.

of products sold, as well as the costs of sup-
porting new installations. Office products 
equipment cost to gross income relationship 
improved in 1977.

worldwide by 4.6%, and overall, compared 
favorably with the increase of 6.7% in re-
lated gross income. Cost of rentals and serv-
ices for data processing equipment in-
creased at a lesser rate than related gross

Greater Energy Conservation 
New energy conservation gains in 1977 
brought IBM's total U.S. reduction from the 
1973 pre-conservation level to 39%. The com-
pany realized a 6.3% reduction in energy 
per sq. ft. at major U.S. locations, compared 
with 1976, exceeding the target 5% reduc-
tion for 1977. 
In slightly more than four years, IBM 
has saved 2.2 billion kilowatt-hours of elec-
tricity in the U.S.—enough to supply the 
needs of 255,000 homes for a year. The fuel 
oil saving has been 117 million gallons, 
enough to serve 97,000 homes in northern 
states for a year. The combined value of elec-
tricity and oil saved has reached $90 million. 
In World Trade countries, traditionally 
energy-conscious, the value of energy saved 
over the pre-conservation level at major 
IBM locations from 1973 through 1977 totaled 
some 30%, or more than $30 million. 
Conservation in the company initially 
involved better energy management through 
lower building temperatures in winter and 
higher temperatures in summer, lighting cut-
backs, and shutting down unneeded equip-
ment. Further savings were achieved later 
through technical means such as improve-
ments in heating and air conditioning systems, 
improved furnace combustion, waste heat 
recovery, computer controls, and greater en-
ergy efficiency in the design of new buildings. 
By year-end 1977, 42 IBM locations were 
using IBM computers for energy management, 
and 20 more systems were scheduled. 
Savings for Customers 
More than 600 IBM computers are being 
used by customers for energy management in 
offices, factories, schools, stores and other 
locations. The total saving is estimated at 1.8 
billion kilowatt-hours a year. 
Added conservation comes from the 
energy efficiency of IBM products. Per calcula-
tion, IBM System/370 uses far less electri-
cal power than was required by IBM computers 
of the early 1960s. The new IBM 3033 
processor has twice the energy efficiency of 
other large IBM System/370 computers. 
Heat pump in IBM's Santa Teresa, 
Calif., 
progrannming 
laboratory 
uses heat from com-
puters as the main heating source for the 
entire eight-building 
complex. 
Converting Solar Energy 
Among IBM's 1977 programs to make use 
of the sun's energy were the following: 
1. Under contract from the National Aero-
nautics and Space Administration, IBM is 
designing and installing seven experimental 
solar energy systems that feature different 
combinations of solar heat and hot water and 
cooling subsystems. The program is testing 
the application of solar energy to both home 
and commercial use. 
2. IBM research scientists have produced 
experimental solar cells of gallium arsenide 
that convert 22% of the sunlight falling on 
them into electricity. This compares with the 
18% efficiency of silicon cells, widely used 
to provide electricity aboard space vehicles. 
The gallium arsenide cells can operate 
under a high concentration of the sun's rays. 
3. IBM scientists are also using computers 
to simulate radiation in the atmosphere in 
studies of the most promising types of solar 
energy plants and the best locations for 
them.

water-
heating system being installed at IBM's

and laboratory 
in Austin, Tex., is 
expected 
to lower the use of natural gas for boiler 
fuel.

underwater objects. In this way, they per-
mit detection and tracking of submarines and 
surface ships. 
IBM will deliver more than 100 of these 
systems. Each weighs 15 tons and has the 
processing power of approximately 10 
large-scale IBM System/370 Model 168

Greater Resource Conservation 
In 1977, IBM accelerated its program for 
reclamation and conservation of resources-
paper, water, chemicals, plastics and metals 
—thus reducing both costs and the energy 
needed to produce such resources. Recy-
cling also helps protect the environment. 
Companywide, the value of such recovery 
from 1973 through 1977 exceeded $80 mil-
lion. In the U.S. during 1977, for example, IBM: 
• Recycled more than 450 million gallons 
of water. 
• Reclaimed more than $4 million in precious 
metals, such as gold and platinum used to 
produce computer circuits. 
• Saved 23 million pounds of materials used 
to package products for shipment. 
Among many resource conservation pro-
grams overseas are the following: 
• The IBM plant in Boigny, France, uses 
waste solvents as fuel to provide 17% of the 
facility's heat requirements. 
• IBM's plant at Bromont, Canada, each 
year reclaims and reuses more than 11/2 
million gallons of degreasing solvent, for an 
annual saving of some $3 million.
