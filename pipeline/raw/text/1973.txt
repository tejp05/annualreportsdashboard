On January 31,1974, Thomas J. Watson, Jr., 
former IBM Board Chairman will retire 
after one of the most distinguished careers in 
the annals of American industry. 
His remarkable leadership has been a key 
element in the success of IBM. He started 
with IBM in 1937, and as chief executive officer 
from 1956 to 1971 he guided the Company 
through its most challenging years. 
This was a period of growth and change in 
the data processing and office products in-
dustry as dramatic as that experienced by any 
other industry. It was a time that called for 
leadership with the vision and courage to make 
and implement many innovative decisions. 
As the achievements of IBM during this impor-
tant period demonstrate, Tom Watson was 
the right man at the right time. Above all, his 
unwavering commitment to excellence 
has touched everyone around him and helped 
make IBM a very special company. All of us 
at IBM will be enduringly grateful to him and 
will hold his example as a legacy for the future. 
Fortunately, Tom Watson will remain as 
a member of the Board and as Chairman of the 
Board's Executive Committee. In these posi-
tions, he will continue to give us the benefit of 
his wisdom and experience. 
The year 1973 was one of growth for IBM. 
Gross income and earnings for the Company's 
worldwide operations reached record levels 
with consolidated gross income exceeding 
$10 
billion and gross income from operations out-
side the United States exceeding $5 billion, each 
for the first time. Consolidated gross income 
amounted to $10,993,241,953, an increase of 
$1,460,649,311 over 1972. Net earnings were 
$1,575,467,055, an increase of $296,199,500 
over the previous year. 
Earnings amounted to $10.79 P^i^ share on 
146,061,750 shares, the average number of 
shares outstanding during the year after adjust-
ment for the 2 5 % stock split effected May 10, 
1973. This compares with earnings of $8.83 per 
share on 144,953,058 shares, the average num-
ber outstanding in 1972 adjusted for the 
2 5 % split. 
Gross income for the quarter ended Decem-
ber 31,1973, amounted to $3,240,216,180, 
an increase of $718,487,616 compared with the 
same period of 1972. Net earnings amounted 
to $468,721,918, or $128,294,875 more than the 
fourth quarter of 1972. 
Operations outside the United States for 
the year 1973, included in the consolidated re-
sults, showed a higher rate of increase over 
1972 than domestic operations, due in part to 
numerous currency fluctuations and realign-
ments during the past year. Overseas gross 
income amounted to $5,142,536,294, an

Net earnings from these operations were 
$852,536,642, an increase of $165,905,202 
over 1972. 
Accompanying a record rate of worldwide 
installations in the final quarter of 1973, 
outright purchases of data processing equip-
ment were also at an all-time high for any 
quarter, and were higher for the full year 1973 
than for 1972. Consolidated gross income 
from rentals and services in 1973 increased 
14.5 % over the previous year. 
Orders for IBM equipment and services 
during 1973 continued strong, and at year end 
the backlog of orders was higher than at year-
end 1972 despite a record level of shipments. 
In 1973, IBM announced a number of 
important new products that expand the appli-
cation of data and word processing. Our Data 
Processing Division introduced new systems for 
supermarkets, retail stores and financial insti-
tutions, and our Office Products Division added 
an innovative Correcting "Selectric" Type-
writer to its line of word processing equipment. 
These and other new products are discussed

Litigation developments last year included 
a trial date set for October, 1974, for the 
Department of Justice civil antitrust suit, and 
a decision in the Telex antitrust suit. Judge A. 
Sherman Christensen's original ruling in the 
Telex suit against IBM was amended by him 
to reduce the damages and significantly modify 
the injunctive relief granted Telex. The 
ruling that found Telex guilty of misappropri-
ating IBM trade secrets was left unchanged. 
We believe the antitrust ruling against IBM is 
erroneous both in its theory of antitrust 
law and its interpretation of IBM business 
practices, and on November 30,1973, our re-
quest for an expedited appeal was granted 
by the Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit. 
Additional details on litigation are on page 26. 
Many of you may be wondering why an 
increasing number of competitors are filing anti-
trust suits against the Company. The most 
obvious reason is that the adverse Telex antitrust 
decision has prompted other companies that 
sell IBM-compatible equipment to take advan-
tage of the decision and seek to use it as a 
precedent. 
There are also, I think, some less obvious 
factors behind the legal actions against IBM. We 
have seen in recent years in our society an in-
creasing distrust of large institutions, a feeling 
that "bigness is badness" no matter how that 
bigness was achieved. This social and political 
atmosphere, I believe, has encouraged smaller 
companies in a number of other industries—not 
just ours—to initiate private antitrust action 
against large, successful competitors. 
In addition, our industry includes a number 
of companies, like Telex, that have chosen to 
rely on IBM-developed technology for their 
products. Some of these companies claim that 
IBM's efforts to introduce new technology 
and to stay competitive in price—the kind of 
efforts that have been considered normal by 
most companies and industries—are now illegal 
acts against them. This doesn't make any sense 
to us. We continue to believe we have com-
peted fairly. 
When a suit filed against IBM by Greyhound 
Computer Corporation was dismissed by a 
Federal court in 1972, the judge stated; 
.. it 
is the opinion of this court from the evidence 
adduced thus far on the record that the defend-
ant's place in the industry has been achieved 
as a result of superior skill, foresight, and indus-
try . . . There is no evidence of an attempt to 
monopolize on the record." He further stated: 
"Apparently it is still the law that size alone 
does not constitute an offense under the Sherman 
Act." We believe that is a sound judgment on 
our legal situation and we hope it prevails. But 
whatever the outcome of litigation against IBM, 
I am confident that the Company's future re-
mains bright for our stockholders, our employ-
ees, and our customers. 
Several external factors cloud the outlook 
for 1974, including the uncertain impact 
here and abroad of a pattern of inflation that has 
yet to be curbed, and the energy shortage. We 
share the public concern about worldwide en-
ergy problems and have taken actions to re-
duce our energy requirements and to conserve 
and recycle critical production materials. In 
addition, we are continuing to develop ways for 
our products to be used to conserve energy. 
In recent years, we have seen a continuing 
public debate between those who regard com-
panies like IBM that operate on an international 
scale as a source of strength to the American 
economy, and those who think otherwise. Some 
critics contend that U.S. foreign investment 
results in American jobs and capital being "ex-
ported" abroad. The IBM experience has been

Products 
at Work: 
Just twenty years ago, IBM announced the 
701 computer—the company's first large-scale, 
stored program electronic computer to be 
produced in volume. Business experts estimated 
that eventually as many as 50 companies in 
the country might use this new labor-saving 
tool. Now it is virtually impossible to count 
the numbers of ways in which computers are 
being used. New markets reach out continu-
ally for computing equipment to support man's 
efforts not only to keep business records, but 
to supply food, health care, transportation, en-
ergy, education and recreation, and to satisfy 
his curiosity about the unknown. 
It has always been IBM's strength to antici-
pate the needs of the world's marketplaces; 
to innovate products and programs for special 
applications; to guide the installation of its 
products and insure their continuous reliability. 
The Company's strength is heavily de-
pendent upon one factor: its people—the engi-
neers, scientists, marketing representatives, 
managers, administrators, who through the de-
mands of their jobs and by their own incli-
nations as well, smdy the elements of human 
life and seek ways to improve social health. 
A review of the marketplace can offer only 
a limited selection at best. But even this covers 
a wide territory of purposes. Most important, 
however, is the probability that each of these 
computer applications will suggest new ways 
in which computers can be put to work for hu-
man benefit. 
Fresh, Pure Water... 
One of Italy's largest petrochemical plants 
desalts millions of cubic meters of seawater per 
year. The process, which is controlled by an 
IBM computer system, provides water for plant 
use, without drawing on the scarce reserves 
of Sardinia.

Nobel Prizewinner: 
Dr. Leo Esaki of IBM's Thomas J. Watson 
Research Center is one of three 1973 recipients 
of the Nobel Prize in Physics. Dr. Esaki's award 
was based on Ph.D. studies he conducted in Japan 
during the late 1950's while a physicist at the 
Sony Corporation. His investigation of the elec-
tron tunneling phenomenon in semiconduc-
tors enabled him to show for the first time the 
conditions under which tunneling could be 
controlled. 
Dr. Esaki shares half of this year's Nobel 
Prize in Physics with Dr. Ivar Giaever of Gen-
eral Electric. The other half of the prize was 
given to Dr. Brian Josephson of Cambridge 
University. The work of all three men rec-
ognized by the Nobel Committee was in the 
field of electron tunneling. Whereas Dr. Esaki's 
work was in the area of semiconductors, that 
of Drs. Giaever and Josephson, which followed 
shortly after, was in the area of superconduc-
tors—materials that exhibit no resistance to elec-
tron flow at extremely low temperatures. Soon 
after Drs. Josephson and Giaever reported their 
results, an IBM researcher realized that their 
findings could form the basis of a new type of 
computer switch. The first device was built 
at IBM Research in 1965 and work on the new 
switches, now called Josephson junctions, 
continues. Their present switching speed is con-
siderably beyond anything that has been at-
tained with semiconductor devices. 
Tunneling is a surprising implication of 
modern quantum theory which says that 
electrons behave like waves as well as particles. 
Because of their wave nature, electrons can 
penetrate a barrier of insulating material sepa-
rating two conductors if the barrier is suf-
ficiently thin. 
Dr. Esaki became a resident consultant 
to the IBM Poughkeepsie lab in i960 and he 
has been with IBM Research ever since, working 
mostly in the field of electron tunneling, for 
the last six years as an IBM Fellow. He continues 
his research in semiconductors today, and his 
goal now is to design a man-made crystal 
structure which exploits electron tunneling to 
create ultra high-frequency devices. 
Dr. Esaki, his wife and their three chil-
dren visit Japan often, and each year some 100 
Japanese—scientists, i B M e r s , academicians-
come to visit him. Although the Esakis keep 
their Japanese passports, their roots in the U.S. 
are strong. They have many American friends 
and Dr. Esaki's colleagues at Yorktown Heights 
were elated over his winning of the award. 
"I'm glad I am considered an American," 
says Dr. Esaki. "And many Japanese think 
of me as Japanese. I'm glad of that too. 
because science is international, and IBM is an 
international business."

New Retail System... 
At the retail store, a salesperson passes 
a hand-held "wand" across a magnetically 
encoded price tag to record a sale. A point-of-
sale terminal records information describing 
the item and displays its price on a lighted panel. 
To complete the purchase, it computes the 
amount due and change to be returned, prints a 
customer receipt and a journal tape for the 
store. The system includes a unit that prints and 
encodes data on price tags. By capturing this 
information at the point of sale, a firm can de-
velop other applications such as inventory 
control and credit authorization.

At the Checkout Counter... 
Fast checkout wiil be possible with IBM's new 
point-of-sale supermarket system, scheduled for 
delivery in late 1974. A high-speed scanner, 
using a laser light source to read a coded symbol 
printed on each grocery item, will identify 
the product and its manufacturer. The checker 
needs only to slide the item over the scanner, 
then directly into the grocery bag. As the prod-
uct is scanned, its name and price appear on 
a panel for the customer's view. A receipt, with 
name or description of each item as well as 
its price, is printed at the same time. 
The system includes a store control unit 
that can accommodate up to 24 point-of-sale 
terminals and scanners in a single store. In 
addition to the check-out function, the system 
can help balance cash registers, calculate the 
correct ending total for each checkstand and 
transmit a report to the central computer 
detailing receipts of cash, checks and store and 
vendor coupons. It can also compile a sum-
mary sales report on each of the approximately 
10,000 items handled by a typical super-
market, and help management with such oper-
ations as reordering, inventory management 
and labor scheduling. 
New Worldwide Finance System... 
The IBM 3600 finance communications system 
was developed to serve the world market of 
financial institutions, fast becoming dependent 
upon data processing to cope with the enormous 
flow of paper generated by the money business. 
Products made for international use nor-
mally have problems with variations in electrical 
power, currencies and language. This system 
was engineered not only to deal with these, but 
also to adapt to the differences in the ways 
other societies handle their banking transactions. 
From the start, the finance system project re-
ceived help to an unusual degree from IBM mar-
keting people brought in from all the major 
banking countries. 
The finance system is a family of six ter-
minal devices which permit self-service banking 
at any hour and let tellers, even those at remote 
branch banks, enter, update and retrieve cus-
tomer data and financial information within sec-
onds. It offers financial institutions of all kinds 
a wide selection of data entry and printing capa-
bilities for handling such typical customer 
transactions as cash withdrawals, checking and 
savings account activity and installment loan 
and mortgage payments. 
A self-service banking terminal can be 
installed in a bank lobby or mounted through 
the wall, to issue cash and answer account status 
queries every hour of the year. 
A finance communication controller can 
supervise all functions of the system's terminals 
and control data transmission between the ter-
minals and the main computer. 
A versatile countertop unit has a keyboard 
for entering data and a visual display panel 
for both verifying entered data and answering 
teller inquiries on the status of customer 
accounts. An important optional feature of this 
keyboard display unit permits encoding and 
reading of magnetic stripes on passbooks, credit 
cards and special ID cards. 
There also are three printers that offer a 
variety of features to meet a bank's requirements 
for passbook posting and printing of customers' 
receipts, journals, reports and statements.

New Storage Technology... 
The IBM Data Module, a new concept in 
information storage, improves data reliability 
by combining, for the first time, disk record-
ing surfaces, access arms and read/write heads 
into a single removable cartridge. The same 
head that writes data on a disk surface reads the 
data back into a computer, eliminating prob-
lems of head-to-track misalignment. 
New Data Entry System... 
To get information into a computer quickly, 
accurately, this system has a new "Diskette" stor-
age medium. It weighs just over an ounce, 
looks like a small phonograph record, is reusable, 
and holds as much information as 3,000 IBM 
8o-column punch cards. To record, the operator 
drops a diskette into a slot and uses a type-
writer-like keyboard. Data on diskettes can be 
converted to magnetic tape for computer 
processing or, with the use of a special unit, 
entered directly into the computer. 
To Coordinate Transmission... 
A communications controller can be installed 
in remote locations, gathering information 
from low-speed lines and transmitting it to a 
computer economically over higher-speed 
lines. IBM's new communications controller is 
designed for smaller networks. It has process-
ing capabilities and in a teleprocessing network, 
it can also free the central computer from 
many data handling tasks. 
Double Disk Storage... 
A significant, cost-saving extension of the 
3330 disk storage family will double the direct-
access storage capacity while maintaining 
high performance. The newest is functionally 
similar to previous models. It requires no 
additional floor space. 
More Tape Storage Capacity... 
There are three new models of the very suc-
cessful IBM 3803/3420 family of magnetic tape 
subsystems. They provide the densest data re-
cording capability yet offered to IBM magnetic 
tape users, tripling the amount of information 
that can be stored on a tape and quadrupling the 
data rate. The new tape drives were developed 
at Boulder, Colorado and are being assembled 
in Boulder, France and Brazil. 
High-Speed Processing... 
A new high-speed document processor, guided 
by a single operator, can read and sort a typi-
cal mixture of checks at a rated speed in excess 
of 125,000 per hour, and print a unique iden-
tification number on each. Improved sorting 
productivity helps a bank quickly obtain credit 
for checks drawn on other banks, helping to 
make more interest-earning funds available. An 
optional microfilming feature can make sharp 
film records of the checks at the same time they 
are being read and sorted by the document 
processor. 
Larger Capacity... 
System/7, rugged, highly reliable, can measure,

System/3 Expanded...

Increased Memory...

New memory sizes allow users to implement

A Typewriter that Erases... 
IBM's new, fast-selling Correcting "Selectric" 
Typewriter helps speed up most typing to 
"rough draft" pace. Corrections are almost as

Equal Opportunity... 
In the United States, there has been a company-
wide effort to recruit and give more respon-
sibility to women and minority group members. 
Of new hires in 1973, 24.9% were minority 
group members and 35% were women. The 
number of minority employees rose from 6.1% 
of the total employee population five years 
ago to 9.7% by year's end, 1973. Over the same 
period, the number of minority managers 
more than tripled, from 300 to 945, and minor-
ity members of the sales force rose from 
2.6% to 8.1%. Today, more than 1 5 % of IBM's 
employees in the United States are women, 
and during the past five years, the number of 
women in management positions has increased 
from 235 to over 700. Six DP Branch Managers 
are women, and women represent nearly 4.5% 
of the sales force—up from 1.6% five years ago.

sions has almost doubled since 1965. The Com-
pany's Equal Opportunities programs are 
designed to increase the number of minority 
and women employees and to assure that indi-
vidual potential is fully realized.

Opportunity Programs is available on request 
to those who wish it. Address requests to the 
Secretary of the Company. 
IBM Fund for Community Service... 
Begun in 1972, this flexible program allows 
IBM employees to obtain funds for projects of 
community organizations of particular inter-
est to them. The organization must be one that 
receives active help from the employee. The 
money can be released simply upon approval of 
the employee's immediate manager and one 
other executive. By the end of 1973, more than 
1300 projects had been approved. 
IBM Faculty Loan Program... 
For three years now, the IBM Corporation has 
loaned IBM professionals to colleges and univer-
sities with substantial numbers of minority 
or disadvantaged students. The objective of the 
program is to provide faculty or administra-
tive support to these institutions to help them 
improve the educational experiences of their 
students. From an original 18 Black colleges, the 
program has been expanded to 31 schools in 
1974, including some that serve American 
Indians, Spanish-surnamed, and students in 
depressed areas. 
Social Service Leaves... 
IBM has, for a number of years, permitted em-
ployees to take leaves of absence for various 
forms of public service. In April of 1970, 
Thomas J. Watson, Jr., then Chairman of the 
Board, encouraged this kind of action. Since 
that time, the number of employees on social 
service leaves has increased significantly. 
During the past year, over 1 3 0 employees were 
on leave. They performed a wide range of 
community services such as work on conser-
vation and environmental protection, training 
minorities for jobs and in better business 
methods and increasing the efifectiveness of 
criminal rehabilitation programs.

Notes to 
Consolidated Financial Statements: 
Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles of Consolidation: The consolidated 
financial statements include the accounts of 
International Business Machines Corporation 
and its domestic and foreign subsidiary com-
panies, including The Service Bureau Corpora-
tion to the date of its sale on January 12, 1973. 
Translation of Foreign Currency 
Amounts: 
Foreign assets and liabilities are translated to 
U.S. dollars at approximate year-end exchange 
rates, except that plant, rental machines and 
other property and long-term debt are translated 
at approximate rates prevailing when acquired 
or incurred. Income and expense items are 
translated at average rates of exchange prevail-
ing during the year, except depreciation which 
is calculated at the approximate rates prevail-
ing when the properties were acquired. Exchange 
adjustments are included in earnings currently. 
Gross Income: Gross income is recognized 
from sales when the product is shipped or 
in certain cases upon customer acceptance, from 
services over the contractual period or as the 
services are performed, and from rentals in the 
month in which they accrue. 
Depreciation: With minor exceptions, depre-
ciation of domestic properties is computed 
using the sum of the years-digits method. De-
preciation of foreign properties is generally 
computed using the straight-line method. How-
ever, methods which provide higher charges 
in early years of asset life, such as the declining-
balance method, are used by certain foreign 
subsidiaries. 
Retirement Plans: Current service costs 
are accrued currently. Prior service costs result-
ing from improvements in the plans are 
amortized generally over 10 years, except that 
certain foreign subsidiaries use longer periods 
not exceeding 25 years. 
Expenses: Marketing expenses and develop-
ment and engineering expenses are charged 
against income as they are incurred. 
Income Taxes: Income tax expense is based 
on reported earnings before income taxes 
and thus includes the effects of timing differ-
ences between reported and taxable earnings 
which arise because certain transactions are 
included in taxable earnings in other years. 
Investment tax credits are deferred and amor-
tized as a reduction of income tax expense 
over the average useful life of the applicable 
classes of property.

Litigation 
In January 1969, the Department of Justice 
filed a civil antitrust complaint against IBM 
under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, 
charging the Company with monopolizing 
commerce in general purpose digital computers 
in the United States. In a preliminary memo-
randum on relief dated October 12,1972, the 
government requests divestiture relief in 
the form of a reorganization of IBM into several 
separate, independent and competitive 
entities, and interim injunctive relief to force 
IBM, pending completion of divestiture, to 
stop certain practices which the government 
claims lead to a monopoly. A trial date has 
been set for October 1974. 
In April 1970, Xerox Corporation filed a 
lawsuit against IBM charging patent infringe-
ment and misuse of confidential information 
in connection with the IBM Copier. In August 
1973, Xerox filed a supplemental lawsuit 
concerning the IBM Copier II. Xerox seeks 
injunctive relief and damages in an unspecified 
amount. 
In January and March 1972, the Telex Cor-
poration instituted two lawsuits against IBM 
alleging violations of the federal antitrust laws 
and claiming damages, which after trebling 
aggregated $1,084 million. These resulted in an 
amended judgment in November 1973 in 
which Telex was awarded trebled damages of 
$259.5 million and certain injunctive relief. 
IBM continues to deny the Telex charges and is 
seeking reversal on appeal. Accordingly, no 
provision has been made in the financial results 
for the contingent liability associated with this 
litigation. It is presently contemplated that 
any amounts which may ultimately become pay-

able would be reported as a restatement of the 
earnings of the applicable prior years. With 
respect to IBM's counterclaim, Telex was found 
to have infringed IBM copyrights and mis-
appropriated IBM trade secrets. The judgment 
on the counterclaim awards IBM damages of 
$21.9 million, requires Telex to return IBM con-
fidential documents and places certain re-
straints on their use of former IBM employees. 
In the last quarter of 1973, five separate 
suits were instituted against IBM alleging 
federal antitrust law violations similar to those 
litigated in the Telex case. The plaintiffs, 
California Computer Products, Inc., Trans-

All of the above cases were brought in

Stock Purchase Plan 
Under the IBM Employees 1971 Stock 
Purchase Plan, employees on the annual offering 
date, each July i, who are not participants in a 
Stock Option Plan, may purchase IBM's 
capital stock one share at a time through pay-
roll deductions not exceeding 10% of their 
compensation. The price an employee pays for 
a share of stock is equal to 8 5 % of the market 
price on the annual offering date or on the date 
he has accumulated enough money to buy the 
share—whichever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 1,052,409 shares in 
1973, for which $265 million was paid to IBM 
and credited to the capital stock account. At 
December 31,1973,2,597,466 shares were re-
served for sale under the 1971 Plan. Numbers 
of shares reflect the 2 5 % stock split effected 
May 10,1973. 
Stock Option Plans 
In April 1971, stockholders approved the 
continuation for a five-year period of a Stock 
Option Plan for officers and other key employees. 
Under this Plan, as under the 1966 Plan, op-
tions may be granted for the purchase of IBM's 
capital stock at not less than 100% of the 
market price on the day the option is granted. 
Qualified options granted under both Plans 
have a maximum duration of five years and 
shares become purchasable in four annual in-
stallments commencing one year after the 
date of grant. 
The 1971 Plan also provides that non-
qualified options with a maximum duration of 
ten years may be granted alone or simultane-
ously with and for the same number of shares as 
qualified options. Nonqualified options 
granted through December 31,1972 were 
granted simultaneously with qualified options 
but purchases can not be made until five years 
and one day after the date of grant, and the 
number of shares purchasable is reduced on a 
one-for-one basis as shares are purchased under 
the related qualified option. Beginning in 
1973, nonqualified options were granted alone 
and shares become purchasable in four annual 
installments commencing one year after the 
date of grant. 
In accordance with the Plans, installments 
are cumulative and the number of shares that 
may be purchased and the price per share are ad-
justed for stock dividends and splits effected 
after the option is granted.

Retirement Plans 
The Company and its domestic subsidiaries 
have had for many years a trusteed, noncontrib-
utory retirement plan covering substantially 
all their employees. At December 31,1973, 
there were over 6,700 individuals receiving 
benefits under this plan. Certain subsidiaries 
outside the United States have retirement plans 
under which funds are deposited with trustees, 
reserves are provided, or annuities are purchased 
under group contracts. The cost of all plans 
totaled approximately $258 million for the year 
1973, compared with $221 million for the 
year 1972.
