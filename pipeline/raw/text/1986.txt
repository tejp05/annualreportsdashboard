Letter to Stockholders 
1986 was a difficult year for IBM. Worldwide orders and shipments 
did not match the levels of 1985. Contributing to this decline were 
sluggish capital spending in North America, moderating economic 
performance in some non-U.S. countries, and unsatisfactory 
levels of demand for parts of our product line. 
Worldwide net earnings for the year ended December 31,1986, 
were $4,789 million, down 26.9 percent from 1985's earnings of 
$6,555 million. Earnings per share for the year were $7.81 compared 
with $10.67 for 1985. The after-tax margin was 9.3 percent in 1986 
compared with 13.1 percent in 1985. Average shares outstanding 
were 612.8 million in 1986 and 614.1 million in 1985. 
Earnings before income taxes were $8,389 million in 1986 and 
$11,619 million in 1985. Before-tax margins were 16.4 percent in 1986 
and 23.2 percent in 1985. 
Worldwide gross income was $51,250 million in 1986, up 2.4 
percent from the prior year's $50,056 million. Currency rates had a 
positive elfect on financial results for 1986. It is estimated that 
the lower value of the dollar compared with 1985 rates improved 
1986 gross income by $4,365 million and improved net earnings 
by $645 million. 
Gross income from non-U.S. operations in 1986, included in 
consolidated results, was $25,888 million, up 20.2 percent from 
$21,545 million in 1985. Non-U.S. net earnings of $3,193 million in 
1986 showed an increase of 3.7 percent over 1985's earnings of 
$3,078 million. 
For the quarter ended December 31, net earnings were 
$1,390 million, compared with $2,681 million for the same period 
last year. Per share earnings were $2.28 compared with $4.36 for the 
1985 period. Gross income for the fourth quarter was $16,945 million, 
compared with $17,155 million for the fourth quarter of 1985. 
Primary Objectives 
Since 1970, the information industry has achieved a remarkable 
record of growth. That record is not a line moving straight up, and it 
reflects several previous downturns. The past two years, however, 
have been as difficult as any in our experience—a period of 
significant restructuring and retrenchment. The industry that is 
emerging is vital and dynamic, with vast potential. It is also more 
competitive, worldwide, than at any time before. 
In this environment, IBM is taking the actions and making 
the investments to continue as a successful world-class competitor. 
We are moving quickly to accomplish three primary objectives: 
• Strengthening and renewing our product line; 
• Significantly increasing the number of IBM people directly serving 
our customers, while sharpening our focus on their requirements; 
• Streamlining our operations and redeploying resources to make 
the company as lean and vigorous as possible. 
Stronger Product Line 
During the early part of the 1980s, our industry delivered 
information-handling products in record volumes and computing 
capacity expanded dramatically. Between 1982 and 1984, for 
example, industry revenues from workstations alone increased 
at a compound annual growth rate averaging 50 percent. 
With the benefits of individual workstations and systems now 
well-established, customers have told us that their priority—and 
ours—must be to improve information systems productivity across 
their organizations. During 1986, we introduced products to 
help customers extend the value of their investment in equipment, 
programming and the skills of their people, while providing 
a clear direction for future growth. 
In mid-range computers, where competition is especially 
strong, 1986 was a year of major product introductions and signifi-
cant enhancements to existing systems. The new 9370 Information 
System extends the performance of IBM'S System/370 to four 
models compact enough to be installed in an office. System/370 cus-
tomers may now choose from the broadest range of performance 
in the industry, and 9370 models can immediately and productively 
use thousands of programs already in the System/370 library. 
Enhancements to the System/36 and System/38 mid-range 
computers increase their performance in both standalone applica-
tions and as parts of larger networks. At the same time, new pro-
gram products dramatically expand customers' ability to connect 
workstations and mid-range and mainframe computers, and to 
share information and resources. While work remains to be done to 
improve connectivity and consistency across the range of our 
products, the foundation is in place, and we will build on it in the 
months to come. 
In 1986, we added two entry-level models to the IBM 3090 
series, IBM'S most advanced computers and the industry's 
first to use one-million-bit memory chips. We subsequently

Comimitment to Customer Partnerships 
While renewing our product line, we are also strengthening our 
ability to work in partnership with our customers. 
The number of IBM people in field marketing and direct 
customer support is being substantially increased, principally 
through redeployments from other parts of the business. By the 
end of 1987, IBM people working directly with our U.S. customers 
will have increased by more than 20 percent in a two-year period. 
We are also rebalancing resources in our World Trade opera-
tions. In each case, a key objective is to increase our presence in 
customers' offices and our ability to work with them to identify 
opportunities to extend the usefulness of information handling 
in their operations. 
Software and services continue to be the fastest-growing 
areas of our business. Increasingly, those services include working 
with customers to plan and implement complex information 
networks that link all levels of their organizations together with 
their suppliers, dealers or other business associates. Based on 
experience gained in similar projects for the federal government, 
we now offer commercial customers support in developing and 
integrating large-scale systems including IBM hardware, software 
and services, as well as those from other companies. 
We continue to find new ways to make it easier and more 
attractive to do business with IBM. Charges for certain software

During 1986, we took actions to further reduce cost, expense 
and unneeded capacity. At the same time, and with the essential 
cooperation of IBM employees, we continued to maintain our 
tradition of full employment. 
At virtually every level of the organization, our staffs have been 
restructured and substantially reduced. Many people were asked 
to accept assignments in our marketing branch offices or other parts 
of the business; in all, more than 12,000 people in our U.S. and 
World Trade operations are being redeployed—moving to new jobs, 
new locations, or both. In addition, we initiated a highly success-
ful early retirement incentive that will help reduce our U.S. 
employee population by more than 12,000 in 1987, and announced 
similar incentives in four other countries. We appreciate the 
willingness of so many people to begin new careers within IBM, and 
are deeply grateful for the long-term contributions of those who 
will be retiring. 
To bring capacity in line with current and projected needs, 
activities at several U.S. locations were consolidated with 
operations elsewhere in IBM. At our Greencastle, Ind., site, which 
we are closing, employees were offered positions elsewhere 
in the company, and we have offered to take significant steps to 
alleviate the effect of this change on the community. 
While making prudent reductions in expenditures for new 
plant and equipment, compared with recent years, we continue to 
make essential strategic investments. On average, the tools and 
processes in our production facilities are less than five years old, 
helping to ensure our competitiveness in manufacturing tech-
nology and cost. 
We continue to concentrate on reducing travel, meeting and 
other discretionary expenses and to give close attention to 
managing inventories and assets. Overall, IBM'S balance sheet 
remains among the strongest of any company in the world. 
The result of these actions is a leaner, more vigorous IBM 
company, prepared to be as efficient as required in order to 
participate fully in the information industry.

Creating New Opportunities 
The information industry continues to be one with potential for 
unparalleled long-term growth. Underpinning this outlook is the 
ongoing demand for new productivity tools that allow businesses 
and other organizations to grow and prosper in an increasingly 
competitive world. At the same time, advances in technology 
continue to drive costs lower, making information products and 
systems available and affordable for a wider range of customers 
and applications. 
The essential link between promise and performance is, 
as it has always been, IBM'S ability to meet customers' needs. 
That is why, to the continuing goals of product leadership, growth, 
efficiency and profitability, IBM is setting an additional goal for 
itself: enhancing the company's partnerships with its customers. 
Key to this is the ability to understand customers' needs... 
to develop and deliver solutions that match their requirements, 
that create new value in their operations, and that are high-
quality, easy to use and competitively priced. 
IBM is focusing its resources and its energies on these objec-
tives. The following pages describe some of the results: innovative 
IBM products and services; more IBM people available to solve 
customers' problems; and a streamlined organization, more 
responsive and committed to providing the best customer service 
of any company. 
When the United Kingdom restructured its 
financial markets and opened its doors to foreign 
investment houses last fall, the London Stock 
Exchange chose IBM Personal Computers and 
PC Networks as the vehicles for providing brokers 
access to Exhange data services. As part of the 
market automation, Exchange members have installed 
over 5,000 IBM PCs and 200 PC Networks.

Flexible Software Solutions 
While new programming products were directed toward 
improving customers' ability to connect systems and networks, 
IBM continued to enhance existing operating systems and 
develop new application programs that give customers greater 
flexibility in addressing their information-handling requirements. 
In October, the company introduced eight innovative packages 
designed to help customers who are installing new applications 
become productive more quickly. Known as SolutionPacs, the 
packaged sets include applications and services for specific system 
configurations. Five of the new SolutionPacs are geared to retail 
stores, colleges, financial institutions and manufacturing plants. 
The others can be applied across a broad range of industries. 
The company also announced a major change in its pricing of 
selected licensed programs, introducing graduated one-time 
charges for more than 90 System/370 programs. Under the new 
pricing approach, software charges will be based on the computing 
power and performance of the system on which the programs 
are run, making these selected programs more affordable to entry-
level customers. 
The company also introduced an array of enhanced Personal 
Computer applications to meet diverse customer requirements 
ranging from word processing and bookkeeping to education. One 
addition was an adult literacy program called PALS that helps 
adolescents and adults who read at or below the fifth-grade level. 
The PALS literacy system, which utilizes the touch-screen technology 
of the IBM InfoWindow, is patterned after IBM'S Writing to Read 
program for teaching young children to read. Introduced about 
two years ago. Writing to Read is now in use by more than 300,000 
kindergarten and first-grade children across the U.S. 
Making software easier to install and learn continues to be an 
important objective for IBM.TO this end, the company has 
established several software usability laboratories to evaluate IBM 
program products. At these laboratories, researchers observe 
customers as they use application programs under study. Their 
findings are then used to identify ways to make future versions of 
the programs easier to use.

In the dynamic and competitive information industry, IBM'S growth 
and profitability depend on accurate anticipation of changing

Organizations Realigned 
During 1986, IBM realigned operations within its U.S. marketing, 
manufacturing and development, and World Trade organizations 
to increase the focus on customer requirements, improve efficiency 
and reduce overhead across the company. 
In September, IBM'S Information Systems Group headquarters 
staff and the headquarters staffs of the group's North-Central 
and South-West Marketing Divisions were consolidated into a single 
national support organization. This new organization will be 
responsible for providing marketing plans and a full range of support 
services to the company's field marketing organizations. The 
economies resulting from this realignment permitted about 2,000 
headquarters personnel to be reassigned to field marketing 
positions in the U.S.

Results of Operations 
Results of operations in 1986 were disappointing. 
Continuing sluggishness in the U.S. economy, 
slack capital investment in North America, 
moderating economic performance in some 
countries outside the U.S. and unsatisfactory 
levels of demand for parts of our product line 
were among the factors contributing to a 
decline in worldwide orders and shipments 
from 1985 levels. 
Worldwide gross income increased 2.4 per-
cent in 1986 to $51.3 billion, following an increase 
of 9.0 percent in 1985 over 1984. Gross income 
from non-U.S. operations increased 20.2 percent 
to $ 25.9 billion in 1986, while revenue from U.S. 
operations declined ii.o percent to $ 25.4 billion. 
Outside the U.S., the strongest performance was 
in the Asia/Pacific area, where gross income 
increased 30.0 percent over 1985. This followed a 
22.4 percent increase in 1985 over 1984. In the 
Europe/Middle East/Africa area, the largest of 
IBM's non-U.S. operations, gross income grew 
21.4 percent over that of 1985, to $17.1 billion. 
The weakening of the U.S. dollar had a posi-
tive effect on reported financial results in 1986. 
The graphs on page 32 depict the movement, 
relative to the dollar, of the six major currencies 
in which IBM conducts most of its non-US. busi-
ness. The dollar began to weaken against most of 
these currencies in early 1985 and continued to 
do so through 1986. It is estimated that had cur-
rency rates remained constant with those of 1985, 
worldwide gross income and net earnings in 
1986 would have been lower by $4,365 million 
and $ 645 million, respectively. 
Worldwide net earnings in 1986 were $4.8 
billion, down 26.9 percent from the previous 
year. Net earnings in 1985 were essentially flat 
when compared with a record performance 
in 1984. 
Gross income from sales was down slightly 
from 1985 due entirely to a decline in sales of 
installed rental equipment. Gross income from 
sales, excluding sales of installed rental equip-
ment, increased 2.2 percent over 1985, following 
an increase of 24.6 percent in 1985 over 1984. 
Gross income from maintenance services and 
program products grew 21.5 percent and 32.4 
percent, respectively, and continued to grow as a 
percent of total gross income. 
The composition of gross income continues 
to change. Five years ago, gross income from 
rentals, combined with that from sales of 
installed equipment, was 45.8 percent of the 
total. In 1985, this declined to 11.2 percent, and in 
1986, was down to 6.4 percent. Gross income 
from maintenance services and program prod-
ucts combined grew from 16.4 percent of total 
gross income in 1982 to 25.2 percent in 1986.

Notes to Consolidated Financial Statements: 
Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles 
of Consolidation: 
Translation of 
Non-U. S. Currency Amounts: 
The consolidated financial statements include the accounts of International Business Machines Corporation 
and its U. S. and non-U. S. subsidiary companies, other than the wholly owned IBM Credit Corporation and non-U. S. 
finance subsidiaries, for which the equity method is used. Investments in joint ventures, and other companies in 
which IBM has a 20 to 50 percent ownership, are accounted for by the equity method. Investments of less than 20 percent 
are accounted for by the cost method. 
For non-U S. subsidiaries which operate in a local currency environment, assets and liabilities are translated to 
U. S. dollars at year-end exchange rates. Income and expense items are translated at average rates of exchange prevailing 
during the year. Translation adjustments are accumulated in a separate component of stockholders' equity. For non-U. S. 
subsidiaries and branches which operate in U. S. dollars or whose economic environment is highly inflationary, 
inventories and plant, rental machines and other property are translated at approximate rates prevailing when acquired. 
All other assets and liabilities are translated at year-end exchange rates. Inventories charged to cost of sales and 
depreciation are remeasured at historical rates. All other income and expense items are translated at average rates of 
exchange prevailing during the year. Gains and losses which result from remeasurement are included in earnings. 
Gross Income: 
Program Products: 
Depreciation: 
Gross income is recognized from sales or sales-type leases when the product is shipped, from program products when 
the program is shipped or as monthly license fees accrue, from maintenance and other services over the contractual 
period or as the services are performed, and from rentals under operating leases in the month in which they accrue. 
IBM equipment offered under term leases by IBM's finance subsidiaries is accounted for by IBM as outright sales. 
Costs related to the conceptual formulation and design of licensed programs are expensed as research and development. 
Costs incurred subsequent to establishment of technological feasibility to produce the finished product are generally 
capitalized as program products assets. The annual amortization of these assets is the greater of the amount computed 
based on the estimated revenue distribution over the products' revenue-producing lives or the straight-line method, 
but not in excess of six years. Ongoing costs to support or service licensed programs are charged to period cost. 
Plant, rental machines and other property are carried at cost and depreciated over their estimated useful lives. 
Depreciation of assets acquired subsequent to December 31,1983 is computed using the straight-line method. Depreciation 
of assets acquired prior to January i, 1984 is computed using either accelerated methods or the straight-line method. 
Goodwill: 
The excess of the cost over the fair value of the net assets of purchased businesses is recorded as goodwill and 
amortized on a straight-line basis over 20 years. Goodwill related to equity investments is included in the investment 
and amortized on a straight-line basis over 20 years. 
Retirement Plans and 
Other Postretirement Benefits: 
Selling Expenses: 
Current service costs of retirement plans are accrued currently. Prior service costs resulting from improvements 
in the plans are amortized over the average remaining service period of employees expected to receive benefits. 
Postretirement health care and life insurance benefits are fully accrued when the employee retires. 
Selling expenses are charged against income as incurred. 
Income Taxes: 
Income tax expense is based on reported earnings before income taxes. It thus includes the effects of timing differences 
between reported and taxable earnings that arise because certain transactions are included in taxable earnings in 
other years. Deferred investment tax credits are being amortized as a reduction of income tax expense over the average 
useful life of the applicable classes of property. 
Inventories: 
Raw materials, operating supplies, finished goods and work in process are stated at the lower of average cost or market.

Retirement Plans 
The company and its subsidiaries have 
retirement plans covering substantially all 
employees. The total cost of all plans for 1986, 
1985 and 1984 w&s $585 million, $868 million and 
$1,096 million, respectively. 
Effective January i, 1986, the company 
and its principal non-U. S. subsidiaries imple-
mented Statement of Financial Accounting 
Standards No. 87—Employers' Accounting for 
Pensions. As a result, the actuarial method 
for determining the annual cost was changed 
from the Frozen Initial Liability method to 
the prescribed Projected Unit Credit method. 
Other changes made relating to the adoption 
of the new standard included the revision of 
actuarial assumptions, a change in the amortiza-
tion of prior service costs from 15 years to a 
straight-line basis over the average remaining 
service period of employees expected to receive 
benefits, and an assumption for modified career 
average plans that the average earnings base 
period will be updated to the years prior to 
retirement. 
The decrease in pension cost in 1986 re-
sulted principally from the adoption of this stan-
dard, partially offset by amendments to the U.S. 
plans including the effect of special termination 
benefits. The decrease in pension cost in 1985 
resulted principally from favorable returns on 
plan assets. Consistent with this experience, 
the rate of return assumption was increased. 
Additionally, 1985 cost also decreased as a result 
of a change in the amortization period for prior 
service cost from 10 to 15 years. 
It is the company's policy to fund accrued 
costs to the extent that such costs are tax deduct-
ible. The assets of the various plans include cor-
porate equities, government securities, corporate 
debt securities and income-producing real estate.

Stock Purchase Plan 
In April 1986, stockholders approved a 
new five-year employees stock purchase plan, 
effective July i, 1986, which authorizes up 
to 25 million shares of capital stock for the plan. 
The plan enables employees who are not 
participants in a stock option plan to purchase 
IBM's capital stock through payroll deductions 
of up to 10% of eligible compensation. The price 
an employee pays for a share of stock is 85% of 
the average market price on the date the employee 
has accumulated enough money to buy a share. 
Under the 1986 plan and its predecessor 
1981 plan, employees purchased 5,341,750 shares 
during the year, all of which were treasury 
shares, for which $638 million was paid to IBM. 
At December 31,1986, 22,350,469 reserved 
unissued shares remain available for purchase 
under the 1986 plan. 
Stock Option Plans 
In April 1986, stockholders approved a new 
five-year stock option plan which authorizes 
up to 12 million shares of capital stock for the 
plan. The 1986 Stock Option Plan and its prede-
cessor plans provide for granting officers and 
other key employees options to purchase IBM's 
capital stock at 100% of the market price on 
the date of grant. Options have a maximum 
duration of 10 years and become exercisable in 
up to four annual installments, commencing 
one year from date of grant. 
The plans are administered by the 
Executive Compensation Committee of the 
Board of Directors. The Committee may, at its 
discretion, grant Stock Appreciation Rights 
(SARs) to holders of options. Such rights offer 
optionees the alternative of electing not to exer-
cise the related stock option, but to receive 
instead an amount in cash, stock, or a combina-
tion of cash and stock, equivalent to the 
difference between the option price and the 
average market price of IBM stock on the date of 
exercising the right. 
The plans also provide, subject to Commit-
tee approval, that payment by the optionee 
upon exercise of an option may be made using 
IBM stock as well as cash.

While IBM operates primarily in a single industry 
segment and manages its worldwide business 
as such, gross income has been categorized into 
major classes of product. 
For purposes of classifying similar 
products of the Information-Handling industry 
segment, user programmable equipment having 
the capability of manipulating data arithmeti-
cally or logically and making calculations, in a 
manner directly addressable by the user through 
the operation of a stored program, has been 
classified as Processors, except that small busi-
ness computers and intelligent workstations are 
included under Office Systems/Workstations. 
Typewriters have also been classified under 
Office Systems/Workstations. Peripherals in-
clude printers, copiers, storage and telecommu-
nication devices. Peripheral functions embedded 
in processors are classified with Processors. 
Maintenance Services consist of separately 
billed charges for maintenance. Program 
Products include both applications and systems 
software. Other consists principally of supplies 
and unit record equipment. 
Some products logically fit in more than one 
class and are assigned to a specific class based on 
a variety of factors. Over time, products tend to 
overlap, merge into or split from existing classes 
as a result of changing technologies, market 
perceptions, and/or customer use. For example. 
market demand may create requirements for 
technological enhancements to permit a 
peripheral product (e.g., printer), to be function-
ally integrated with a display, telecommunication 
device and a processor to form an office system. 
Such interchangeability and technological 
progress tend to make year to year comparisons 
less valid than they would be in an industry 
less subject to rapid change. 
Federal Systems consists of specialized 
information-handling products and services 
primarily for the United States defense, space 
and other agencies. 
Other Business consists of training and test-
ing materials for school, home and industrial use.
