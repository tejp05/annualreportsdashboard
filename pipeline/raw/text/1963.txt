To the Stockholders: 
The IBM Company last year continued to supply 
its customers throughout the free world with a 
steady flow of valuable new products and services. 
The Company's gross income from its domes-
tic operations amounted to 12,059,610,111, an 
increase of $134,388,254, or 7 % , over 
1962. 
Gross income from IBM regular products showed 
an increase of 12.1 % last year. Military products 
gross income showed a 33.3 % decrease compared 
with 1962. This decrease reflects a reduction in 
supply or production type contracts and a greater 
emphasis toward research and development con-
tracts. 
Earnings were $290,463,523, a $49,076,255 
increase over the prior year. 
Earnings per share were $10.45, based on the 
27,792,981 shares outstanding at the end of the 
year. This compares with earnings of $8.72 per 
share based on the 27,678,322 shares outstanding 
on December 31, 1962. 
The IBM World Trade Corporation, a wholly-
owned subsidiary which carries on IBM'S business 
through its subsidiaries outside the United States, 
had a gross income of $788,009,311, an increase 
of $134,894,098 over 1962. IBM World Trade's 
earnings were $ 104,604,052 last year, an increase 
of $17,924,966 over the year before.

Serving Customers Around the World 
IBM provided its world-wide customers with many 
valuable new services and products in 1963. 
These new IBM methods for solving informa-
tion-handling problems, together with those al-
ready in use, are enabling the Company to serve 
the widest range of customer needs in its history. 
An idea of the scope of this service is presented 
in a special report beginning on page 17. 
IBM serves its customers through a growing 
family of data processing systems ranging from 
small accounting machines to powerful com-
puters. These systems are supported by more spe-
cialized units used to feed raw data into this 
equipment, and to produce finished reports based 
on this data after it has been processed. 
During the year, IBM introduced many new 
information systems and associated machines. 
Two of the systems are unusually powerful ma-
chines of particular value to larger customers. 
The IBM 7094 II, for example, is the most power-
ful computer in the Company's current product 
line. The system has an internal processing speed 
nearly twice as fast as its predecessor, the IBM 
7094. Customers who have installed a 7094 com-
puter, and wish to step up to the greater power of 
the new system, can have their computer con-
verted to a 7094 II in their office or plant. 
The second new product of value to larger cus-
tomers is an electronic storage system capable of 
holding more than one billion characters of infor-
mation. It is composed of new IBM 1302 disk stor-
age files, each of which can hold approximately 
234 million characters of information. Up to five 
units can be combined into a single system. 
The information within the file can be contin-
uously updated so that a company's records can 
be kept as current as the latest transaction. Any 
item of information can be retrieved from the sys-
tem, and entered into an IBM computer for proc-
essing in less than two-tenths of a second. 
IBM has long been interested in serving the 
data processing requirements of smaller custom-
ers, and last year it introduced several systems de-
signed for their problem-solving needs. 
The IBM 1240 banking system introduced last 
year enables small and medium-size banks to 
use the same advanced data processing methods 
which up to now were only feasible for larger in-
stitutions. The 1240 posts checks and deposits to 
customers' accounts by reading and sorting mag-
netically encoded documents at speeds of up to 
1,200 per minute. 
The 1240 can also perform the accounting 
work involved in handling such basic banking 
operations as savings accounts, mortgage loans, 
and the routing of checks back to the banks on 
which they were drawn. 
Two other new systems are outgrowths of 
IBM's widely used 1401 data processing system. 
The 1401-G, which is smaller than the 1401, was 
designed for customers who need to process in-
formation contained in punched cards at great 
speed and in large volume. The 1460, on the 
other hand, was created for customers whose data-
handling requirements exceed the capacity of the 
1401. It can process information nearly twice as 
fast as the 1401, and utilizes a new high-speed 
IBM printer to produce finished reports at the rate 
of 1,100 lines of data per minute, or almost dou-
ble the speed of IBM'S fastest existing printers. 
The Company began deliveries last year of IBM 
1440 systems and 6400 data-handling machines, 
both of primary importance to smaller firms. 
The 1440 was developed for firms whose data 
processing needs exceed basic punched card ac-
counting equipment, but do not yet require the 
capacity of a larger computer such as the 1401. 
The value of the 1440 was increased last year by 
enlarging its data storage and handling capability. 
The IBM 6400 Magnetic Ledger Accounting 
Machine features the first ledger cards on which 
both alphabetic and numeric information can be 
stored magnetically. A company using the 6400 
for customer billing, for example, can store on its 
ledger cards basic information such as a custom-
er's name, address, and credit terms. The data 
can be read automatically by the machine as it cal-
culates the customer's running balance or prints 
his monthly statement.

The 6400 can also be used to handle such jobs

IBM also continued to broaden its family of 
machines which customers use to get information 
in and out of data processing systems. The Com-
pany recently introduced the IBM 7770 audio 
response unit which enables customers to query 
a computer for data by dialing the appropriate 
digits on an office telephone, and receive a pre-
recorded voice answer within seconds. A retailer, 
for instance, could use this unit to check a cus-
tomer's credit rating while the purchase still was 
being made. 
Other new machines introduced during the 
year can read printed or hand-marked informa-
tion automatically, and transmit data from re-
porting terminals to computer centers located a 
distance away. 
Three of these new machines utilize optical 
scanning techniques to read certain kinds of orig-
inal data so it can be more easily fed into a com-
puter for processing. 
The IBM 1282 optical reader card punch can 
read printed numbers and handwritten marks on 
cards, and then punch this data into the cards so 
they can be processed by a computer. The ma-
chine is expected to be useful in speeding the 
processing of credit card purchases. 
The other two scanning machines are IBM op-
tical mark page readers, which can read marks 
penciled in between guide lines on standard let-
ter-size data sheets. One model of the machine 
can read the data and then punch it into cards 
for processing. The other can read the data direct-
ly into a data processing system at a rate of up to 
2,000 data sheets an hour. An insurance com-
pany, for instance, could use this machine to read 
the facts on a client's application directly into a 
computer for processing without first having to 
convert the information into punched card form.

New IBAi Tele-Processmg Systems 
IBM TELE-PROCESSING systems are used to trans-
mit and process data from reporting terminals to 
computer centers located nearby or thousands of 
miles away. Last year several different kinds of 
TELE-PROCESSING devices were introduced. 
The new IBM 1030 data collection system, for 
example, permits factory employees to enter pro-
duction facts into an electronic information net-
work. The data is then transmitted directly into 
the company's computer center which analyzes 
the data, and then converts it into up-to-the-min-
ute management reports on labor and machine 
utilization, tool availability, inventory and order 
status, and other aspects of manufacturing. 
To speed the transmission of day-to-day busi-
ness information between a company's outlying 
locations and its computer center, IBM has intro-
duced the 1050 data communications terminal. 
A supermarket chain could use these terminals, 
for example, to permit individual stores to ask its 
computer center about the availability of supplies 
at the company's various warehouses. 
A still more advanced type of TELE-PROCESS-
ING machine announced last year is the IBM 7740 
communication control unit. This machine can 
receive information transmitted from data ter-
minals in outlying company locations, convert it 
into machine language, feed it into a computer, 
and then transmit the processed data back to the 
sender—all automatically. The 7740 can also act 
as an independent message switching center ca-
pable of routing thousands of messages per day 
between numerous far-flung locations. 
The largest and most advanced commercial 
IBM TELE-PROCESSING systems announced to 
date are the passenger reservations systems being 
installed by three of the nation's major airlines. 
The system for American Airlines is already in 
operation, linking sixteen of the thirty cities 
served by the company. It represents a noteworthy 
effort by specialists from American Airlines and 
IBM who spent ten years developing the system. 
Progress also is continuing toward the installation 
of the Delta Air Lines reservations system, and 
the Pan American World Airways global reserva-
tions network, which will begin linking cities 
served by these companies later this year. 
The transmission of data from remote points 
to a central computer is also an integral part of 
the new IBM 7700 data acquisition system. This 
computer can collect facts from thirty-two differ-
ent points simultaneously, process them, and 
flash back the results in seconds. 
Last year, IBM also expanded the capabilities 
of its 1710 control system which is helping to 
increase the efficiency of production operations 
in the chemical, petroleum, paper, electronics, 
utility, cement, and other industries. 
New Computer Programs 
Information-handling systems can perform ex-
traordinary computational feats with tremendous 
speed, yet before they can begin solving even the 
simplest problem they must be given a precise, 
step-by-step program of instructions telling them 
exactly what to do. 
These programs are as important to the solu-
tion of a problem as the data processing equip-
ment itself. IBM is devoting an increasingly large 
proportion of its resources to the development of 
new programs so it can continue to provide its 
customers with the finest programming support 
available. 
During the year, IBM developed new com-
puter programming systems which will permit 
its machines to process information continuously 
and handle new types of assignments. The Com-
pany introduced a powerful new programming 
system that enables six different IBM computers 
to process a continuous flow of assignments with-
out interruption. The new system enables a com-
puter to select automatically the program needed 
to do a particular job, process the job, and control 
the transition from one job to another. 
Another new programming system introduced 
during the year makes it possible for engineers 
to write problems for solution by a computer in 
engineering terms familiar to them.

Space and Defense 
IBM information systems continued to contribute 
to our country's space and defense programs. 
IBM people and computing systems, for exam-
ple, were part of the National Aeronautics and 
Space Administration's ground-based control net-
work which tracked Mercury astronaut L. Gor-
don Cooper on his 546,000 mile orbital flight 
around the earth. The system helped provide NASA 
scientists with the up-to-the-second data they 
needed to determine whether Major Cooper's 
spacecraft should be permitted to go into orbit, 
where his spacecraft was from one moment to 
the next during its 22 trips around the globe, and 
where the spacecraft would land even if it was 
necessary to fire the retro-rockets outside the 
planned recovery area. During the Mercury series 
of manned space flights, IBM computers per-
formed approximately nine trillion computations. 
IBM is presently developing a still more advanced 
information-handling and control system at NASA's 
new Manned Spacecraft Center in Houston, Texas. 
It will be part of the world-wide tracking and 
control network which will be used for the two-
man Gemini flights around the earth, and later, 
for the three-man Apollo flights which will lead 
to a landing on the moon. 
During 1963, IBM delivered for ground testing 
prototype models of the on-board computer for 
the Gemini spacecraft. This computer will help 
guide the capsule from its point of lift-off into an 
orbital rendezvous with another space vehicle 
and then back to a predetermined landing area. 
NASA has also selected IBM to develop the 
guidance computer and related electronic equip-
ment for the advanced versions of its Saturn 
series of giant rockets, one of which will be used 
to launch an Apollo spacecraft to the moon. This 
system will monitor changes in the rocket's speed 
and position to keep it on course during its flight 
through the earth's atmosphere. Once in orbit, 
the system will shut down the engines automati-
cally and check out the instruments the Saturn 
will use in performing its space maneuvers. 
In the area of defense, IBM continued to de-
liver computers for the all-inertial Achiever guid-
ance system which directs the TITAN II intercon-
tinental ballistic missile to its target, and has 
begun a study of ways to modify this computer 
for still more advanced missions. The Company 
also provided the first computers for flight testing 
in the more powerful TITAN HI space booster. 
Other special IBM systems were installed by 
the Strategic Air Command and the U. S. Navy's 
Fleet Intelligence Center in Europe. The S. A. C. 
system will process data needed to plan and con-
trol its world-wide resources, while the Navy's 
installation will record and analyze reports on 
the movement of ships and aircraft in the Euro-
pean theater of operations. 
The Company also undertook exploratory proj-
ects to develop a laser device that would enable 
astronauts in flight to communicate with ground 
control stations over a beam of light; an on-board

tinued to grow last year as customers in 96 for-

important services.

expanding operations abroad which now include

India, for example, started production of punched

Advances in Research and Development

and development laboratories is aimed at provid-

products. During 1963, progress was made in a 
number of areas including the processing of 
medical information, language translation, crys-
tallography, and the transmission of computer 
information via light beam. 
Several projects were undertaken for utilizing 
IBM data processing techniques in the medical 
field. One project is directed toward speeding the

without merit.
