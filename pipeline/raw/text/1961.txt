To the Stockholders: 
The past year was an eventful one for the IBM 
Company. 
Significant progress was made in the estab-
lished areas of our business, and in those new 
areas that will help to determine IBM'S progress 
in the years ahead. 
The Company's gross income from its domestic 
operations last year amounted to $1,694,295,547, 
an increase of $258,242,462 over i 9 6 0 . 
Earnings were $207,227,597, a $39,046,717 
increase over the prior year. 
Earnings per share were $7.52, based on the 
27,561,531 shares outstanding at the end of the 
year. Earnings in i 9 6 0 were $6.12 a share on the 
27,466,431 shares outstanding on December 31, 
i 9 6 0 adjusted for the 5 0 % stock split effected 
May 5, 1961. 
The IBM World Trade Corporation, a wholly-
owned subsidiary that carries on IBM's business 
through its subsidiaries outside the United States, 
had a gross income of $497,601,962 in 1961, an 
increase of $125,328,886 over i 9 6 0 . Earnings 
were $64,480,536, an increase of $15,671,022 
over i 9 6 0 . IBM earnings include $18,540,853 of 
IBM World Trade's results in 1961, compared 
with $12,300,000 in i960. 
IBM's business comes primarily from the rental 
of its data processing machines. During 
1961, 
however, stockholders were informed of a sub-
stantial rise in the outright sale of this equipment 
to our customers. Outright sales have the effect 
of causing income to be realized immediately, 
which on a rental basis would be realized gradu-
ally over future months and years. These sales, 
therefore, have the effect of increasing current 
earnings and decreasing future earnings. Stock-
holders' attention is again directed to the fact that 
the higher proportion of outright sales rather 
than rentals during the year does not represent 
an accelerated long-term growth rate for the 
Corporation.

The IBM Company is engaged in the creation 
of machines and methods to help find solutions 
to the increasingly complex problems occurring 
in business, government, science, defense, educa-
tion, space exploration and nearly every other 
area of human endeavor. 
Last year, for example, IBM customers were 
using our equipment on such basic applications 
as preparing payrolls, processing bank checks and 
utility bills, and issuing insurance policies. 
At the same time, other IBM products were 
busy handling more unusual assignments, such 
as performing the computing work within sys-
tems that guide intercontinental ballistic missiles, 
controlling the way machine tools cut out intri-
cate metal parts, and assisting in the preparation 
of long-range weather forecasts. 
The most advanced use of IBM equipment is in 
the creation of information systems. These sys-
tems consist of integrated groups of machines 
linked together to solve specific problems. 
The capability of IBM information systems has 
grown to the point where our customers are ap-
plying them to such complex tasks as controlling 
the operation of a paper-making plant, coordi-
nating passenger reservations as they are made in 
hundreds of airline offices throughout the coun-
try, and monitoring the flight of satellites as they 
orbit the earth. 
The development of systems solutions to the 
wide range of existing customer problems, and 
the even more complicated ones that will arise 
in the future, is our greatest challenge. Last year, 
the Company strengthened its capacity to meet 
this challenge by: 
Expanding the number of systems engineers 
who are assisting our customers in defining their 
systems problems and determining the best com-
bination of IBM equipment to solve them. 
Placing greatly increased emphasis on the vital 
work of IBM programmers who prepare the in-
structions that computers need to generate the 
answers to these problems. 
Delivering a rising flow of new IBM products, 
with the most advanced performance character-
istics we have yet produced, to our customers in 
the United States and abroad. 
On May 16, 1961 the Board of Directors elect-
ed Mr. A. L. Williams as President of the Corpo-
ration. All of us who have been with the IBM 
Company over a number of years were delighted 
at this change. From a personal point of view it 
was most gratifying to me, for Mr. Williams has 
worked very closely with me in all phases of IBM 
management since January, 1946, when I re-
turned to the Company after the war. Since we 
have worked as a team for so many years, I am 
confident that the new relationship will bring 
about increased benefits to the Company and all 
of its employees. 
The IBM Company's ability to continually im-
prove its products, and its knowledge of how to 
use them most effectively, is directly attributable 
to the resourcefulness and dedication of our many 
employees. These people are our greatest asset. 
The Company has always endeavored to com-
pensate its employees properly and maintain 
benefit programs to help assure the security of 
both the employees and their families. During 
1961, our expenditures in this area totaled I676,-
821,836 in the United States. 
In the years ahead, IBM employees will be at 
work producing the finest products in our history. 
These products are already taking shape in our 
research and development laboratories, and we 
look forward to the future with confidence.

Electric Typewriter Division: 
The Electric Typewriter Division last year intro-
duced the revolutionary IBM SELECTRIC type-
writer that has met with unprecedented customer 
approval. This new writing instrument repre-
sents the first major technological breakthrough 
in the typewriter industry since IBM introduced 
proportional spacing in 1940. 
The SELECTRIC typewriter replaces the con-
ventional typewriter's metal type bars and mov-
ing paper carriage with a single sphere-shaped 
element covered with 88 raised characters. The 
element types by skimming across the paper in 
the same way your hand does when you write. 
This single element permits a typist to change 
type styles in a few seconds by simply replacing 
one typing sphere with another. The ribbon can 
be changed in an instant by removing a cartridge 
located in front of the typing sphere and snap-
ping a fresh one into place. 
The new machine also has a mechanical stor-
age system that prevents the running together of 
characters that occurs on regular typewriters when 
two keys are struck almost simultaneously. If this 
happens on a SELECTRIC typewriter, the system 
allows the character struck first to print, while 
automatically storing the other for a split-second 
before typing it.

Data Systems Division: 
The Data Systems Division develops and builds 
the large-scale data processing systems needed in 
solving our customers' most demanding prob 
lems. The range and power of these systems were 
considerably increased by new developments an-
nounced last year. 
The IBM 7000 series of large solid-state com-
puters was expanded to provide customers with 
a wider range of systems to meet their individual 
requirements—particularly in the scientific area. 
The speed with which IBM's biggest commer-
cial computers can complete a data processing 
job was dramatically increased by the introduc-
tion of the IBM Hypertape System. 
This magnetic tape system can read problems 
into a computer, or record solutions as they come 
out of a computer, at a rate of up to 340,000 
digits per second. At top speed, the unit could 
enter all 137 million Social Security nine-digit 
numbers into a computer in about one hour. This 
speed was achieved by greatly increasing the den-
sity at which data is recorded on the tape, reduc-
ing the gaps between groupings of data, and by 
increasing the width of the tape itself. 
The Hypertape System has also reduced the 
time it takes to change tape reels. The new unit 
eliminates the need for a computer operator to 
place a fresh reel of tape on a transport, and then 
manually thread it onto a take-up reel. Instead, 
he simply loads a portable tape cartridge into the 
tape system and it does the rest.

Advanced Systems Development Division: 
The search for opportunities to assist customers 
in solving new kinds of problems with advanced 
IBM systems is conducted by the Advanced Sys-
tems Development Division. 
The division explores information-handling 
problems in many fields, and concentrates its 
efforts on those problems that appear to be the 
most technically feasible and that hold the best 
market potential. Once a system has been de-
veloped that shows promise as a new line of busi-
ness, it is turned over to other IBM divisions for 
manufacturing and marketing. Since its forma-
tion in 1959, ASDD has developed IBM TELE-
PROCESSING and process control systems that are 
leading IBM into new commercial markets. 
Last year, ASDD announced several new devel-
opments that also hold promise for the future. 
Progress in information retrieval technology 
was demonstrated, for example, with delivery of 
the first units of a prototype electronic system 
known as "WALNUT" to the Central Intelligence 
Agency. This system will be able to retrieve and 
exhibit any one of millions of pages of randomly-
filed documents within ten seconds. Similar sys-
tems may eventually enable our customers to 
make far more efficient use of fast-expanding hu-
man knowledge. 
ASDD also improved its techniques for recog-
nizing and processing human speech. Late last 
year, the division demonstrated a small experi-
mental device that can instruct an adding machine 
to add and subtract the numbers one to ten spoken 
into a microphone. 
The growing importance of information sys-
tems that interconnect widely-separated customer 
data processing facilities has prompted IBM to in-
crease further its knowledge of data communica-
tions techniques. ASDD is responsible for insuring 
that IBM information systems make the most 
effective use of advances in data communications 
technology. This involves continuing studies of 
the characteristics of data transmission media 
such as telephone and telegraph lines, light beams, 
and microwaves—including their use in commu-
nications systems based on orbiting satellites. 
Among the projects transferred by ASDD to 
other IBM divisions during 1961 were two major 
innovations in advanced computer methodology, 
or "software." One was a new type of program 
for simulating information systems before they 
are built. This program is widely used within 
IBM for designing systems and creating models 
that can simulate various parts of a customer's 
business. With this new type of program, systems 
analysts can perform in a few days a simulation 
that formerly would have required months of 
special programming effort. 
The other innovation was a new type of com-
puter application that enables organizations to 
direct the daily flood of reports, memos, maga-
zines and other materials to the people who need 
them the most. This is done by having the com-
puter match each person's known areas of inter-
est against the contents of incoming documents.

rising demand for IBM products. The Company 
is meeting this demand through the IBM World 
Trade Corporation. 
IBM World Trade's more than 40,000 employ-
ees, 17 manufacturing plants, 6 development en-
gineering laboratories, and 298 sales locations are 
bringing IBM products and service to customers 
in 92 countries outside the United States. 
During 1961, IBM World Trade customers 
were using IBM machines to help solve an ex-
traordinarily wide range of problems. Many na-
tions in all parts of the world, for example, em-
ployed IBM punched card machines to tabulate 
their population censuses. Meteorologists in Japan 
and Belgium used large-scale IBM computers to 
help plot major weather fronts, while a group of 
European physicists used several similar machines 
in probing the mysteries of the atom. 
A West G e r m a n automobile manufacturer used 
an IBM computer in scheduling the daily produc-
tion of 4,000 cars. A n Argentine insurance com-
pany employed another IBM system to keep its 
policyholders' records up to date. And an inter-
national oil company used several computers to 
manage its production, refining and distribution 
operations in Australia and India. 
The new IBM SELECTRIC typewriter created 
as much interest outside the United States as it 
did within this country last year, and IBM World 
Trade began making deliveries of the machine 
from its manufacturing plant in the Netherlands. 
IBM World Trade also initiated deliveries of 
IBM 1401 data processing systems from its plant 
in West Germany, along with deliveries of IBM 
7070 computers from its plant in France. The 
French plant has started production of the new 
IBM 1410 computers and deliveries of these ma-
chines will begin in 1962. 
The consolidation of European manufacturing 
operations was continued by centralizing the pro-
duction of individual products within specific 
plants. This is enabling IBM World Trade to in-
crease the efficiency of its manufacturing opera-
tions, and take further advantage of the internal 
tariff reductions occurring among the nations of 
Europe. 
Production facilities were enlarged in Sweden 
and Japan during the year, and plans were an-
nounced for a new plant in India that will pro-
duce IBM punched card equipment. 
Progress was also made in expanding the ca-
pacity of IBM World Trade's development engi-
neering laboratories. The new facility in West 
Germany was completely occupied last year, and 
personnel began moving into the newly com-
pleted building in the Netherlands. The labora-
tory in the south of France will be ready for occu-
pancy this year, and ground has been broken for 
additional construction in the United Kingdom. 
These new laboratories will further strengthen 
IBM's world-wide product development program.
