To the Stockholders : 
Chairman Thomas J. Watson, Jr. 
The year 1966 marked the achievement of full-scale produc-
tion and installation of the new IBM System/360 data processing 
product family, as well as significant advances in all other areas 
of the business, thanks to outstanding efforts by IBM people 
throughout the world. 
Consolidated gross income from worldwide operations 
amounted to $4,247,706,091, an increase of $674,881,372 over 
1965. Earnings were $526,130,192, a $49,227,702 increase 
over the prior year. 
The rate of increase in gross income is 18.9% compared to an 
increase of 10.3 % in net earnings. The lower increase in earn-
ings resulted from the heavy introductory expenses and higher de-
preciation expense associated with the phasing in of System/360. 
Earnings per share were $9.66 based on the 54,448,200 shares 
outstanding at the end of the year. This compares with earnings 
of $9.03 per share on the 52,837,371 shares outstanding on 
December 31,1965, adjusted for the 50% stock split effected 
May 3, 1966. 
Foreign operations, which are included in consolidated results, 
showed gross income of $1,318,099,225, an increase of $232,-
593,474 over 1965. Earnings from foreign operations were $174,-
605,772 in 1966, an increase of $30,579,442 over the year before. 
Our industry is experiencing an expanding potential for its 
products and services. In many fields of business, government, and 
science, new opportunities are developing for the application of 
advanced information-handling technologies and methods. 
Customer demand for System/3 60 remains strong and resulted 
in a continued high level of orders. A number of new products 
and services were introduced in 1966, some of which are described 
in this report. In our laboratories, important advances were made 
in developing the technologies of the future. Worldwide em-
ployment grew to more than 198,000 during 1966 as over 25,000 
new employees joined the Company. Approximately three mil-
lion square feet of manufacturing and laboratory space were com-
pleted throughout the world. 
Most of the early production problems encountered with 
System/360 have been overcome and production has reached a 
worldwide rate of over 1,000 systems a month. To bring this 
new product family to maximum performance is requiring ex-
tensive programming systems support. A substantial part of 
this work was completed during 1966, but we have experienced 


===== PAGE 7 =====
delays in meeting our original objectives for some of the ad-
vanced programming systems needed to enable the large-scale 
computers and time-sharing systems to achieve their maximum 
capability. While significant progress is being made in this 
area, it has been necessary to withdraw at this time certain of the 
most advanced programming systems support for time-shar-
ing. The highest priority will continue to be placed on program-
ming systems development to improve the usefulness of 
System/360 to our customers. We have also expanded our field 
organization's resources to assist customers in the successful 
installation and utilization of their System/360's. 
The rental and sale of data processing machines and systems 
accounted for approximately three-quarters of the Company's 
business in 1966. Most of IBM's customers choose to rent their 
equipment rather than purchase it outright, which means that 
the capital investment in these rental machines must be financed 
by IBM. Because of the plant construction program and the 
System/360 production build-up, 1966 required a worldwide in-
vestment of approximately $1.6 billion in rental machines 
and parts, factory, laboratory and office equipment, and land and 
buildings. To help finance this expansion in our business, addi-
tional capital stock was offered to stockholders last June. $371 mil-
lion of new capital was raised in this manner. In addition, the 
long-term indebtedness of IBM World Trade Corporation and its 
subsidiaries was increased by $65 million, borrowed abroad, 
to help finance our foreign operations. Continued heavy rental 
machine production during the coming year is anticipated 
both here and abroad, and while most of the necessary funds will 
be provided from operations of the business, substantial addi-
tional funds will be required from outside sources. It is expected 
that borrowings will be utilized to meet these requirements. 
The increased volume of System/360 rental machine instal-
lations, for new and additional business and to replace older 
IBM equipment, will result in a higher level of depreciation expense 
in 1967 as the introduction of this new product line progresses. 
However, this high volume of installations is most encouraging 
for further revenue and profit growth. 
On January 25,1966, the Board of Directors elected three 
principal officers of the Company to new posts. Mr. Albert L. 
Williams, President of the Company since 1961, was elected 
Chairman of the Executive Committee of the Board of Directors. 
Mr. T Vincent Learson, Senior Vice President, was elected Presi-
dent of IBM to succeed Mr. Williams. Mr. Arthur K. Watson, also 
a Senior Vice President, was elected Vice Chairman of the Board 
of Directors. Mr. Williams asked to be relieved of his day-to-day 
responsibilities in keeping with a long-standing personal desire to 
retire at age 55. However, at our request he has agreed to con-
tinue to give a portion of his time to IBM general management 
matters. Everyone who has worked with him knows of the great 
contributions he has made to the success of IBM over the past 
30 years, and I personally am deeply indebted to him for the help 
he has given me. Mr. Learson and Mr. A. K. Watson bring to 
their new posts over 50 years of combined service in all phases of 
IBM's operations. They have held a number of senior manage-
ment positions in IBM and were the Company's two senior vice 
presidents from 1964 until they assumed their additional re-
sponsibilities in 1966. 
The IBM Company's ability to improve its products and to 
make them most useful to customers is attributable to the dedicated 
efforts of our many employees. Nineteen-sixty-six was a year of 
particularly great accomplishment by the people in IBM. Their 
achievements have enhanced the Company's ability to show 
continued progress in our dynamic and challenging industry. 
January 24,1967, 
order of the Board of Directors, 
Chairman of the Board

System/360 Progress Report 
Left: A group of experimental monolithic 
circuit chips as seen through a microscope. Each of 
these chips—i/igth of an inch square—contains 
150 electronic elements making up 16 circuits. 
Incoming orders for System/360 continued 
at a record rate during 1966, and produc-
tion capacity was expanded accordingly. By 
year end the worldwide production of 
System/360 had reached the rate of over 
1,000 systems a month. 
System/360 is an integrated family of 
computers usable for both business and sci-
entific applications. Today, there are eight 
basic models of the system with a wide range 
of peripheral units permitting many com-
binations of processing speed and memory 
capacity. This range of capability and cost 
allows the customer to choose the equipment 
best suited for his particular requirements. 
As his data processing needs grow, the archi-
tecture of System/360 and the program-
ming support available allow him to add 
more powerful units to his present system or 
to move up to more powerful models of 
System/360 without extensive reprogram-
ming. 
To make System/360 even more useful 
to the customer, the Company continued 
to introduce new system capabilities. A vari-
ety of new peripheral units has been 
added to the 44 devices originally announced 
for use with the basic computer models. 
For example, the power of the Model 20, 
the smallest System/360, was expanded dur-
ing the year through the addition of two 
direct access auxiliary memories previously 
available only on larger models. They can 
store up to 10.8 million characters of infor-
mation on magnetic disks and enable the 
Model 20 to perform hundreds of jobs 
previously practical only with larger, more 
powerful computer systems. 
In addition, last year the IBM 1287 Optical 
Reader was announced to give System/360 
a new source of input. This unit can read 
hand-printed numbers and five different 
hand-printed alphabetic characters from a 
number of different types of source docu-
ments and feed the information directly into 
a System/360 for processing. 
System/360 has been applied to a wide 
variety of uses. For example: In the South-
west, a System/360 is helping a natural 
gas company analyze the profit potential of 
proposed major capital expenditures by 
calculating, within minutes, the efl^ect of 
numerous variables. 
A market research organization in Aus-
tralia uses its System/360 to process market 
research reports for pharmaceutical and 
grocery concerns to show the flow of 5,500 
products from the factories through the 
distribution channels to 900 retail stores. 
A major U.S. manufacturer of men's 
clothing has installed a System/360 to ana-
lyze incoming orders from 2,000 dealers 
who can select from a choice of over 250 
clothing styles, each of which can be tailored 
from 1,700 variations in fabric and in-
numerable combinations of threads, linings, 
zippers, buttons and other garment com-
ponents. 
In the Midwest, a large manufacturer of 
animal feed concentrates uses a System/360 
for applications ranging from feed formu-
lization to billing, accounting and salesmen's 
compensation. 
The U. S. Coast and Geodetic Survey is 
using a System/360 at its Rockville, Mary-
land, headquarters, for a number of sci-
entific applications, such as analyzing data 
obtained from satellites, locating earth-
quakes, providing tidal data, and assisting 
in geomagnetic studies. 
Expanded Worldwide 
Manufacturing Capacity 
During 1966, the expansion of manu-
facturing and development facilities for 
System/360 neared completion. 
The plant at Burlington, Vermont, came 
on line to produce Solid Logic Technology 
(SLT) modules, the basic microcircuit units 
of System/360 electronics, adding its ca-
pacity to that of Corbeil-Essonnes, France, 
and the expanded East Fishkill, New 
York, plant, to make IBM the world's largest 
producer and user of these hybrid inte-
grated circuits. Further progress in the auto-
mation of the production lines in these 
plants added materially to the increased 
output of SLT modules. 
New plants at Boulder, Colorado, and 
in the Research Triangle Park near Raleigh 
and Durham, North Carolina, were com-
pleted during the year. The Boulder location, 
where construction was started in mid-
1965, now has over 3,900 employees. In 
North Carolina, where construction be-
gan shortly thereafter, approximately 2,800 
manufacturing, development and adminis-
trative people are employed. Abroad, major 
new plants in Mainz, West Germany, and 
Vimercate, Italy, went into production of 
System/360 last year. As is the case with 
other plants around the world, in order to 
take advantage of mass production tech-
niques, each of these new facilities has been 
assigned prime responsibility for the pro-
duction of certain System/360 units for the 
overseas market. 
A number of existing facilities were ex-
panded during the year to further support 
the System/360 effort. Major building proj-
ects were completed or neared completion

Customer Support 
Basic to the success of any computer in-
stallation is the support given to the cus-
tomer. IBM customer engineers—the people 
in the local branch offices who maintain 
the system—have the responsibility for the 
installation of the equipment. 
These men are equipped with several 
newly developed aids designed to help them 
install and service computers quickly and 
efficiently. For example, a communications 
network speeds new and updated techni-
cal information daily to key field locations. 
The Company also expanded the use of 
computer simulators for training purposes. 
The simulators duplicate significant por-
tions of System/360 operation and provide 
valuable "hands on" experience for the 
customer engineer while reducing the num-
ber of System/360's needed to train them. 
IBM systems engineers in the branch office 
are also key members of this support organ-
ization. These men and women work with 
the salesmen and the customers before, 
during and after installation on such vital 
areas as the analysis of the customer's re-
quirements and the selection of the com-
puter and peripheral units best suited to do 
the job. They also assist in the training 
of customer personnel on equipment and 
programming and the testing of the cus-
tomer's programs prior to the installation. 
After installation, the systems engineers 
continue to assist the customer in making 
necessary modifications to the system as 
requirements change and in applying the 
computer to additional applications. 
To supplement the work of systems engi-
neers in the branch office, the Company 
has expanded its Field Systems Center or-
ganization. These centers are staffed by 
systems engineers who are specialists in such 
areas as programming systems, equipment 
capability or the application of data process-
ing to specific industry requirements. 
Each year, selected systems engineers 
attend the IBM Systems Research Institute 
to study advanced data processing subjects 
on a graduate school level. 
Other Company education facilities 
played a vital role in the support program 
for System/360. IBM salesmen and system 
engineers attended courses, not only on 
new equipment, but also on the specifics of 
the industries—such as banking, transpor-
tation, utilities—to which they have been as-
signed. Courses for customers ranged from 
"Executive Computer Concepts," tailored for 
top management, to basic programming 
and computer operations for their data proc-
essing personnel. These training programs 
were conducted at branch offices and district 
education centers throughout the United 
States and in 66 foreign cities, as well as at 
the Company's residence centers for cus-
tomer executives, at Poughkeepsie and Endi-
cott. New York; San Jose, California; 
Cuernavaca, Mexico; and Blaricum, The 
Netherlands.

System/360 Programming 
Programs are the step-by-step instruc-
tions which tell the computer what to do. 
During the year, IBM programmers, techni-
cians, machine operators and technical 
writers at 14 locations in the United States 
and overseas were engaged in this vital 
support activity. 
They continued developing System/360 
operating and control support programs, 
the basic programming packages which auto-
matically schedule and supervise the flow 
of work through the computer, translate pro-
gramming instructions into computer 
language and perform other computer-man-
agement services. In addition, IBM pro-
grammers continued work on a variety of 
application programs which enable the 
System/360 to process specific jobs. 
Last year, the Company developed and re-
leased to customers more than 125 new 
program packages, made up of nearly 
2,000,000 individual computer instructions. 
A typical customer application program 
released last year is Retail IMPACT, a pro-
gram for department stores and other large 
retail organizations. This program pack-
age enables a System/360 to keep track of 
the movement and sale of a vast number of 
inventory items and thus helps to gear inven-
tories more closely to consumer demands. 
It automatically produces reorder documents 
and sales reports which help buyers and 
executives of the stores make vital merchan-
dising decisions much faster and more 
accurately than before.

Other Products and Services 
A number of other products in the field of 
information-handling were announced or 
first delivered by the Company during 1966: 
Deliveries of the low-cost IBM 1130 com-
puting system began in June. This small 
computer was designed for individual use 
by engineers, scientists and mathematicians. 
Customers for this system range from the 
National Aeronautics and Space Adminis-
tration, which is using it in antennae de-
sign for its Saturn rocket; to engineering 
firms which use it for structural analyses; to a 
small foundry in Georgia, which uses it to 
determine the least-cost mix it can achieve 


===== PAGE 13 =====
for its melting furnaces. Modifications to 
this computer permit it to act as a communi-
cations terminal which can be linked to a 
System/360 to tap the power and memory 
capacity of the larger system. 
Initial deliveries of the IBM 1800 Data 
Acquisition and Control System began dur-
ing 1966. This system is a process control 
computer that operates on a "closed-loop" 
basis. This means that in controlling a 
process—such as a manufacturing line in a 
plant, refinery or mill—the system reads 
measuring and sampling instruments, checks 
the readings for conformance to pre-set 
limits, relays corrections to adjusting devices 
and then rereads the measurements in a 
continuing cycle or "loop." The system is 
being used in a variety of process applica-
tions. In Belgium, a manufacturer of 
glass fiber uses an IBM 1800 system to con-
trol automatically the operation of five 
glass furnaces, and has improved quality and 
efiiciency. Other i8oo's are controlling 
manufacturing processes for paper mills, 
basic metals firms and refineries. In the 
cities of San Jose, California, and Wichita 
Falls, Texas, the 1800 system is used to 
control traffic by sensing the movement of 
vehicles and regulating traffic lights. 
The Company announced, during 1966, 
the new IBM 1080 Data Acquisition System 
designed to help speed the computation and 
reporting of laboratory test results. A 
prototype installed in a hospital in Ohio 
records, on punched cards, readings from 
automated chemistry instruments. The 
cards are then processed by a computer and 
the results made quickly available to the 
examining physician. The system can moni-
tor a number of different tests simultane-
ously, and eliminates much of the time-
consuming clerical work performed by medi-
cal technologists. 
In May, IBM announced a new industry 
information service which provides 
computer-generated market research reports 
based upon electronic banks of data. 
One data bank contains Dun and Bradstreet 
statistics on some 390,000 businesses 
throughout the country. The other data 
bank, now being compiled by IBM, will be a 
detailed model of the national industrial 
economy, tracing the flow of goods and 
services among manufacturers. With the in-
formation stored in these two files, IBM 
computers can provide to customers market 
descriptions and analyses that have been 
largely unavailable until now and at a cost 
within reach of smaller businesses. 
IBM began to market DATATEXT during 
1966. DATATEXT is a text-editing capability 
that allows a number of customers at dif-
ferent locations to use a single computer for 
preparing, storing and revising business 
and technical reports ranging from legal 
documents to sales proposals. DATATEXT is 
similar to QUIKTRAN, introduced by IBM 
in 1965. QUIKTRAN provides remote scien-
tific computing capability to scientists, 
engineers and others with mathematically 
oriented short run jobs to process. In both 
of these systems the customer is provided 
with a typewriter-like terminal unit in his 
office which is linked to a central com-
puter owned and managed by IBM. During 
1966, DATATEXT centers were opened in 
San Francisco, Los Angeles, New York and 
Cleveland. IBM QUIKTRAN centers are 
now operating in New York City, Chicago, 
Los Angeles, Philadelphia and Toronto, 
Canada. 
Also introduced and demonstrated last 
year was the IBM 9370 Document Repro-
ducer. This machine is an offset duplicator 
which uses rolls of paper instead of single 
sheets, and can reproduce on both sides of 
the paper at the rate of 50,000 impressions 
per hour. It operates faster than comparable 
duplicating methods, and can substantially 
reduce paper costs. 
The Company also began deliveries of its 
Document Viewers which are used for the 
display of microfiche. Microfiche is a minia-
ture microfilm sheet, about the size of 
an IBM card, containing up to 90 individual 
page images. Each image can be mag-
nified larger than original document size. 
IBM's customer engineers are being equipped 
with these Document Viewers so that they 
can use microfilmed diagrams and main-
tenance instructions instead of bulky printed 
service manuals. The American Broad-
casting Company used an IBM Document 
Viewer on television to illustrate the 
highlights of the launch, flight, space walk 
and re-entry of the Gemini 9 space flight.

SBC Establishes 
Computer Network 
The Service Bureau Corporation, a wholly 
owned and independently operated sub-
sidiary of IBM, installed more than 30 Sys-
tem/360's during 1966, as it began es-
tablishing its nationwide network of inter-
connecting data processing centers. The 
network will be fully operational in 1969, 
at which time more than 125 IBM Sys-
tem/360's and more than 181,000 miles of 
leased private lines will be utilized. 
System/360's in the network will range 
from the smallest to one of the most power-
ful. This will permit SBC to use the facility 
best suited to the customer's problem. 
During the year, SBC developed and an-
nounced several new computer application 
packages on the basis of which it ren-
ders data processing services for a fee. One 
of these packages, an SBC management 
information system for accounts receivable, 
provides more data for businessmen to 
control accounts receivable, improve collec-
tions, regulate credit and pinpoint trouble 
areas. Another development consolidates sev-
eral general purpose computer programs 
for chemical engineering data processing. 
This system, called CHIPS (Chemical 
Engineering Information Processing Sys-
tem) , is designed to simulate, within a 
computer, the operation of part or all of a 
chemical or petroleum plant to find the 
best operating methods. 
Scientists at Walter Reed Army Institute 
of Research in Washington, are using an 
SBC information retrieval system in search 
of a cure for new forms of malaria, a dis-
ease which is a growing problem among 
armed forces in Vietnam. The SBC system 
permits researchers to probe the effects 
of over 100,000 chemical compounds to find 
those which may be effective against this 
disease.

New Technologies 
During 1966, scientists and engineers in the 
Company's research and development facil-
ities continued to explore many avenues 
of science and technology to extend the use-
fulness of existing products and to develop 
areas for future growth. 
The Solid Logic Technology (SLT) cir-
cuits used in today's System/360 function in 
a few billionths of a second. The SLT cir-
cuits are made by assembling minute elec-
tronic elements, such as silicon chip 
transistors and diodes the size of a pinhead, 
onto conductive patterns printed on a 
ceramic base of thumbnail size. 
Engineers and scientists in IBM have 
developed experimental monolithic circuits 
that will function faster than high-per-
formance circuits currently in production. 
They have also produced monolithic de-
vices containing more than 50 circuits, all 
infused into, rather than assembled on, a 
single tiny chip of silicon. Many of the same 
manufacturing techniques that are used 
to produce the transistors and diodes for SLT 
circuits are also used to make these mono-
lithic circuits—namely photographing a tiny 
screen or mask onto the chip and diffusing 
desired elements into the exposed areas. 
The Company's continuing advances in 
monolithic circuit development hold prom-
ise for even more powerful computers. 
A new mathematical technique, devised 
by Company scientists, provides a scien-
tific approach to the design of optical lenses 
used in special applications such as the 
fabrication of silicon chips into transistors 
and diodes and into monolithic circuits. 
With traditional design methods, it was dif-
ficult to include all the desired properties 
of a lens in the design equation. 
IBM research scientists have reported a 
number of significant developments in their 
continuing investigation of lasers. These 
are devices which emit intense, coherent 
beams of amplified light. For the first 
time, scientists were able to "scan" with a 
laser beam by controlling the direction 
of the beam with a small electrical signal 
before it leaves the laser rather than 
deflecting the beam after it leaves the laser. 
This conserves more of the output power of 
the laser for useful purposes. A system that 
allows laser beams to be separated into their 
color components was also developed. 
The results of these laser studies may lead to 
computer memories in which the data is 
stored in optical, rather than magnetic, form, 
and may be useful in optical displays and 
in the fabrication of microminiature circuits. 
Company research scientists also de-
veloped a computer program which makes it 
possible to complete in minutes certain 
computations which formerly took hours, or 
even days, on the most powerful com-
puters. Based on a mathematical technique 
called the Cooley-Tukey algorithm, the 
program provides a simplified method of 
analyzing complex wave patterns gen-
erated in studies of geophysics, astronomy, 
electroencephalograms, image process-
ing and many other scientific applications. 
This program was included among a 
number of scientific programs announced 
last year for System/360. 
Important progress has been made in ap-
plying the Company's capabilities to the 
field of medicine. Several products are in the 
advanced development stage and are be-
ing tested in cooperation with hospitals and 
medical teaching institutions. 
For example, an experimental Clinical 
Decision Support System is being tested as a 
tool to help physicians diagnose adult ill-
nesses. This system is designed to store clini-
cal knowledge electronically and provide 
the doctor with the facts he needs in the care 
and treatment of a patient. Through a ter-
minal, a doctor can communicate with the 
computer to gain access to pertinent 
medical knowledge. This also helps him to 
keep abreast of the rapidly progressing 
medical field. 
IBM engineers are also working with 
leading medical institutions to develop an 
experimental electrocardiogram (ECG) 
system which allows ECG'S to be recorded 
and then read and analyzed by a computer. 
Eventually, physicians hope to enter all 
ECG'S directly into computers and give car-
diologists more time for direct patient 
care and medical research.

The Evolution of a Product 
At the Brentwood Elementary School in East 
Palo Alto, California, the Company, last 
year, installed an IBM i^oo 
Instructional 
System—the first such instructional system 
to he used as an integral part of a school 
classroom. The purpose of this installation 
is to explore the value of the system for 
use in teaching first graders arithmetic and 
reading. The story of its development is 
told below. 
Every day at 9 a.m., six-year-old Gary 
Pounds and a group of his friends enter a 
neat wooden building on the playground of 
the Brentwood Elementary School in East 
Palo Alto, California, near the Stanford Uni-
versity campus. At a word from the teacher 
Gary slips into one of 16 booths and seats 
himself in front of an unusual console, con-
sisting of a film projector screen, a familiar 
television-like screen which displays his 
name on the bottom, and a typewriter key-
board. Gary places earphones on his head 
and then touches a spot on the TV screen 
with the tip of a special "light sensing" pen 
which electronically communicates with 
a computer. In a flash numbers and simple 
pictures appear on the screen and a teacher's 
voice livens the earphones with instructions. 
Gary has begun his lesson in arithmetic. 
For the next 30 minutes he will be entirely 
on his own in this unique computer ter-
minal booth, absorbed with answering tape 
recorded questions about material dis-
played on the screen, and encouraged by the 
teacher's recorded voice. When he "signs 
off," he will have left recorded on mag-
netic tape a complete and permanent record 
of how well he responded. 
Gary is one of a hundred first graders who 
this year started to take part in a Stanford 
University experiment that some authorities 
believe could contribute to a dramatic 
change in education. A form of computer-
assisted instruction (CAI), the Brentwood 
project is the first full-fledged attempt to 
determine whether elementary school chil-
dren can efficiently learn two of their R's 
(reading and arithmetic) with the help of 
high-speed computers. 
This story of computer-assisted instruc-
tion in IBM begins in 1958. At that time in-
terest was high in programmed instruction. 
Studies had shown early in the 1950's 
that learning of complex tasks can be broken 
down into simple steps. As each step is 
successfully completed, the individual stu-
dent is rewarded and hence reinforced, 
and he is motivated to proceed. 
IBM researchers, looking beyond pro-
grammed instruction, believed an entirely 
new method of individualizing instruction 
could be developed by using a computer. 
The computer clearly has great speed, large 
storage and unique logical capabilities. It 
can modify its operation on the basis of in-
formation fed it, and it can act on ele-
ments of the response, not just on the entire 
response. 
The time was ripe for in-depth explora-
tion of the idea. Educators were becoming 
concerned about problems such as the 
knowledge explosion and teacher shortage, 
and whether new techniques could be de-
veloped to help schools meet these problems. 
In this atmosphere IBM researchers were 
encouraged to develop preliminary com-
puter programs. Such courses as stenotyping 
and statistics were early targets. In York-
town, New York, a group of youngsters re-
ceived computer-assisted instruction in 
arithmetic. Other researchers at the same 
time set up a program with the New York 
Board of Cooperative Educational Services 
of North Westchester, based on the IBM 
7090 data processing system. This experi-
ment was in the use of economic games, 
a problem-solving technique which was then 
coming into prominence. 
While these experiments were going on. 
Company planners were studying the new 
market opportunities presented by computer-
assisted instruction. In 1964, the Company 
set up the Instructional Systems Develop-
ment Department to explore the application 
and development of new technology in ad-
vanced educational systems. The program, 
which had begun in research, grew into a 
collaborative study involving more than a 
dozen groups in universities and other edu-
cational institutions. 
In the meantime, at Stanford University, 
Professors Patrick Suppes and Richard 
Atkinson decided to test computer-assisted 
instruction on elementary school pupils, 
and, as a first step. Professor Suppes called 
for various components from a number 
of companies. IBM's Advanced Systems De-
velopment Division contributed an ex-
perimental instructional display terminal 
equipped with the "light sensing" pen, 
which was installed and tried out with a 
group of children. 
The results were so encouraging that Pro-
fessor Suppes, with a grant from the U. S. 
Office of Education, decided to test a com-
plete system to see how it would work 
with children over an extended period of 
time.

Capital Stock Offering: 
In 1966, 1,324,136 shares of capital stock 
were offered to stockholders at a subscription 
price of $285 for each new share, at the rate 
of one additional share for each forty shares 
held at the close of business on May 27, 
1966. The subscription offer expired on June 
21, 1966, and subscriptions through the ex-
ercise of rights totaled 99.5% of the offering. 
The remaining shares were sold through the 
underwriters, a group of investment firms. 
This equity financing, required for an-
ticipated capital expenditures and as work-
ing capital, resulted in net proceeds to the 
Company of $371,556,341 after payment of 
underwriting commissions and other 
expenses. 
Stock Purchase Plan for IBM Employees: 
At the April annual meeting, stockholders 
adopted the IBM Employees 1966 Stock 
Purchase Plan and authorized up to 1,350,-
000 shares of unissued capital stock to be 
reserved from time to time over a five-year 
period for purposes of the Plan. To date, 
750,000 shares have been reserved by the 
Board of Directors. 
The 1966 Plan became effective on July i, 
1966 following the expiration of the 1961 
Plan. Under the 1966 Plan, employees on the 
annual offering date, each July i, who are 
not participants in a Stock Option Plan, may 
purchase IBM's unissued capital stock one 
share at a time through payroll deductions 
not exceeding 10% of their compensation. 
T h e price an employee pays for a share of 
stock is equal to 85% of the market price on 
the annual offering date or on the date he 
has accumulated enough money to buy the 
share—whichever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 255,104 shares in 
1966 under the two plans, for which 
$72,415,283 was paid to IBM and credited 
to the capital stock account. At December 
31, 1966, 614,869 shares were reserved for 
sale under the 1966 Plan. 
N u m b e r s of shares reflect the 50% stock 
split effected May 3, 1966. 
Stock Option Plans: 
Last April, stockholders approved the 
continuation of a Stock Option Plan for offi-
cers and other key employees and author-
ized up to 300,000 shares of unissued capital 
stock to be reserved from time to time over 
a five-year period for purposes of the 1966 
Stock Option Plan. To date, 150,000 shares 
have been reserved by the Board of Direc-
tors. Both figures reflect the 50% stock split 
effected May 3, 1966. Under the 1966 Plan, 
as under the 1956 and 1961 Plans, options 
may be granted for the purchase of IBM's un-
issued capital stock at 100% of the market 
price on the day the option is granted. 
Options granted after January i, 1964 
run for a five-year term and purchases can 
only be made in installments commencing 
one year after the date of grant and extend-
ing over a period of the next four years. 
Options granted prior to January i, 1964 
under earlier Plans have a term of ten years 
and purchases can only be made in install-
ments commencing two years after the date 
of grant and extending over a period of 
the next eight years. 
In accordance with the Plans, the num-
ber of shares that may be purchased and the 
price per share are adjusted for any stock 
dividends and splits effected after the option 
is granted.
