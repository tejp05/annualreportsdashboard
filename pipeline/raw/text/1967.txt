To the Stockholders: 
IBM Chairman Thomas J. Watson, Jr. at the 
dedication, last April, of the Company's new 
manufacturing and development facility 
near Raleigh, North Carolina. 
During 1967, IBM's continued progress was 
highlighted by the strong demand for its 
products and services throughout the world. 
Consolidated gross income from world-
wide operations amounted to $5,345,290,993, 
an increase of $1,097,584,902 over 1966. 
Earnings were $651,499,558, a $125,369,366 
increase over the prior year. 
Earnings per share were $i 1.61 based on 
the 56,114,279 shares outstanding at the end 
of the year. This compares with earnings of 
$9.43 per share on the 55,809,405 shares out-
standing on December 31, 1966, adjusted 
for the 2^/2% stock dividend paid June 10, 
1967. 
Operations outside the United States, in-
cluded in consolidated results, showed gross 
income of $1,624,990,742, an increase of 
$306,891,517 over 1966. Earnings from these 
operations were $209,360,460, an increase 
of $34,754,688 over the year before. 
The 1967 results reflect the continuing 
high level of business volume of the Com-
pany's System/360 data processing product 
line, as well as a very substantially increased 
level of outright sales of this equipment 
during the last six months of the year. As we 
have previously commented to stockholders, 
outright sales have the effect of realizing 
income currently which, on a rental basis, 
would be realized in future periods. Changes 
in the level of these sales can result in sig-
nificant fluctuations in income from year to 
year and even wider fluctuations from 
quarter to quarter. 
Users of IBM equipment have the choice 
of either renting or purchasing their equip-
ment from IBM or procuring it from third 
parties who are in the business of owning 
equipment and leasing it to users. While the 
great majority of IBM equipment users 
continue to rent directly from IBM, there 
has been an increase in the amount of data 
processing equipment purchased by leas-
ing companies for lease to users. 
Orders for System/360 and other computer 
systems continued at a high level through-
out the year and worldwide production 
exceeded 1,000 systems a month. Of partica-

Orders for, and installations of, IBM data proc-
essing equipment continued at a high rate 
during 1967, particularly in the System/360 
line. System/360 is an integrated family of 
computers designed for both business and 
scientific applications. The standard archi-
tecture of the System allows customers to 
move from smaller models to more power-
ful ones with little or no change in their 
programs or data processing procedures. 
W h e n announced in April, 1964, System/ 
360 consisted of six basic models and 
a range of 44 peripheral devices, such as 
card and tape readers, printers and display 
terminals. 
To provide customers with a broader 
selection of computing capability, the Com-
pany has added to the System/3 60 line 
since its introduction. Today the line has 
grown to nine models of System/360 and 
more than 90 peripheral devices. 
A new model, announced this month, is 
the Model 25 which joins the Model 20 and 
Model 30 to offer users a wider choice of 
smaller System/360's. The Model 25's proc-
essing speed, program compatibility and wide 
range of input/output devices, make it espe-
cially attractive for peripheral operations 
in support of a larger computer. T h e Model 
25 can use programs written for the 
IBM 1400 computer series. The new system 
thus permits normal growth for 1400 sys-
tem customers into the System/360 product 
line and is compatible with all larger 
models of the System/360. 
The growth needs of many small systems 
users were met during the year with the 
extension of the capabilities of IBM's small 
general purpose 1130 computing system. 
A faster magnetic core memory has in-
creased its processing speed, and data stor-
age capacity has been made five times 
larger. The 1130 system also has the ability 
to present data on the television-like 
IBM 2250 Graphic Display Terminal, and to 
be linked with a larger central computer. 
During 1967, IBM delivered the first 
Model 91 computer to the Goddard Space 
Flight Center of the National Aeronautics 
and Space Administration in Greenbelt, 
Maryland. T h e Model 91 is the most power-
ful system IBM has ever built, and is being 
produced in limited numbers to satisfy the 
special needs of a relatively small group 
of highly sophisticated customers. 
The IBM 1800 Data Acquisition and Con-
trol System continues to find wide accept-
ance in the marketplace. T h e 1800 system is a 
process control computer which gathers in-
formation from sensing devices and automat-
ically adjusts the process to produce pre-
programmed results. It is being installed for 
a variety of applications such as controlling 
traffic light systems, pipeline operations, glass 
production and drug research. 
IBM still does a substantial business 
throughout the world in unit record prod-
ucts. These are the familiar punches, sorters, 
collators and accounting machines that 
operate with punched cards. Much of this 
equipment is used by smaller customers 
where the applications do not yet require the 
speed and power of electronic computers. 
Tele-processing networks have become 
a highly important tool for many customers. 
Tele-processing utilizes a central computer 
linked by communications lines to any of a 
variety of input/output devices—includ-
ing other computers. Computers in such a 
network exchange data across a city— 
or a continent—via telephone or telegraph 
lines. Thus a user can respond rapidly to 
an event originating anywhere in his organi-
zation—a salesman's order, a customer's 
inquiry or a financial transaction. 
During 1967, IBM developed an advanced 
communications technique that increases 
the speed with which computers can com-
municate over telephone lines. Called 
Binary Synchronous Communications, the 
technique makes it possible to send f r o m 
25 to 100 per cent more digital information 
over a communications line in a given 
period of time by more effectively regulat-
ing the flow of data characters into the 
transmission line.

The Company continued to devote major efforts 
to programming. Programs are the written 
step-by-step instructions which are fed into a 
computer to tell it what to do with the data 
it receives. 
The effort is broadly classified into three 
categories. One category is comprised of 
control programs which manage the way a 
computer does its work by telling it how 
to schedule its internal functions and when 
to use its various units. T h e second cate-
gory is the development of programming 
languages by which people can write in-
structions for the computer in terms they 
both understand. The third area is made 
up of application programs which set up the 
computer to perform specific jobs such as 
payroll or inventory control. 
An important accomplishment in control 
programming was the release of a new 
version of Operating System/360. This new 
version, called MVT—Multi-Programming 
with a Variable number of Tasks—adds to 
the functions of this master control program 
the capacity to have a computer simulta-
neously perform several completely different 
jobs and automatically set its own schedule 
for maximum efficiency. There have also 
been improvements in several of the com-
ponents of Operating System/360 re-
sulting in increased "through-put" or total 
work done. 
Another control programming develop-
ment, announced this month, allows the 
customer to combine two System/360 Model 
65's into a single large-scale system, increas-
ing the efficiency of both machines. This 
configuration of computers together with the 
program is called a multiprocessor system. 
T h e increased efficiency results from the pro-
gram's ability to balance the total workload 
so the combined system can operate at close 
to its top theoretical capability. 
Time-sharing is another area of control pro-
gramming. A time-sharing computer sys-
tem is one designed and programmed so that 
many users, working at remote terminal 
units, can use the computer at what seems to 
be the same time, so rapidly does the com-
puter switch from one problem to another. 
In this advanced area, the Company con-
tinued its development efforts in various 
aspects of time-sharing. 
In a related area, IBM announced improve-
ments in its marketing program known as 
QUIKTRAN. IBM inaugurated QUIKTRAN in 
1965 to allow scientists, engineers and 
businessmen to use a typewriter-like termi-
nal in their own offices to share a central 
computer at an IBM QUIKTRAN center. 
The new program—called QUIKTRAN 2—is 
up to ten times faster than its predecessor 
and supports more users. It can be bought by 
customers in time segments as small as 
five hours a month. QUIKTRAN 2 computer 
centers are currently located in five cities 
across the nation and access is available 
in 18 other major metropolitan areas. 
QUIKTRAN 2 is bringing the power of a 
large-scale IBM computer to scores of users 
who may not need a full-time computer. 
Last year several new versions of the new 
programming language called PL/I were 
released which increased its power in both 
performance and function, PL/I was de-
veloped to combine in one common language 
a full capability in both scientific and com-
mercial problem solving—a facility not 
readily available with earlier programming 
languages. Work is continuing to further 
improve the performance and function of

an established search pattern for that office, 
determines the nearest IBM parts support 
location that stocks that part and, without 
human intervention, orders it sent to the 
requesting location.

During the year, IBM's research scientists and

The Company's research and systems de-

IBM laboratories also develop and con-

requirements. One of these, the IBM Photo

University of California Lawrence Radia-

mation to process a complete payroll for a 30,-
000-man company. Any item of informa-
tion in the file is quickly retrieved upon re-
quest of a computer and read by a scanner 
for computer processing.

An important step was made by IBM 
research scientists in the area of integrated 
circuits. A computer was applied to de-
signing and producing the extremely com-
plex ""masks" used in the photographic 
fabrication process by which these circuits 
are made. From the designer's instructions, 
the computer determines the patterns re-
quired on each of many masks and then con-
trols a device which ""draws" the desired 
patterns on a photographic plate from 
which the actual masks are made. The tech-
nique makes it possible to fabricate and 
test new chip circuit designs many times 
faster than previously possible. 
Company scientists solved some complex 
technological problems to produce the first 
reported experimental monolithic integrated 
circuits made of the element germanium. 
Until now, such circuits have been made of 
silicon, but since electrons move approxi-
mately three times as fast in germanium as in 
silicon, germanium offers ultimately higher 
speed in electronic devices. 
Advances continue to be made in the area 
of lasers, devices which emit intense beams 
of coherent light. These developments may 
someday be applicable to data storage de-
vices, printing, and optical radars. 
A new method of energizing gas lasers 
makes it possible to achieve a much wider 
bore in the laser tube. This is important 
for such applications as a scanning laser in 
which the light beam is moved in differ-
ent directions. A way of inducing lasing in a 
wide range of dyes was developed, which 
provides an inexpensive laser medium that 
can emit light in any desired color of the 
spectrum. 
In related research, an IBM scientist de-
veloped a method of accurately measuring 
the duration of extremely short pulses of 
laser light or other pulses that last only a 
fraction of a trillionth of a second. These are 
the most minute time measurements ever 
made outside the realm of nuclear physics.

The Saturn V eventually will be used to

of the giant vehicle. T h e instrumentation 
unit, a three-foot-high ring, 68 feet in circum-

manufacturers, is assembled and programmed 
by IBM at Huntsville, Alabama.

has contracts to handle these critica:l assign-
ments for every Saturn mission.

ton, Texas—control center for all manned 
spaceflights. These powerful computers, which 
replace the IBM 7094's used during the Gem-
ini program and earlier Apollo flights, perform 
up to 80 billion calculations a day during an 
Apollo/Saturn mission. They are programmed, 
operated and maintained by IBM people. 
Installation of System/360's also began at

Greenbelt, Maryland. The new computers, 
which replace older IBM equipment, will 
be used to test tracking and communications 
equipment in the Manned Space Flight

Orbiter missions, which photographed the

Stock Purchase Plan for IBM Employees: 
At the April 1966 annual meeting, stock-
holders adopted the IBM Employees 1966 
Stock Purchase Plan and authorized up to 
1,383,750 shares of unissued capital stock 
to be reserved f r o m time to time over a five-
year period for purposes of the Plan. To 
date, 768,750 shares have been reserved by 
the Board of Directors. 
Under this Plan, employees on the annual 
offering date, each July i, who are not par-
ticipants in a Stock Option Plan, may purchase 
IBM's unissued capital stock one share at a 
time through payroll deductions not exceed-
ing 10% of their compensation. T h e price 
an employee pays for a share of stock is equal 
to 85 % of the market price on the annual 
offering date or on the date he has accumu-
lated enough money to buy the share—which-
ever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 268,684 shares in 
1967, for which $88,213,960 was paid to IBM 
and credited to the capital stock account. 
At December 31, 1967, 361,557 shares were 
reserved for sale under the 1966 Plan. 
N u m b e r s of shares reflect the 2 V^ % stock 
dividend paid June 10,1967. 
Stock Option Plans: 
In April 1966, stockholders approved the 
continuation of a Stock Option Plan for offi-
cers and other key employees and author-
ized up to 307,500 shares of unissued capital 
stock to be reserved f r o m time to time over 
a five-year period for purposes of the 1966 
Stock Option Plan. To date, 153,750 shares 
have been reserved by the Board of Direc-
tors. Both figures reflect the 2V2% stock divi-
dend paid June 10,1967. Under the 1966 
Plan, as under the 1956 and 1961 Plans, op-
tions may be granted for the purchase of 
IBM's unissued capital stock at 100% of the 
market price on the day the option is granted. 
Options granted after January i, 1964 
run for a five-year term and purchases can 
only be made in installments commencing 
one year after the date of grant and extend-
ing over a period of the next four years. 
Options granted prior to January i, 1964 
under earlier Plans have a term of ten years 
and purchases can only be made in install-
ments commencing two years after the date 
of grant and extending over a period of 
the next eight years. 
In accordance with the Plans, the num-
ber of shares that may be purchased and the 
price per share are adjusted for any stock 
dividends and splits effected after the option 
is granted.
