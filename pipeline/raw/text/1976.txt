Worldwide 
demand for IBM's 
products and services, coupled with record 
outright purchases of data processing 
equipment during 1976, produced new rec-
ords for gross income and net earnings, 
despite uncertain economic conditions In 
many countries. 
Worldwide net earnings for the year 
ended December 31,1976 amounted to 
$2,398 million or $15.94 per share. This com-
pared with $1,990 million or $13.35 per 
share for the year 1975. Consolidated gross 
income amounted to $16,304 million com-
pared with $14,437 million for last year. 
Net earnings for the quarter ended 
December 31,1976 amounted to $674 mil-
lion or $4.47 per share. This compared 
with $589 million or $3.94 per share for the 
same period last year. Gross income 
amounted to $4,519 million compared with 
$4,068 million for the same 1975 period. 
Operations outside the United States 
for the year 1976, included in the consoli-
dated results, showed net earnings of $1,318 
million, an increase of $212 million over 
1975. Gross income from those operations 
was $8,154 million, an increase of $882 
million over the year before. 
During 1976, purchases of data process-
ing equipment by customers were strong 
around the world, with the fourth quarter 
setting an historic high. This high level of 
purchases throughout the year was the

major contributor to the record earnings. 
The effect of the heavy purchase activity 
in both 1976 and 1975 was reflected in a 
relatively low year-to-year increase of 4.6% 
in consolidated gross income from rentals 
and services in 1976. 
Incoming orders were significantly 
higher than in 1975. This resulted in a year-
end backlog which was well above that 
of a year earlier, despite the high volume of 
shipments during 1976. 
The favorable results of our operations in 
1976 are directly attributable to the skill, 
dedication, and creativity of IBM people in 
all parts of the company. They have our 
deepest gratitude for their special efforts 
during the year. 
On January 31,1977, Gilbert E. Jones will 
retire as IBM Vice Chairman of the Board 
after 38 years of service. IBM is indebted to 
Gil Jones for his many contributions to 
IBM's success worldwide. He headed our 
data processing marketing operations 
in the United States before becoming presi-
dent of IBM World Trade in 1963. In the 
years since, he has played a vital role in 
building sound international relation-
ships for the company and in helping guide 
the expansion of IBM's business in many 
countries throughout the world. We are 
pleased that we will continue to have the 
benefit of his experience and counsel as 
a member of the IBM Advisory Board. 
Greater Customer Productivity 
To assist customers in accomplishing 
more productive work, we continued to 
enlarge the IBM product line in 1976. For ex-
perienced data processing users, capable 
of their own programming, we introduced a 
new, low-cost computer, the Series/1. 
It offers a variety of attachments for different 
kinds of work, and it can also provide a 
data communications capability. The small 
Series/1 has a computing power match-
ing that of large-scale computers of the early 
1960s. 
For customers of our medium-sized sys-
tems, we introduced two new System/370 
models, the 138 and 148, with improved per-
formance in their size ranges. Also, the 
speed of our existing large-scale System/370 
models was increased through the attach-
ment of additional processor units. 
Advances in technology and new prod-
ucts are enabling us to provide customers 
with broader data processing capabilities 
at lower costs. Whether the customers' 
needs involve small or large computers or 
word processing for the office, IBM's prod-
uct line offers an ever-widening range of 
speeds, capacities, functional attachments, 
and price categories. Such offerings en-
able customers to choose the combinations 
best suited to their requirements. 
Some customers are finding it advanta-
geous to decentralize their data processing 
through use of so-called "intelligent" 
terminals-machines designed for use in 
specific industries, including retail stores, 
banks, and supermarkets. The IBM prod-
uct line includes such specially designed 
terminals. 
Another trend is toward the use of large 
data bases and data communications net-
works. With the systems stability provided 
by System/370, the customers' informa-
tion files are becoming well established, 
making possible expansion into many new 
applications. 
The Lawsuits 
Currently being tried in court are the anti-
trust suits against IBM by the U.S. Justice 
Department and California Computer Prod-
ucts, Inc. Thus far in the Justice Depart-
ment's trial, which began in May, 1975, only 
the government's witnesses have been 
heard. This trial is likely to continue for an 
extended period. 
The action brought by California Com-
puter Products probably will be submitted 
to the jury for a verdict by late spring. 
Other antitrust suits against the company 
remain in pre-trial stages. 
We continue to believe we have com-
peted fairly and ethically and that our 
legal position is sound in each case. Further 
detail on the lawsuits is contained in the 
financial section of this report. 
Outlool< for 1977 
We expect that the favorable business 
conditions which we experienced in the 
past year will continue in 1977. Our industry 
is still very young and dynamic. Devel-
opments in technology and in the way cus-
tomers are using IBM products support 
our belief that both data processing and 
word processing will continue to expand 
for a long time to come. It appears likely 
that our overseas business will continue to 
be a strong growth area. More of the 
world is developing and industrializing, 
increasing the need for the kinds of 
products that IBM provides. 
The responsiveness and loyalty of IBM 
men and women in 128 countries around 
the world are the best assurance that we will 
be able to take full advantage of our op-
portunities in the years ahead.

New Small Computer 
In 1976 IBM introduced a new small 
computer, Series/1, designed for use by 
experienced data processing users. 
Other small IBM computers, such as 
System/32 and System/3, have been de-
livered primarily to customers who require 
the programming support that IBM sup-
plies with those systems. 
The new Series/1, however, is intended 
primarily for customers with programming 
capabilities and a need for multiple small 
systems. 
Series/1 is a general purpose computing 
system that offers both communications 
and sensor-based capabilities. It enables 
users to attach a large number and variety 
of input and output units, including cus-
tom-built devices for special applications. 
Word Processing Versatility 
Several new IBM products announced 
during the year were designed for offices-
particularly offices in which high volumes 
of paperwork are processed. One of the 
most important, the IBM 46/40 document 
printer, operates as a high-speed ink jet 
printing unit for all models of IBM mag 
card typewriters. It accepts input from 
magnetic cards and prints results at rates 
up to 92 characters per second. In addi-
tion to speed, the 46/40 offers high-quality 
print, variable type styles, and automatic 
feeding of paper and envelopes. 
IBM's 
new low-cost

fixed

IBM's two models of the new Series III 
copier/duplicator produce high-quality 
copies of printed matter, photographs, and 
bound volumes at a speed of 75 sheets 
per minute. Copies can be made on a variety 
of paper stocks, weights and sizes, even 
on materials such as transparencies or 
gummed stock. One of the two models fea-
tures copy reduction. 
Faster Intermediate Systems 
IBM's line of medium-sized computers 
was extended significantly with the an-
nouncement of two new System/370 com-
puters, Models 138 and 148. Both offer 
substantially improved internal perform-
ance over previous computers in their size 
ranges, at improved price-performance 
ratios. Customers in search of more com-
puter power can easily replace less 
powerful models, in many cases with little 
or no reprogramming. 
Also introduced in 1976 were new at-
tached processor systems that can increase 
by more than 50% the internal perform-
ance of IBM's large-scale System/370 Mod-
els 158 and 168. 
Data Network Improvements 
In 1976 IBM announced important 
extensions to its Systems Network Archi-
tecture, which enables multiple comput-
ers to be connected so that any of their 
terminals can use application programs in 
any of the computers. 
IBM also announced new models of 
its supermarket, general purpose, and bank-
ing terminal systems. Included was a 
hand-held keyboard for entering personal 
identification numbers, a device giving 
both consumers and financial institutions 
greater security against unauthorized use 
of credit cards, checks and passbooks. 
Programming for Office Systems 
For offices that require advanced word 
processing-the capacity not only to 
generate quantities of documents, but also 
to handle extensive revision and refor-
matting-IBM announced a new program 
product called IBM Word Processor/32. 
This program operates with IBM's low-cost 
System/32, adding the power of the com-
putertothe productivity of word processing.

Evolving 
new 
technologies 
to meet tomorrow's 
customer 
needs 
In few technologies has performance 
increased or cost decreased as fast as in 
computers. Yet computer users con-
tinue to find applications that tax the 
capacity of existing machines. 
So IBM scientists, engineers and pro-
grammers continue to search for ways 
to speed the flow of information inside com-
puters and to simplify the task of a 
human being who needs to find and use 
that information. 
In basic materials research, IBM scien-
tists have developed new laser tech-
niques to study the interaction of electrons 
in the outer shells of atoms, and to study 
how molecules exchange energy. Novel 
techniques have been developed to 
study in great detail what happens to atoms 
on the surfaces of materials. 
IBM people have also developed ways 
to fabricate electrical conductors only a 
few tens of atoms wide. Such ultra-fine lines 
have several important potential scientific 
applications. 
In an effort to boost computer calculat-
ing speed beyond what can be attained 
with transistors, IBM people continue to 
study Josephson junctions-using them 
in experimental computer circuits such as 
multipliers and memory arrays. IBM com-
puter scientists are also investigating basic 
system architecture and organization and 
new or expanded uses for computers. 
IBM system designers are looking for 
simpler ways to organize information to 
make it easily accessible to those who need 
it, but secure and protected from unau-
thorized use. At the same time, IBM engi-
neers and programmers are developing 
new devices and systems to meet the spe-
cial needs of industry, government agen-
cies and other customers.

Gross income from worldwide IBM 
operations amounted to $16,304 million in 
1976, an increase of $1,867 million over 
the $14,437 million for 1975. 
During 1976, sales, rentals and services 
of data processing machines and systems 
accounted for approximately 78% of gross 
income, other regular products and ser-
vices accounted for 19%, while special prod-
ucts and services for United States space, 
defense and other agencies contributed 3%. 
Earnings 
Worldwide earnings before taxes totaled 
$4,519 million, which was $798 million 
more than the $3,721 million earned in 1975. 
U.S. Federal and non-U.S. income taxes 
were $2,121 million in 1976, an increase of

or $408 million more than the $1,990 million 
earned in 1975. 
Earnings in 1976 equaled $15.94 per 
share, compared with $13.35 per share 
for 1975.

Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles of Consolidation: The con-
solidated financial statements include the 
accounts of International Business Machines 
Corporation and its U.S. and non-U.S. sub-
sidiary companies. 
Translation of Non-U.S. Currency 
Amounts: Non-U.S. assets and liabilities are 
translated to U.S. dollars at year-end ex-
change rates, except that inventories and 
plant, rental machines and other property 
are translated at approximate rates prevail-
ing when acquired. Income and expense 
items are translated at average rates of ex-
change prevailing during the year, except 
that inventories charged to cost of sales and 
depreciation are translated at historical 
rates. Exchange gains and losses are in-
cluded in earnings currently. 
Gross Income: Gross income is recog-
nized from sales when the product is shipped 
or in certain cases upon customer accep-
tance, from rentals in the month in which they 
accrue, and from services over the contrac-
tual period or as the services are performed. 
Depreciation: With minor exceptions, 
depreciation of U.S. properties is computed 
using the sum of the years-digits method. 
Depreciation of non-U.S. properties is com-
puted using either accelerated methods 
or the straight-line method. 
Retirement Plans: Current service costs 
are accrued currently. Prior service costs 
resulting from improvements in the plans are 
amortized generally over 10 years, except 
that certain non-U.S. subsidiaries use longer 
periods not exceeding 25 years. 
Expenses: Marketing expenses and 
development and engineering expenses are 
charged against income as they are incurred. 
Income Taxes: Income tax expense is 
based on reported earnings before income 
taxes and thus includes the effects of 
timing differences between reported and 
taxable earnings which arise because 
certain transactions are included in taxable 
earnings in other years. Investment tax 
credits are deferred and amortized as a re-
duction of income tax expense over the 
average useful life of the applicable classes 
of property.

Litigation 
In January, 1969, the Department of 
Justice filed a civil antitrust complaint 
against IBM under Section 2 of the Sherman 
Antitrust Act, charging the company with 
monopolizing commerce in general purpose 
digital computers in the United States. 
Trial of the case began in May, 1975 and is 
currently in progress. 
The government continues to seek di-
vestiture relief, requesting that IBM be 
reorganized into several independent and 
competing organizations and that IBM 
be enjoined from continuing its alleged mo-
nopolistic practices. 
In April, 1970, Xerox Corporation filed a 
lawsuit against IBM charging patent in-
fringement and misuse of confidential infor-
mation in connection with the IBM Copier I. 
In August, 1973, Xerox filed a supplemental 
lawsuit concerning the IBM Copier II. In 
November, 1975, IBM filed an action against 
Xerox for infringement of an IBM patent 
relating to office copiers. In that action, 
Xerox has filed a counterclaim for breach of 
contract and for patent infringement con-
cerning the IBM Copier II, but involving a 
different patent from the 1973 action. 
These lawsuits, which have all been consol-
idated, are in the pre-trial stage. In its 
lawsuits and its counterclaim, Xerox seeks 
injunctive relief and damages in an un-
specified amount. Patent infringement ac-
tions between Xerox and IBM are also 
pending in Canada. 
Seven suits instituted against IBM 
since 1973, alleging Federal antitrust law vio-
lations, remain in progress. The plaintiffs, 
California Computer Products, Inc., Trans-
america Computer Company, Hudson

General Corporation, Memorex Corporation, 
Forro Precision, Inc., DPF Incorporated 
and Sanders Associates, Inc., seek damages 
which, after trebling, aggregate $4,458 
million (of which $3,150 million is sought by 
Memorex Corporation and its subsidiaries 
alone) and, in some cases, injunctive relief. 
Five of these suits are presently consoli-

the Northern District of California for the

In July, 1972, the trial of the Greyhound 
Computer Corporation lawsuit, filed in Oc-

Greyhound's appeal to the United States 
Circuit Court of Appeals for the Ninth 
Circuit was argued in April, 1974, and IBM is 
currently awaiting the decision. 
IBM has denied the charges in all of

Stock Purchase Plans 
In April, 1976, stockholders approved a 
new five-year Employees Stock Purchase 
Plan, effective July 1,1976, which authorizes 
up to 8,500,000 shares of capital stock for 
the Plan. Under this Plan, employees who 
are not participants in a Stock Option 
Plan may purchase IBM's capital stock 
through payroll deductions not exceeding 
10% of their compensation. The price an 
employee pays for a share of stock is 85% 
of the average market price on the date 
the employee has accumulated enough 
money to buy the share. At December 31, 
1976, 2,000,000 authorized and unissued 
shares were reserved for issuance under the 
Plan. The total number of authorized 
shares was not reserved because the com-
pany intends to purchase the shares in 
the market to the extent feasible. During 
1976, the company purchased 567,100 
shares in the market for $155 million and 
sold 494,721 of these treasury stock shares 
to employees. The difference between the 
$135 million cost to IBM forthese shares and 
the $115 million proceeds from employees 
was charged to retained earnings. 
Under the 1971 Plan, employees pur-
chased 894,269 shares in 1976 for which 
$159 million was paid to IBM and credited 
to the capital stock account. Less than 1,000 
shares, from those previously reserved, 
will be issued from December 31,1976 
through June 30,1977 to complete require-
ments of this Plan. 
Stock Option Plans 
In April, 1975, stockholders approved 
the continuation for a five-year period of a 
Stock Option Plan for officers and other 
key employees. For this purpose, 1,800,000 
shares of authorized capital stock were 
reserved. Under this Plan, as under the 1971 
Plan, options may be granted for the pur-
chase of IBM's capital stock at not less than 
100% of the market price on the day the 
option is granted. 
Two types of options are granted: quali-
fied and nonqualified. Qualified options 
have a maximum duration of five years. 
Nonqualified options have a maximum du-
ration of 10 years. With one exception, 
both types may be purchased in four annual 
installments, commencing one year from 
the date of grant. The exception covers non-
qualified options which were granted 
simultaneously with qualified options dur-
ing 1971 and 1972. Shares relating to 
these nonqualified options cannot be pur-
chased until five years and one day after 
the date of grant. The number of shares pur-
chasable is reduced by the number of 
shares purchased under the related quali-
fied option. 
In accordance with the Plans, install-
ments are cumulative, and the number 
of shares that may be purchased and the 
price per share are adjusted for stock 
dividends and splits effected afterthe option 
is granted.

Gross income from sales, rentals and 
services increased 12.9%, compared to the 
1975 increase of 13.9%, reflecting con-
tinued growth in all major product areas. In 
1976, the rates of growth in U.S. opera-
tions and non-U.S. operations were approxi-
mately the same. 
Gross income from sales increased 
31.1%, compared to a 6.2% increase in 1975. 
The 1976 increase resulted principally 
from record high worldwide levels of pur-
chases of data processing equipment. The 
larger systems accounted for the major-
ity of these purchases. Sales of all other 
products also increased, but at somewhat 
more moderate rates. 
Gross income from rentals and services 
increased 4.6% in 1976 and 17.8% in 1975. 
The relatively low rate of growth in 1976 
reflects the impact of the heavy purchase ac-
tivity of 1976 and 1975 in both U.S. and 
non-U.S. operations. 
Cost of sales increased by 20.2% in 
1976, which was a lower rate of increase 
than that of related gross income. This 
was due principally to a change in mix of 
products which have different levels of 
cost. The most significant change in this mix 
was the increase in data processing equip-
ment. The 1975 increase in cost of sales 
was 14.3%. 
Cost of rentals and services increased by 
4.0%, which was in line with the rate of 
increase in related gross income. The 1975 
increase was 11.8%. 
Selling, development and engineering, 
and general and administrative expenses 
increased 13.1%. This increase was in line 
with the growth in the business and reflects 
increased costs of employee compensa-
tion and benefits. The increase was 19.0% 
in 1975. 
Interest on debt in 1976 decreased by 
28.2%, reflecting the effects of reduction of 
debt. This compares to a 9.4% decrease 
in 1975. 
Other income, principally interest 
from marketable securities, increased 
37.2%. This increase is attributable to a 
larger portfolio. In 1975, other income 
increased 5.8%.

Satellite System Approved 
The applications of Satellite Business 
Systems, a partnership formed by wholly 
owned subsidiaries of The Aetna Casualty & 
Surety Company, COMSAT General Cor-
poration, and IBM, for authority to construct 
and operate a domestic satellite communi-
cations system were approved by the Federal 
Communications Commission in January, 
1977. The SBS objective is to apply the inher-
ent advantages of satellite communications 
in offering a unique, all-digital service in 
the form of private networks. Such networks 
provide an integrated communications 
capability to geographically dispersed orga-
nizations with complex and voluminous 
communications requirements. Plans call 
for the system to be operational in 1980. 
Support of OECD Declaration 
In June, 1976, the member countries of 
the Organization for Economic Cooperation 
and Development issued a declaration 
aimed at improving the international invest-
ment climate, strengthening confidence 
between multinational enterprises and 
countries, and encouraging the positive 
contributions of these enterprises. 
IBM is in full support of the aims of 
the OECD declaration and will take the busi-
ness guidelines, which are a part of it, 
into full consideration in the formulation of 
company policies. IBM has, in fact, long 
abided by the spiritembodied intheseguide-
lines, and many are specifically represented 
in IBM policies. 
The voluntary nature of the guidelines 
is commendable, since it allows for the 
great diversity among enterprises and gives 
them the opportunity to show that they 
can and will apply high standards to busi-
ness practice.

Equal Opportunity Activity 
During 1976, IBM increased the number 
of its minority employees in the U.S. from 
10.9% to 11.3% of the total employee popu-
lation, while the number of women em-
ployees rose from 16.9% to 17.6%. The U.S. 
company now has 1,437 minority man-
agers, 14.5% more than in 1975, and 1,273 
women managers, an increase of 21.2%. 
The effect of IBM's Equal Opportunity Pro-
gram has continued to be felt in the hiring 
of new employees. Of the total hired in 
1976 in the U.S., 24.4% were minorities and 
44.3% were women.
