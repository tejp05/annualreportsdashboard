1972 Financial Results. IBM worldwide gross 
income and earnings grew to record levels 
in 1972. As the economy in the United States 
continued its recovery, IBM domestic gross 
income and earnings showed the first significant 
increase over the prior year since 1968. Gross 
income and earnings from operations outside 
the United States continued to increase at a 
very satisfactory rate. 
Consolidated gross income from world-
wide operations for 1972 amounted to 
$9,532,592,642, an increase of $1,258,989,273 
over 1971. Net earnings were $1,279,267,555, an 
increase of $200,420,648 over the previous year. 
Earnings amounted to $i 1.03 per share on 
115,962,446 shares, the average number of 
shares outstanding during the year. This com-
pares with earnings of $9.38 per share on 
115,049,434 shares, the average number out-
standing in 1971.

New Products and Technology. The past year 
was an active and significant one for new IBM

Litigation. The increased pace of hearings in the 
Department of Justice antitrust suit against 
IBM, initiated in January 1969, attracted wide 
press attention during 1972. In response to the 
court's request to be specific, the Department 
said again that if it could prove its original 
charges against IBM, it would ask the court for 
divestiture. IBM has denied the charges and is 
engaged in vigorously defending against them. 
Many issues still need to be resolved in the case, 
and it may be some time before it is concluded. 
Earlier this month IBM announced the 
out-of-court settlement of the litigation between 
Control Data Corporation and IBM on a basis 
which the Company considers favorable to both 
parties. I am glad that this complex legal matter, 
which has constituted a substantial drain on 
management time and energy as well as on our 
legal staff for more than four years, is finally 
behind us. The terms of the settlement included, 
among other things, the sale of The Service 
Bureau Corporation to Control Data Corporation. 
Additional details on the settlement and other 
litigation during 1972 can be found on page 26. 
Challenge of Worldwide Competition. Both here 
and overseas, the data processing industry is 
becoming increasingly competitive. Domesti-
cally, many new companies enter the industry 
each year, offering new data processing products 
and services. Overseas, we are increasingly 
challenged by national data processing compa-
nies supported by their governments. 
Within this competitive worldwide envi-
ronment, I'm confident that IBM has the talent, 
resources and commitment to maintain its 
leadership. But there is reason for concern about 
the potential effects of economic nationalism 
in this country and abroad. IBM believes the best 
way to compete in the international market-
place is to provide superior products and serv-
ices consistent with the needs and priorities of 
the countries in which we operate. 
However, there is some sentiment in this 
country to resort to protectionism as a reaction 
to foreign competition. This point of view is 
reflected in such proposals as the Hartke-Burke 
bill, which has been under consideration in 
Congress. I believe stockholders, the business 
community and the general public need to 
be aware of the implications. 
It has been documented that multinational 
companies like IBM contribute substantially 
to the U. S. balance of payments and support 
thousands of jobs in the United States. In IBM, 
we estimate that one out of every eight jobs 
in our American plants is supported by our ex-
port trade. 
Any action by the United States that would 
set up tax barriers, quotas, or other trade or 
investment restrictions inevitably would cause 
retaliation by foreign governments against 
American products and companies. American 
jobs supported by overseas business would 
diminish and our balance of payments problems 
would worsen. The result, we believe, would 
be to undercut the basic economy of this country 
and seriously damage international relation-
ships. This is why we oppose such measures and 
are urging the business community to speak 
out on this issue.

Again in 1972, IBM announced an array of 
products to help customers utilize information 
more efficiently and to further simplify their 
growth into new and more profitable uses of 
computers. 
Especially noteworthy are three advanced 
System/370 computers: Models 125, 158 and 
168. Featuring improved methods of handling 
data in the central processing unit and a new 
storage technology, these computers perform at 
new levels of data processing efficiency. 
The advanced data handling technique, 
called virtual storage, allows the customer to use 
his system as though it has up to 16 million 
characters of main storage, even though main 
storage really has only a fraction of that. At any 
given time, only the small portions of programs 
or data which are needed immediately for a 
computer operation are kept m main storage. 
When one of these portions, called pages, is no 
longer needed, it is moved back into a less 
expensive disk storage location, and its place in 
main storage is taken by a needed page. The 
central processing unit automatically super-
vises this flow of information and keeps track of 
the location of every page, so the programmer 
no longer must concern himself with the loca-
tion and movement of information inside the 
system. This saves programming time and 
speeds the development and installation of new 
applications as well as modification or expan-
sion of existing applications. 
To improve the performance and reduce the 
physical size of main storage in these new 
computers, IBM introduced new memory cir-
cuits that use MOSFET (Metal Oxide Semi-
conductor Field Effect Transistor) technology. 
With this technology, a silicon chip about an 
eighth-of-an-inch square can store up to 1,024 
binary digits. 
These dramatic new developments are a 
tribute to the creative eff^orts of research, manu-
facturing, system design and programming 
specialists from both the domestic Company 
and the IBM World Trade Corporation.

From People to Computer... 
This new lower cost IBM optical character 
reader provides many of the benefits of optical 
scanners to more computer users. It can 
read hand-printed numbers and a variety of 
type styles and formats, and send data directly 
to a computer or magnetic tape. Internal data 
storage and processing enable it to handle docu-
ments customized to a user's several different

New Faster IBM Copier II... 
This new IBM Copier features an advanced 
document feed that takes hold of an original and 
automatically positions it for copying. It de-
livers the first copy in just six seconds and addi-

Three copies each of three single-page letters, 
for example, can be made in less than 30 sec-
onds. The Copier II handles both letter and legal 
size documents, photographs, drawings, pages 
from thick books, and even rolled documents.

Help for Hearts... 
Among the number of new computer applica-
tion programs announced during the year 
was one developed by the Advanced Systems 
Development Division to help cardiologists 
interpret large numbers of electrocardiograms 
more quickly and easily. Electrical signals 
from a patient's heart are recorded on magnetic 
tape, then analyzed in a computer in less than 
a minute. Cardiologists compare this with their 
own analysis of an electrocardiogram, then 
guide attending physicians in patient care. 
More Textile Mill Productivity... 
The new Textile Industry Monitoring System 
helps minimize costly machine stoppages in 
knitting and weaving mills. The system, which 
includes an IBM System/y computer, senses 
and records the activity of hundreds of machines 
every 60 seconds. By using a computer terminal, 
a mill supervisor can spot-check production on 
any of his machines as a supplement to regular 
reports generated by the monitoring system. 
Computer Productivity Up... 
A new communications controller can handle 
many data-handling tasks that formerly re-
quired valuable time in the central processing 
unit of a computer. The new controller can 
coordinate data transmission and assemble mes-
sages from terminals connected to as many 
as 350 telephone lines. It enables the central 
computer to be used on more productive jobs. 
More Time on the Road... 
Another new application program, called 
Fast Information Retrieval for Surface Trans-
portation, gives motor freight companies a fast, 
economical way to route tractor-trailers and 
keep them on the road carrying profitable loads. 
From a central data base that contains up-to-
the-minute information on equipment status 
throughout the country, management receives 
reports to control operations, and freight 
terminals receive advance information on ship-
ments headed their way. 
Mobile Police Terminal... 
Normally, when a police officer needs informa-
tion from a computer, he gets it by radio from 
a police dispatcher. Now, an officer in a patrol 
car can communicate directly with the com-
puter, using a new IBM mobile terminal that 
can be installed right in his car. As a result, 
he can obtain information on stolen vehicles 
and wanted persons more quickly. 
Input Word Processing... 
A new line of improved IBM dictation equip-
ment is now called "input word processing 
equipment," which better describes the equip-
ment's function. One of the new systems, 
announced last fall, permits any push-button 
telephone to be used as a remote dictation sta-
tion. Another turns in-house dial telephones 
into dictating instruments. A third lets an exec-
utive use a microphone to dictate directly to 
a recorder-transcriber at a secretary's desk. 
Up to eight microphones can be connected to 
each recorder-transcriber. A new desk-top 
input unit for private use and a new secretary's 
transcriber complete the new line. 
From Pencil to Computer... 
A photoelectric device to detect pencil marks 
on paper documents, such as multiple choice 
tests, has been used for some time as a conven-
ient way to enter data into a computer. A new 
IBM mark reader now should expand this 
market. The new 3881 can be adjusted to read 
many different sizes of documents in a variety 
of applications. 
Mag Card Typing Improved... 
IBM's new Mag Card "EXECUTIVE" Typewriter 
adds proportional spacing to the speed and 
efficiency of automated typing. It automatically 
proportions the spacing between individual 
letters, allowing less space for narrow letters than 
for wide ones. Tests show that this increases 
readability and improves appearance of finished 
documents. 
Building Protection... 
A new computer-based controlled-access system 
can give greater protection to the occupants, 
property and documents in a building. This 
system connects card readers located at entrance 
doors to a System/7 computer which verifies 
magnetically encoded data on ID cards inserted 
in the readers. The computer unlocks the door 
if the card is valid, then checks the closing of the 
door. Up to 64 card readers can be handled 
by a System/7. 
World's Fastest Switch? 
Right: This experimental electronic compo-
nent may lead to the computer technology that 
will be needed tomorrow. It is a Josephson 
junction, which can be as small as one twenty-
five-thousand th-of-an-inch across—so small 
that a powerful microscope is needed to see it. 
And so fast that it can switch an electric voltage 
on or off in less than 10 trillionths of a sec-
ond. It is being studied by IBM research scientists 
in Switzerland and the United States.

Significant Accounting Policies 
Principles of Consolidation: The consolidated 
financial statements include the accounts of 
International Business Machines Corporation 
and its domestic and foreign subsidiary 
companies. 
Translation of Foreign Currency 
Amounts: 
Foreign assets and liabilities are translated to 
U.S. dollars at year-end exchange rates, except 
that plant, rental machines and other property 
and long-term debt are translated at approxi-
mate rates prevailing when acquired or incurred. 
Income and expense items are translated at 
average rates of exchange prevailing during 
the year, except depreciation which is calculated 
at the approximate rates prevailing when the 
properties were acquired. Gains or losses which 
result are included in earnings currently. 
Gross Income: Gross income is recognized 
from sales when the product is shipped or 
in certain cases upon customer acceptance, from 
services over the contractual period or as the 
services are performed, and from rentals in the 
month in which they accrue. 
Depreciation: With minor exceptions, depre-
ciation of domestic properties is computed 
using the sum of the years-digits method. De-
preciation of foreign properties is generally 
computed using the straight-line method. How-
ever, methods which provide higher charges 
in early years of asset life, such as the declining-
balance method, are used by certain foreign 
subsidiaries. 
Retirement Plans: Current service costs are 
accrued currently. Prior service costs resulting 
from improvements in the plans are amortized 
generally over l o years, except that certain for-
eign subsidiaries use longer periods not ex-
ceeding 25 years. 
Expenses: Marketing expenses and develop-
ment and engineering expenses are charged 
against income as they are incurred. 
Income Taxes: Income tax expense is based 
on reported earnings before income taxes 
and thus includes the effects of timing differ-
ences between reported and taxable earn-
ings which arise because certain transactions 
are included in taxable earnings in other 
years. The U.S. investment tax credit is deferred 
and amortized as a reduction of income tax 
expense over the average useful life of the ap-
plicable classes of property.

Litigation 
In January 1969, the Department of Justice 
filed a civil antitrust complaint against IBM 
under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, 
charging the Company with monopolizing 
commerce in "general purpose digital com-
puters" in the United States. The government 
requests that IBM be required to stop certain 
practices which it claims lead to a monopoly, 
and asks the court to give such other relief by 
way of divorcement, divestiture and reorganiza-
tion as it may deem necessary to dissipate the 
effects of the practices charged. 
In January 1972 and in March 1972, The 
Telex Corporation instituted two lawsuits 
against IBM alleging violations of the Federal 
antitrust laws. These complaints, which have 
been consolidated, seek injunctive relief and 
damages to be fixed by the court. 
In April 1970, Xerox Corporation filed a 
suit against IBM charging patent infringement 
and misuse of confidential information in 
connection with the IBM Copier. 
All of the foregoing cases were brought in 
the U.S. Federal district courts. IBM denies 
the charges in all of these cases and is engaged 
in preparing its defenses against them. 
In July 1972, the trial of the Greyhound 
Computer Corporation suit against IBM in the 
Federal district court resulted in a directed 
verdict in favor of IBM. The plaintiff sought 
damages and injunctive relief for alleged 
violations of the Federal antitrust laws and in 
connection with IBM's 1969 announcement 
changing the way it charges for and supports its 
data processing equipment. The Greyhound 
Computer Corporation has appealed this deci-
sion to the Federal Circuit Court of Appeals. 
IBM continues to deny the charges in this case 
and is engaged in contesting the appeal. 
In January 1973, the litigation between 
Control Data Corporation and IBM in the 
Federal district court was dismissed pursuant 
to an out-of-court settlement which included 
mutual releases of all pending claims between 
the parties. The principal terms of the settle-
ment provide that IBM: sell The Service 
Bureau Corporation to CDC for approximately 
$16,000,000 (SBC operations for 1972 are in-
cluded in the consolidated financial state-
ments and amounted to approximately 
$1,500,000 of net earnings on gross revenues of 
$63,000,000); not engage in the data services 
business in the United States for six years; 
contract for data processing services from SBC 
of approximately $5,000,000 per year for five 
years; contract for research and development 
work by CDC in the amount of $6,000,000 per 
year for five years with rights to products 
developed thereunder available to both parties; 
provide for reimbursement to SBC for retire-
ment and other fringe benefits of current SBC 
employees of approximately $2,600,000 per

IBM Employees Stock Purchase Plan 
Under the IBM Employees 1971 Stock 
Purchase Plan, employees on the annual offering 
date, each July i, who are not participants in a 
Stock Option Plan, may purchase IBM's unissued 
capital stock one share at a time through pay-
roll deductions not exceeding 10% of their 
compensation. The price an employee pays for 
a share of stock is equal to 85 % of the market 
price on the annual offering date or on the date 
he has accumulated enough money to buy the 
share—whichever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 762,717 shares in 
1972, for which $221,347,024 was paid to IBM 
and credited to the capital stock account. At 
December 31, 1972, 2,919,904 shares were re-
served for sale under the 1971 Plan.

Retirement Plans

utory retirement plan covering substantially

under which funds are deposited with trustees, 
reserves are provided, or annuities are purchased 
under group contracts. The cost of all plans 
totaled approximately $221 million for the year 
1972, compared with $170 million for the 
year 1971.
