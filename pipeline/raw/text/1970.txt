1970 was a year of contrasts for IBM. 
Significant increases in gross income and 
earnings from operations outside the United 
States and decreases in the results from 
domestic operations combined to produce a 
modest rate of growth in the Company's 
consohdated gross income and earnings. 
ConsoHdated gross income from worldwide 
operations amounted to $7,503,959,690, an 
increase of $306,664,249 over 1969. Earnings 
were $1,017,521,072, an $83,647,878 in-
crease over the previous year. 
Earnings amounted to $8.92 per share on 
114,095,398 shares, the average number of 
shares outstanding during the year. This 
compares with earnings of $8.21 per share 
on the 113,718,068 shares outstanding on 
December 31,1969. 
Operations outside the United States, in-
cluded in consolidated results, showed gross 
income of $2,933,234,084, an increase of 
$437,241,140 over 1969. Earnings from these 
operations were $512,520,318, an increase 
of $114,737,146 over the year before. 
1970 was a difficult year for U. S. business as 
a whole. The condition of the economy 
affected our customers and, in turn, affected 
IBM. Data processing equipment previously 
installed with customers on a rental basis 
was discontinued at a higher rate than in 1969. 
But the substantially lower level of outright 
sales of computer products, previously re-
ported, was the primary factor which contrib-
uted to the decline in domestic operations 
during 1970. 
On the other hand, the worldwide backlog 
of orders for data processing equipment is at 
a record high. Installations of new equip-
ment continued at about the same rate as the 
previous year, with a greater proportion 
being installed on a rental basis. This factor,

A Parade of Product Activity 
In the high-technology, service-oriented 
world in which IBM does business, leader-
ship requires a continuing flow of new 
products that fill or anticipate the needs of 
the customer. Among the many announce-
ments by IBM in 1970, these are some that 
made it a memorable product year: 
January 26: 
Terminal in a Briefcase 
The IBM 2721 Portable Audio Terminal fits 
into an attache case and enables a business-
man to dial his computer from any telephone. 
Using a keyboard, he can ask questions and 
the computer "answers" in audible English, 
selecting words from a pre-recorded vocab-
ulary. The keyboard incorporates the elastic 
switch technology developed by the 
Advanced Systems Development Division. 
April 2 1 : 
IBM Enters Copier Industry 
After more than four years of development 
effort, the Office Products Division intro-
duced the IBM Copier. Designed for conven-
ient use in an office environment, the copier 
uses an IBM-developed photoconductor 
that allows the machine to produce copies 
of consistent high quality on plain paper. 
June 30: 
IBM System/370 
Opens New Data Processing Era 
"The computer landmark for the 1970s," 
System/370 gives customers dramatically in-
creased performance for their data processing 
dollars. The advanced design of System/370 
provides high reliability and program compat-
ibility with IBM's famous System/360. 
Models 155 and 165 were announced along 
with the high-speed, large-capacity IBM 
3330 disk storage and the 2,000-line-per-
minute IBM 3211 printer.

System/3 70 Model 145-
a Breakthrough in Monohthics 
Less than three months after the initial 
System/370 announcement, IBM added Model 
145, the first general purpose business com-
puter to use monolithic circuits in all memory 
and logic functions. With this pioneering 
step, IBM further extended the performance-
per-dollar advantages of System/370 to 
many more intermediate computer users. As 
with the previously announced Models 155 
and 165, it features program compatibility 
with IBM's System/360 computer line. 
October 28: 
A New Model of System/3 
The Model 6, a new addition to IBM's 
System/3, brings new capabilities to this 
computer designed for small businesses, 
including the ability to process standard 
ledger cards. The system also makes it pos-
sible for the operator to communicate 
with the computer through a typewriter-like 
keyboard and see results on a television-
like visual display. The new model can use 
the small System/3 punched cards developed 
by the Information Records Division and 
announced last year. First customer installa-
tion of a Model 6 took place in December. 
October 28: 
New Low-cost Control 
Computer Announced 
The new IBM System/7, using monolithic 
circuits in its memory and logic functions, is 
a sensor-based computer designed to moni-
tor and control industrial and laboratory 
processes. It can take as many as 250,000 
readings a second and analyze them almost 
instantly. System/7 will operate by itself 
or in a network with IBM computers offering 
users a variety of ways to handle their real-
time applications. 
November 5: 
Keypunch with a Memory 
The keypunch, a basic input device for many 
years, was given an electronic memory capa-
bility in the IBM 129 Card Data Recorder. 
The monolithic memory can hold the contents 
of two 8o-column card records and six con-
trol cards. The new machine speeds the key-
punching process by making it possible to 
correct mistakes before data is actually 
punched into the card, and by allowing the 
operator to key information without wait-
ing for the next card to be positioned. 
November 5: 
Magnetic Tape Subsystem 
The IBM 3803/3420 Magnetic Tape Sub-
system features speed, flexibility and operator 
convenience. It can use tapes recorded at a 
number of densities, allows faster access to 
data, and provides several automatic tape 
handling features. The control unit, with 
monolithic circuitry, is half the size of 
earlier units and can handle up to eight tape 
drives. An innovative connection tech-
nique makes it possible to take any tape 
drive out of service without interrupt-
ing the operation of the others. 
November i o: 
Version 2 of Generalized 
Information System ( G i s / 2 ) 
More than 80 new program products were 
announced last year. One of them, Gis/2, is a 
significant step in IBM's continuing effort to 
give the user increased flexibility and power 
in creating and using a central collection 
of data. The Company's 1970 programming 
efforts also provided greater capabilities 
in time-sharing—the simultaneous use of 
a computer by a number of persons at 
remote locations. In addition, program prod-
ucts were announced for a wide range of 
specific applications for particular businesses 
and industries.

What does a lunar trajectory have in common with the migratory journey of a whale? 
Or a medical screening center in Florida with a nuclear accelerator in Chicago? 
Or a steel plant in Australia with a real estate office in Germany? 
These all represent human activities with questions to answer and problems to solve. And they demonstrate the 
human urge to know, to predict and to make right decisions. 
IBM's products enable people around the world to solve significant problems better, faster, more economically. 
On the following pages are some of the ways IBM customers used those products. 
The University of Florida's College of Med-
icine, in cooperation with private physicians, 
is now operating a multiphasic screening 
center to make preventive medical check-
ups easier and quicker. Information from 
health history questions and an extensive 
battery of tests, such as the hearing test 
pictured, is fed into an IBM computer which 
stores and reports them to the patient's 
physician for interpretation and follow-up.

The Personal Meaning 
of "New Product" 
The words "new product" were used quite often in IBM last year. But frequency of use never diminishes the excite-
ment and sense of accomplishment they send through the organization. In IBM, "new product" has a special meaning 
for everyone. 
To IBM's scientists, engineers and programmers, it's the culmination of years of research and development; to iBMers 
in manufacturing and related areas, it's a challenge to maintain schedules and quality while adapting to something 
new and different; to IBM salesmen, it's an opportunity to offer customers the most advanced and efficient equipment. 
The June 30 announcement of System/370 was a red-letter event. For hundreds of iBMers it was recognition 
that their development efforts were successful—for thousands more around the world it marked the beginning of 
new activity and new opportunity. The people here and on the following pages are representative of both groups.

Organization 
To conduct its business throughout the world, 
IBM is organized into 12 divisions and 
three wholly owned subsidiaries. 
Divisions: 
Advanced Systems Development: identifies and 
establishes the potential value of innova-
tive concepts that can lead IBM into new 
business areas for the future, and devel-
ops advanced systems, new technologies, 
product and application prototypes. 
Components: develops, manufactures and 
purchases electronic components used in 
IBM data processing systems. 
Data Processing: markets IBM's full line 
of information-handling systems, equip-
ment, program products, systems engi-
neering, education, custom contracts 
and other related services through offices 
across the United States. 
Federal Systems: concentrates on advanced 
technology and systems for the ground-
based, airborne and spaceborne infor-
mation-handling and control needs of 
the U.S. Government. 
Field Engineering: 
provides maintenance 
and related services for the Company's 
domestic information-handling systems, 
equipment, and programming systems. 
General Systems: develops and manufactures 
low-cost information-handling systems 
and equipment and provides related pro-
gramming systems support. 
Information Records: develops, manufactures 
and markets data processing cards, 
magnetic tape and cards, business forms 
and other consumable products used 
in information-handling systems. 
Office Products: develops, manufactures, 
markets and services electric typewriters, 
magnetic media typewriters, dictation 
equipment, direct-impression compos-
ing products, copying equipment, and 
related supplies. 
Real Estate and Construction: manages the 
selection and acquisition of sites, the de-
sign and construction of buildings, 
and the purchase or lease of facilities. 
Research: brings scientific understanding to 
bear on areas of Company interest 
through basic research and the develop-
ment of technologies of potential long-
range importance. 
Systems Development: develops, through a 
multinational effort, IBM's mid-range 
and large information-handling systems 
and associated peripheral equipment, 
including system-related programming 
support. 
Systems Manufacturing: 
manufactures 
System/360 and System/370 data proc-
essing systems and associated equipment. 
Subsidiaries: 
IBM World Trade Corporation: conducts 
IBM's business outside the United States 
directly or through subsidiaries. 
Science Research Associates, Inc.: develops and 
produces textbooks, educational kits, 
learning systems, guidance products, and 
a wide range of testing materials and 
services for elementary and secondary 
schools, colleges and industry. 
The Service Bureau Corporation: offers to 
American business time-sharing and 
other data processing services on a daily, 
weekly, monthly, or one-job basis.

Financial Review 
Gross Income 
Gross income from worldwide IBM opera-
tions amounted to $7,503,959,690 last 
year, an increase of $306,664,249 over the 
$7,197,295,441 total for 1969. 
During 1970, sales, service and rentals of 
data processing machines and systems 
accounted for approximately 7 9 % of gross 
income; other regular products and services 
accounted for 18%, while special products 
and services for United States space, 
defense and other agencies contributed 3%. 
Earnings 
The Company's earnings before taxes 
totaled $2,011,521,072, which was 
$32,647,878 more than the $1,978,873,194 
earned in 1969. 
U. S. Federal and foreign income taxes 
were $994,000,000 in 1970, a decrease of 
$51,000,000 from the $1,045,000,000 in 
1969. 
IBM's earnings after taxes were 
$1,017,521,072, or $83,647,878 more than 
the $933,873,194 earned in 1969. 
IBM's earnings in 1970 equaled $8.92 per 
share on 114,095,398 shares, the average 
number outstanding during the year. 
This compares with $8.21 per share for 1969 
based on the 113,718,068 shares outstand-
ing at December 31,1969. Net earnings for 
1969 would have been $8.24 per share 
computed on the average number of shares 
outstanding during that year. Because of 
the minor effect of this change in the basis 
for computation, earnings per share for 
years prior to 1970 have not been restated 
in the statement of earnings or the com-
parative statement of operations for the past 
ten years. 
Cash Dividends 
Cash dividends totaling $547,604,277 
were paid to stockholders in 1970 compared 
with $407,825,977 in the previous year. 
The regular quarterly cash dividend pay-
able March 10,1971, will be at the rate of $1.30 
per share, an increase of $.10 a share over 
the previous rate. This dividend will be IBM's 
224th consecutive quarterly cash dividend. 
Capital Expenditures 
During 1970, IBM's growing business, 
together with its need to replace obsolete 
equipment, required a worldwide investment 
of $2,160,454,284 in plant, rental machines 
and other property. 
Retirements amounted to $634,785,020, 
covering obsolete and dismantled equip-
ment, as well as rental machines sold which 
previously were under lease to customers. 
These write-offs were charged against 
reserves provided out of prior and current 
years' earnings, or to cost of sales.

Litigation:

filed a civil antitrust complaint against IBM 
under Section 2 of the Sherman Antitrust Act, 
charging the Company with monopolizing 
commerce in general purpose digital com-
puters in the United States. The government 
requests that IBM be forced to stop certain 
practices which it claims lead to a monopoly, 
and asks the court to give such other relief 
by way of divorcement, divestiture and reor-
ganization as it may deem necessary to 
dissipate the effects of the practices charged. 
In December 1968, Control Data Cor-
poration instituted a lawsuit against IBM 
alleging violations of the Federal antitrust 
laws. In September 1970, Greyhound Com-
puter Corporation, which had earlier filed 
a lawsuit in connection with IBM's 1969 
announcement changing the way it charges

Stock Purchase Plan for IBM 
Employees: 
Under the IBM Employees 1966 Stock Pur-
chase Plan, employees on the annual offering 
date, each July i, who are not participants 
in a Stock Option Plan, may purchase IBM's 
unissued capital stock one share at a time 
through payroll deductions not exceeding 
10% of their compensation. The price an 
employee pays for a share of stock is equal to 
8 5 % of the market price on the annual 
offering date or on the date he has accumu-
lated enough money to buy the share— 
whichever price is lower. 
Employees purchased 822,977 shares in 
1970, for which $193,535,447 was paid to 
IBM and credited to the capital stock account. 
At December 31,1970,1,900,952 shares 
were reserved for sale under the 1966 Plan. 
Stock Option 
Plans: 
In April 1966, stockholders approved 
the continuation of a Stock Option Plan for 
officers and other key employees. Under 
the 1966 Plan, as under the 1956 and 1961 
Plans, options may be granted for the 
purchase of IBM's unissued capital stock at 
100% of the market price on the day the 
option is granted. 
Options granted after January i, 1964 
run for a five-year term and purchases can 
only be made in installments commencing 
one year after the date of grant and extend-
ing over a period of the next four years. 
Options granted prior to January 1,1964 
under earlier Plans have a term of ten years 
and purchases can only be made in install-
ments commencing two years after the date 
of grant and extending over a period of 
the next eight years.
