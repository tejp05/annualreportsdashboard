Letter to Stockholders 
Earnings, revenues and shipments for 1987 
exceeded those of 1986, demonstrating 
that our continuing efforts to make IBM 
more competitive are beginning to pay off 
Worldwide net earnings for 1987 were 
up 9.8 percent to $5,258 million from $4,789 
million in 1986. Net earnings were $ 8.72 
per share, compared with $781 per share for 
1986, while the after-tax margin was 9.7 
percent, compared with 9.3 percent. Average 
shares outstanding were 603.0 million in 
1987 and 612.8 million in 1986. 
Earnings before income taxes were 
$8,609 million in 1987 and $8,389 million in 
1986. Before-tax margins were 15.9 percent 
in 1987 and 16.4 percent in 1986. 
Worldwide gross income increased 5.8 
percent to $54,217 million in 1987 from the 
prior year's $51,250 million. It is estimated 
that the lower value of the dollar, compared 
with 1986 rates, improved 1987 gross 
income by $3,475 million and improved net 
earnings by $615 million. 
Gross income from non-U.S. operations 
in 1987, included in consolidated results, 
was $29,280 million, up 13.1 percent from 
$25,888 million in 1986. Non-U.S. net earn-
ings of $3,309 million in 1987 showed an 
increase of 3.7 percent over 1986's earnings of 
$3,193 million. 
For the quarter ended December 31, net 
earnings were $2,087 million, compared 
with $1,390 million for the same period last 
year, an increase of 50.2 percent. Per share 
earnings were $3.47, compared with $2.28 
for the 1986 period. Gross income for the 
fourth quarter was $18,011 million, compared 
with $16,945 million for the fourth quarter 
of 1986.

New Products: 
the building blocks for 
system solutions 
The information industry has undergone 
remarkable changes since computers entered 
the business environment. Back then, most 
of IBM's customers were large firms which 
relied on powerful computers to handle big, 
repetitive administrative tasks.Today, thanks 
to rapid advances in technology, customers 
from storefront businesses to global compa-
nies depend on a broad range of powerful 
and flexible information products and 
programs to address an immense variety of 
information-handling requirements. 
As information systems moved from the 
back room to the manufacturing floor, 
department and desktop, customers realized 
that to achieve even greater productivity, 
their information systems and products had 
to be able to communicate with each other. 
Applications developed for departmental 
systems, for example, had to be compatible 
with personal computers and mainframe 
systems. Increasingly, these customers 
sought integrated solutions that could 
address their total text, data, graphics, voice 
and image-processing needs. 
Responding to this rapidly evolving 
requirement, IBM introduced and shipped 
important new products in 1987. These 
products provide customers with the build-
ing blocks for systems and networks that can 
connect and communicate readily, yet are 
easily adaptable to individual needs. 
The IBM 9370 Information System, for 
example, extends the processing power and 
flexibility of the industry's most widely used 
mainframe programming architecture — 
System/370 — downward to small office and 
departmental environments for the first time. 
This compact mid-range system, which the 
company began shipping in volume in July, 
gives new customers the long-established 
compatibility of the System/370 family, as 
well as access to thousands of existing appli-
cation programs.The 9370 allows current 
IBM mainframe customers to make more 
efficient use of their existing programming 
skills and application program libraries. 
IBM directed sharper attention to 
program products in 1987, announcing a 
major, new unifying architecture. Systems 
Application Architecture (SAA)™ is designed 
to make application programs look and 
work in the same manner. 
The new architecture will make it easier 
for users to access and work with applica-
tion programs. A common communications 
support will enable customers to build 
networks of systems with greatly differing 
capabilities and to readily share data 
among them. SAA also provides a set of 
application building blocks that allows

1987 was a year of transition for IBM. Cost and 
expense actions began to yield results. The com-
pany is now able to achieve earnings growth with 
significantly less revenue growth than was needed 
two or three years ago. In the third quarter, the 
earnings per share year-over-year increased for 
the first time in five quarters; and in the fourth 
quarter, earnings per share increased 52.2 percent 
over the comparable period of 1986. 
Worldwide gross income increased 
5.8 percent in 1987 to $54.2 billion, following an 
increase of 2.4 percent in 1986 over 1985. Gross 
income from non-U.S. operations increased 13.1 
percent to $29.3 billion while revenue from U.S. 
operations declined 1.7 percent to $24.9 billion. 
Non-U.S. operations accounted for 54.0 percent 
of total gross income in 1987 compared with 
50.5 percent in 1986 and 43.0 percent in 1985. 
The strongest performance outside the U.S. con-
tinued to be in the Asia/Pacific area where 
gross income increased 16.5 percent in 1987 after 
a 30.0 percent increase in 1986 over 1985. The 
Europe/Middle East/Africa area also enjoyed 
good revenue growth, and the Americas area 
increased 70 percent in 1987 after a decline 
of i.o percent in 1986 from 1985. 
The value of the U.S. dollar continued to 
weaken in relation to most major currencies in 
1987 It is estimated that had currency rates 
remained constant with those of 1986, and had 
the direct and indirect effects of currency rate 
changes on business volumes, pricing and other 
operating decisions not occurred, worldwide 
gross income and net earnings in 1987 would 
have been lower by $ 3,475 million and $ 615 
million, respectively. 
Worldwide net earnings of $5.3 billion 
reflect a 9.8 percent increase over 1986, as com-
pared to a decrease of 26.9 percent in 1986 from 
1985. Net earnings from U.S. operations increased 
by 22.1 percent over 1986 and from non-US. 
operations by 3.7 percent. Earnings per share were 
$8.72 in 1987, $781 in 1986, and $10.67 in 1985. 
Gross income from sales, which accounted 
for 670 percent of total gross income, was $36.3 
billion, an increase of 6.0 percent over 1986. This 
follows a slight decline in 1986 compared to 1985. 
In 1987, revenue from program products contin-
ued to show a substantial increase over the pre-
ceding year, and now accounts for 12.6 percent of 
total gross income compared with 5.7 percent 
five years ago. The rate of growth in maintenance 
services gross income slowed in 1987 and declined 
in the U.S., while gross income from rentals 
and other services continued to decline.

classes of similar products or services is presented

6.3 percent in 1987 over 1986.This increase was 
led by the 3090 System including the new, top-
of-the-line IBM 3090 Model 600E and a new 
entry level 3090 processor, the Model 120E. Gross 
income from peripherals declined 6.0 percent in 
1987 following an 11.o percent decline in 1986

increased 10.6 percent in 1987.This increase is 
attributable to the success of the IBM Personal 
System/2, a family of computing products which 
offers customers significant improvements in 
performance, value and ease of use.

In December of 1987, the Financial Account-
ing Standards Board issued a new standard 
(SFAS 96) which will change the accounting for 
income taxes. A significant feature of the standard 
is the use of an approach under which recorded 
deferred taxes would be adjusted for changes 
in tax rates. Under the current approach (APB 11), 
deferred taxes are provided at current tax rates 
and are not adjusted for changes in those rates. 
Consequently, if income tax rates remain at their 
current level, implementation of SFAS 96 should 
result in a benefit to reported earnings since 
IBM's deferred tax liability was recorded in previ-
ous years at higher rates than those currently 
in effect. IBM anticipates implementing the new 
standard in 1988.

The consolidated financial statements include the accounts of International Business Machines Corporation and 
its U. S. and non-U. S. subsidiary companies, other than the wholly owned IBM Credit Corporation and non-U. S. finance 
subsidiaries, for which the equity method is used. Investments in joint ventures, and other companies in which 
IBM has a 20 to 50 percent ownership, are accounted for by the equity method. Investments of less than 20 percent are 
accounted for by the cost method. 
For non-U. S. subsidiaries which operate in a local currency environment, assets and liabilities are translated to U. S. dollars 
at year-end exchange rates. Income and expense items are translated at average rates of exchange prevailing during the 
year. Translation adjustments are accumulated in a separate component of stockholders' equity. For non-U. S. subsidiaries 
and branches which operate in U. S. dollars or whose economic environment is highly inflationary, inventories and 
plant, rental machines and other property are translated at approximate rates prevailing when acquired. All other assets 
and liabilities are translated at year-end exchange rates. Inventories charged to cost of sales and depreciation are 
remeasured at historical rates. All other income and expense items are translated at average rates of exchange prevailing 
during the year. Gains and losses which result from remeasurement are included in earnings. 
Gross income is recognized from sales or sales-type leases when the product is shipped, from program products when 
the program is shipped or as monthly license fees accrue, from maintenance and other services over the contractual period 
or as the services are performed, and from rentals under operating leases in the month in which they accrue. IBM 
equipment offered under capital leases by IBM's finance subsidiaries is accounted for by IBM as outright sales. 
Costs related to the conceptual formulation and design of licensed programs are expensed as research and development. 
Costs incurred subsequent to establishment of technological feasibility to produce the finished product are generally 
capitalized as program products assets. The annual amortization of these assets is the greater of the amount computed 
based on the estimated revenue distribution over the products' revenue-producing lives or the straight-line method, 
but not in excess of six years. Costs to support or service licensed programs are charged against income as incurred or when 
related revenue is recognized, whichever occurs first. 
Plant, rental machines and other property are carried at cost and depreciated over their estimated useful lives using 
the straight-line method. 
Goodwill: 
The excess of the cost over the fair value of the net assets of purchased businesses is recorded as goodwill and amortized 
on a straight-line basis over 20 years. 
Retirement Plans and 
Other Postretirement Benefits: 
Selling Expenses: 
Current service costs of retirement plans are accrued currently. Prior service costs resulting from improvements in 
the plans are amortized over the average remaining service period of employees expected to receive benefits. 
Postretirement health care and life insurance benefits are fully accrued when the employee retires. 
Selling expenses are charged against income as incurred. 
Income Taxes: 
Inventories: 
Income tax expense is based on reported earnings before income taxes. It thus includes the effects of timing differences 
between reported and taxable earnings that arise because certain transactions are included in taxable earnings in 
other years. Deferred investment tax credits are being amortized as a reduction of income tax expense over the average 
useful life of the applicable classes of property. 
Raw materials, operating supplies, finished goods and work in process are stated at the lower of average cost or market.

Stock Purchase Plan 
The employees stock purchase plan enables 
employees who are not participants in a stock 
option plan to purchase IBM's capital stock 
through payroll deductions of up to 10% of eli-
gible compensation.The price an employee 
pays for a share of stock is 85% of the average 
market price on the date the employee has accu-
mulated enough money to buy a share. 
During 1987, employees purchased 
5,467,411 shares, all of which were treasury 
shares, for which $ 661 million was paid to IBM. 
At December 31,1987,16,769,627 reserved 
unissued shares remain available for purchase. 
Stock Option Plans 
The stock option plans provide for granting 
officers and other key employees options 
to purchase IBM's capital stock at 100% of the 
market price on the date of grant. Options have 
a maximum duration of 10 years and become 
exercisable in up to four annual installments, 
commencing one year from date of grant. 
The plans are administered by the Execu-
tive Compensation Committee of the Board of 
Directors.The Committee may, at its discretion, 
grant Stock Appreciation Rights (SARs) to 
holders of options. Such rights offer optionees 
the alternative of electing not to exercise the 
related stock option, but to receive instead an 
amount in cash, stock, or a combination of cash 
and stock, equivalent to the difference between 
the option price and the average market price of 
IBM stock on the date of exercising the right. 
The plans also provide, subject to Com-
mittee approval, that payment by the optionee 
upon exercise of an option may be made using 
IBM stock as well as cash.

While IBM operates primarily in a single 
industry segment and manages its worldwide 
business as such, gross income has been 
categorized into major classes of product. 
For purposes of classifying similar prod-
ucts of the Information-Handling industry 
segment, user programmable equipment having 
the capability of manipulating data arithmeti-
cally or logically and making calculations, in a 
manner directly addressable by the user through 
the operation of a stored program, has been 
classified as Processors, except that small busi-
ness computers and intelligent workstations are 
included under Workstations.Typewriters have 
also been classified under Workstations. Periph-
erals include printers, copiers, storage and tele-
communication devices. Peripheral functions 
embedded in processors are classified with 
Processors. Maintenance Services consist of 
separately billed charges for maintenance. 
Program Products include both applications 
and systems software. Other consists principally 
of supplies and miscellaneous services. 
Some products logically fit in more than 
one class and are assigned to a specific class 
based on a variety of factors. Over time, prod-
ucts tend to overlap, merge into or split from 
existing classes as a result of changing technolo-
gies, market perceptions, and /or customer use. 
For example, market demand may create 
requirements for technological enhancements to 
permit a peripheral product (e.g., printer), to 
be functionally integrated with a display, a tele-
communication device and a processor to form a 
workstation. Such interchangeability and tech-
nological progress tend to make year to year 
comparisons less valid than they would be in an 
industry less subject to rapid change. 
Federal Systems consists of specialized 
information-handling products and services pri-
marily for the United States defense, space and 
other agencies. 
Other Business consists of training and test-
ing materials for school, home and industrial use.
