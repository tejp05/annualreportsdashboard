To the Stockholders.. 
John R. Opel, President, 
Frank 
T. Cary, Chairman

The year 1975 was one of records for both 
worldwide gross income and net earn-
ings. Gross income and earnings from oper-
ations outside the United States were well 
ahead of 1974, while U.S. operations resulted 
in a modest gain in gross income and a 
small decrease in earnings. The decrease in 
earnings in the U.S. was due principally 
to reduced outright purchases of data proc-
essing equipment and the continued 
effects of inflation on costs and expenses. 
Consolidated gross income from 
worldwide operations amounted to 
$14,436,541,062, an increase of $1,761,249,230 
over 1974. Net earnings were $1,989,876,966, 
an increase of $152,237,605 over the pre-
vious year. 
Earnings amounted to $13.35 per share 
on 149,044,427 shares, the average number 
of shares outstanding during the year. 
This compares with earnings of $12.47 per 
share on 147,400,733 shares, the average 
number outstanding in 1974. 
Gross income for the quarter ended De-
cember 31,1975 amounted to $4,068,094,413, 
an increase of $779,823,415 compared 
with the same period of 1974. Net earnings 
amounted to $588,639,351, or $142,100,544 
more than the fourth quarter of 1974. 
Operations outside the United States 
for the year 1975, included in the con-
solidated results, showed gross income of 
$7,271,473,429, an increase of $1,324,575,371 
over the year before. Net earnings from 
these operations were $1,105,713,738, an 
increase of $185,877,262 over 1974. This 
is the first year that gross income from oper-
ations outside the United States has ex-
ceeded that of the U.S. operations. 
Consolidated gross income from rentals 
and services in 1975 increased 17.8% over 
the previous year. 
Outright purchases of data processing 
equipment increased quarter by quarter 
during the yearfrom a relatively low amount 
in the first quarter to a record high in the 
fourth quarter, with the total for the year 
1975 slightly exceeding the previous record 
amount of 1974. However, the accelerat-
ing trend in these purchases experienced 
during 1975 is not expected to continue. 
Shipments were below 1974's record 
pace. Incoming orders improved as the year 
progressed and for the full year showed a 
small increase over 1974. The backlog at 
year-end is virtually unchanged from 1974. 
Inflationary rates throughout the world, 
while moderating somewhat in 1975, con-
tinue to run substantially above historic 
levels. During 1975, the company continued 
its efforts to offset the effects of these 
pressures through cost and expense control, 
increased productivity, and selective price 
changes. 
The operating results shown in this report 
reflect the fine efforts, loyalty and dedi-
cation of IBM people throughout the world 
in the face of an unusually difficult year. 
They have my admiration and gratitude for 
their hard work and for the high stan-
dards they have maintained in meeting the 
challenges of 1975. 
One long-time IBMer whose wise coun-
sel and services we will especially miss in 
the years ahead is T. Vincent Learson, retired 
Chairman of the Board, who has been 
appointed Ambassador-at-Large and Special 
Representative of the President for the 
Law of the Sea Conference and Chief of the 
Delegation. In conformity with federal law, 
Mr. Learson resigned from the IBM Board of 
Directors, where he had been a member 
of the Executive and Finance Committees, 
bringing to an end forty years of associa-
tion with IBM. I know I speak for all mem-
bers of the board in expressing our deep 
gratitude for his key contributions to IBM 
and in wishing him every success in his 
important new task. 
Productivity 
As reflected in a special section of this 
report, our customers have been most 
innovative in increasing their productivity

through creative new applications of IBM 
equipment. Such productivity increases 
are essential to any long range improve-
ments in the world economy, and we 
believe that data processing and word 
processing have a vital role to play in that 
improvement. 
In the past year, microminiaturization of 
electronic circuitry and other techno-
logical advances continued to push down 
the cost of computing in a wide variety 
of applications. 
A stream of new IBM products helped 
bring these savings to the marketplace. 
They include a new high-technology printer 
that can produce computer reports at 
13,360 lines per minute, six times faster than 
the fastest previous IBM printer; a new 
desk-top computer with computing capacity 
that, in the early days of data processing, 
would have required a room full of equip-
ment; and a new dictation system using 
magnetic disks for a long-recording capac-
ity. These and other new products are 
described later in this report. 
Satellites can play a major role in the 
future in bringing the problem-solving 
power of the computer to many locations 
and in holding down communications costs. 
In that regard, we are pleased that Aetna 
Life and Casualty became the third mem-
ber, with IBM and COMSAT, of a three-way 
partnership to establish a domestic satel-
lite communications system. Called Satellite 
Business Systems, the new firm petitioned 
the Federal Communications Commission 
in December for permission to establish and 
operate such a system. 
Full Employment 
IBM has instituted a number of programs 
in the past year to respond to opportunities 
in the marketplace and uncertainties in the 
economy. Retraining and voluntary reloca-
tion have created new career opportunities 
for some 3,800 IBMers worldwide, while 
another 1,900 people in the U.S. have left the 
company under provisions of a special op-
portunity program through which employ-
ees with 25 years of service may leave 
voluntarily, with up to four years of special 
compensation. As a result of these and 
other measures, the company has been 
able to avoid lay-offs, in keeping with our 
long tradition of full employment. 
Social Responsibility 
Both inside and outside the company, 
IBM continued its efforts to help provide 
equal opportunity for minorities, women 
and the disadvantaged. 
At the newly created Urban League Data 
Processing Training Center in Atlanta, 
IBM furnished a full-time teaching staff and 
data processing equipment, including a 
System/370. The program was modeled on 
the successful experience in Los Angeles, 
where more than 1,200 graduates have been 
trained in the past eight years for work 
as computer operators, programmers, key-
punch operators, secretaries and clerks. 
Similar programs, in cooperation with 
Opportunities Industrialization Centers of 
America, are in effect in Harlem and have 
been expanded significantly during the past 
year in Dallas. 
Continuing a program begun in 1968, 
IBM conducted management development 
seminars for key executives of social 
agencies. The three-day seminars last year 
were for agencies working with Spanish 
Americans in the Southwestern United 
States (SER-Jobs for Progress, and the 
League of United Latin American Citizens), 
Puerto Ricans in New York (ASPIRA of 
America, Inc.) and Navajo Indians in 
Arizona. 
An IBM computer center served as 
the "nerve center" of the Interagency Task 
Force for Indochina Refugees. The rapid 
response of IBM people in getting the com-
puter system operational helped relocate 
more than 130,000 refugees in less than 
eight months. IBM donated the use of a 
System/370, Model 158, together with a net-
work of video display terminals linking 
various agencies with the computer. The 
system helped match refugee skills and 
needs with the wants and interests of thou-
sands of sponsors. 
The Lawsuits 
The antitrust case brought against IBM 
by Telex has been disposed of without 
payment by either side. Telex initiated the 
proposal to settle, withdrew its appeal to 
the U.S. Supreme Court (to reverse a unani-
mous Circuit Court decision in favor of IBM), 
and agreed that the claims made against 
IBM by Telex cannot again be raised. IBM 
released Telex from the $18.5 million judg-
ment affirmed by the Tenth Circuit Court of 
Appeals on January 24,1975, involving 
misappropriation of trade secrets. However, 
the court injunctions against Telex related 
to such misappropriation by Telex remained 
in effect. 
The trial of the Justice Department's 
antitrust suit against IBM began in May. 
Thus far, only witnesses for the government 
have been called. It will probably be many 
more months before witnesses for IBM are 
heard. Nothing that has happened so far 
has dimmed my belief that our position is 
correct. 
The Future 
In all of the countries where IBM does 
business, we look to an improving economy 
in 1976 as countries which have lagged 
in their recovery begin to emerge from the 
recession. We see good potential for our 
products and services, worldwide. 
Ours is a young and dynamic industry, 
increasingly competitive around the world. 
The challenge of the future is to continue to 
apply ourtechnology and skills in providing 
still more effective tools for improving the 
world's productivity. We believe we have 
the people and the technology to make the 
most of the great opportunities ahead.

DP Product Group 
General Products Division...Worldwide develop-
ment and U.S. manufacturing responsibility for high 
performance storage systems including tape units, 
disk files, mass storage systems, as well as system 
printers, program products, programming languages 
and related programming. Its headquarters is in 
San Jose, California. It is responsible for operating 
manufacturing facilities at Campbell and San Jose, 
California. Development activities are conducted at 
Palo Alto and San Jose, California; Boulder, Colo-
rado; and Endicott, New York. It is supported by 
development units at Hursley, England; and Boeb-
lingen, West Germany. 
System Communications Division...Worldwide 
development and U.S. manufacturing responsibility 
for communications equipment, terminal products 
and related communications technologies and 
programming. Its headquarters is in Harrison, New 
York. It is responsible for operating a manufacturing 
facility and development laboratory at Raleigh, 
North Carolina. Development activities are also con-
ducted at Endicott, Kingston, Poughkeepsie, and 
Yorktown Heights, New York; Gaithersburg, Maryland; 
Los Gatos, California; and Manassas, Virginia. The 
division is supported by development units at Fujisawa, 
Japan; Hursley, England; La Gaude, France; and 
Uithoorn, The Netherlands. 
System Products Division...Worldwide responsi-
bility for systems definition and development (includ-
ing associated systems programming) and United 
States manufacturing responsibility for IBM's larger 
central processor products, including main storage 
and logic technologies, packaging technology, chan-
nels and cables, and standardized functional sub-
assemblies used in the products of other IBM 
organizations; development and implementation of 
advanced design and manufacturing support 
systems. Its headquarters is in Harrison, New York. 
It is responsible for operating manufacturing facil-
ities and development laboratories at Burlington, 
Vermont; East Fishkill, Poughkeepsie and Endicott, 
New York; and manufacturing facilities at Brooklyn 
and Kingston, New York. It is supported by develop-
ment units at Boeblingen, West Germany; Essonnes, 
France; and Vienna, Austria; and by a facility at 
Sterling Forest, New York. 
The Data Processing Product Group has 
worldwide development, U.S. manufactur-
ing, product assurance, and product line 
management responsibility for IBM's larger 
data processing systems and their associ-
ated communications, data storage, and ter-
minal products. The Group also has respon-
sibility for worldwide development and the 
U.S. manufacture of electronic components. 
A reorganization of the DP Product 
Group early in the year further strengthened 
IBM's ability to develop and build advanced 
products closely attuned to customer re-
quirements. 
Data Processing Systems 
The System Products Division was reor-
ganized in 1975 to achieve greater efficiency 
in product development and manufactur-
ing, and to bring together in close coordina-
tion the skills needed to meet increasing 
demand for data processing. The SPD mis-
sion now encompasses technology, 
product development, manufacturing, and 
most control programming support for 
the Division's product line. 
In 1975, improvements were announced 
in the System/370 Models 115 and 125, to 
be shipped in the first half of 1976; and in 
System/370 Models 158 and 168, shipped in 
1975. The improvements made available to 
customers a wide range of new data proces-
sing capabilities. In parallel with this hard-
ware development effort, SPD continued to 
provide control programming enhancements. 
SPD continued during 1975 as the prime 
supplier of components and subproducts 
critical to major IBM products marketed by 
other operating units-an effort ranging from 
technological innovation and development 
to the actual production of logic and memory 
modules, circuit cards and boards, power 
supplies, terminal printers and other func-
tional assemblies. 
Data Communications 
Data communications through comput-
ers and computer terminals, like the highly 
successful IBM 3270 Information Display 
System, are central to future growth of the 
data processing industry. To better serve 
this growing demand IBM formed the 
System Communications Division in 1975. 
The new division supports a broad range 
of IBM terminals, and announced several 
additions to its product line this year. 
- A new passbook printer, a family of fi-
nancial services terminals and two new key-
board displays that extend the use of the 
IBM 3600 Finance Communication System to 
the customer point of sale and to banks' 
administrative operations. 
- A number of important programming ad-
ditions to the control program that provides 
greater user flexibility for computer net-
work control and recovery. 
- A new version of the supermarket sys-
tem that extends advanced electronic check-
out capabilities to small and medium-sized 
supermarkets. 
Also introduced in 1975 were new ter-
minal printers and a new communication 
controller which coordinates data trans-
mission between computers and remotely 
attached terminals. 
Printers and Storage Media 
IBM's non-impact printer, the IBM 
3800, was announced in 1975. Combining 
laser technology and electrophotography, 
it produces computer reports at a rate 
of up to 13,360 lines per minute-over six 
times the speed of the fastest IBM printer 
previously in use. It prints in a variety of 
type styles and sizes and can create its own 
business formats as it prints, helping re-
duce the total cost of printing. A new fea-
ture enables the printer to separate, 
trim and stack printed sheets in one con-
tinuous operation. 
The second major development from 
the General Products Division in 1975 was a 
new approach to storing computer infor-
mation on magnetic disks. Called the IBM 
3350 Direct Access Storage Device, the 
new technology provides higher perform-
ance at lower cost. The new media can 
record, on disk surfaces, more than 3 million 
bits of data per square inch-the highest 
recording density of any IBM direct access 
storage device. 
GPD also developed a new impact printer 
for use as a remote output unit in a new com-
munication terminal. A speed of 1,000 lines 
per minute can be achieved and the printer 
can operate with any of 15 type sets.

New Product Installations 
Business turned to computers to help 
manage its resources more efficiently 
through the economic downturn. The Data 
Processing IVlarketing Group-focusing on 
advanced function and large data base sys-
tems-responded with a wide range of 
products and services designed to help 
customers improve their productivity. 
Data communications technology en-
abled the power of the computer to be 
extended directly to the user via remote ter-
minals-to the bank teller, the insurance

Users continued to benefit from Systems

enable retail merchants to quickly validate

customers a dramatic increase in printing 
speed and economy. 
Enhancements to four central process-

systems. 
The division's newest Field Support

In its network of distribution centers

Government Programs 
Hundreds of Federal Systems Division 
engineers, programmers and systems ana-
lysts teamed with the National Aeronautics 
and Space Administration for the Apollo/ 
Soyuz mission linking American astronauts 
and Soviet cosmonauts in space. This final 
flight of Apollo brought to a successful con-
clusion the National Aeronautics and Space 
Administration program that included six 
moon landings. 
A program which permits air traffic con-
trollers across the U. S. to track the flight 
of aircraft en route was completed for the 
Federal Aviation Administration. One of the 
largest and most complex efforts ever under-
taken by the Federal Systems Division, 
it permits data on course changes, weather, 
aircraft altitude, speed and heading to be 
displayed for controllers at 20 En Route Air-
Traffic Control Centers across the U.S. 
Customer Service 
The Field Engineering Division set up 
a new network of advanced IBM video ter-
minals to train its customer engineers 
and provide information to its management. 
During 1975, a typical customer engineer 
spent 16 days in training-either using 
this terminal network in his or her branch 
office or by attending an education center.

New Portable Computer 
A major new entry into the small com-
puter area is the IBM 5100 Portable Com-
puter, introduced in 1975. A compact, stand-
alone computer that weighs some 50 
pounds and can be moved readily from of-
fice to office, the 5100 brings data proc-
essing power within the reach of business-
men, engineers, financial analysts and 
others with or without expert knowledge of 
computers. It was developed at the 
General Systems Division laboratory in 
Rochester, Minnesota. 
Word Processing 
Products used in word processing-a 
program for improving the effectiveness and 
efficiency of business communications-
are an important segment of IBM's business. 
Several new products were introduced 
during the year that are expected to add sig-
nificantly to the efficiency and versatility 
of the word processing line. One of these is 
the new IBM 6:5 Cartridge Dictation System. 
It features a cartridge loaded with 25 mag-
netic disks, each capable of holding six min-
utes of dictation. The recording unit will 
hold two cartridges, permitting up to five 
hours' dictation without reloading. The 
Cartridge System can be fitted with control 
boxes to serve more complex dictation 
needs. 
Desk-top Composer 
The new desk-top IBM Electronic 
"Selectric" Composer has a memory that 
will retain and replay automatically up to 
8,000 characters of text. Of interest to com-
mercial printers as well as publishers of 
in-house material, the new machine prints 
at 150 words per minute, can easily re-
format any material entered, and is avail-
able with 125 type fonts. 
Blood Processor 
The IBM Blood Cell Processor is used to 
"wash" red cells to decrease potential trans-
fusion reactions and reduce the chance of 
post-transfusion hepatitis. Manufactured by 
the Information Records Division, the proc-
essor is used in community blood banks. 
Red Cross blood centers, civilian and 
military hospitals and research laboratories.

Research D i v i s i o n . . . Brings scientific understanding 
to bear on areas of company interest through basic re-
search and the development of technologies of po-
tential long-range importance. The Thomas J. Watson 
Research Center in Yorktown Heights, New York, 
one of the largest centers in the world for study of

A number of scientific discoveries and prog-
ress in advanced technologies were achieved 
by IBIVI's Research Division. 
In science, researchers discovered the 
first polymer that is superconducting-
that is, it loses all resistance to electric 
current flow at low temperatures. The dis-
covery may provide scientific guidelines 
in the search for polymers-materials made 
of long-chain molecules-which are super-
conducting at higher temperatures. If such 
materials can be found, they would revo-
lutionize electrical technology. 
A novel microscopic technique was de-
veloped which should be valuable for 
ultra-high resolution studies of biological 
specimens. The technique involves shining 
x-rays through the specimen into a photo-
sensitive plastic material to produce a rep-
lica of the specimen in the plastic. The 
replica is then viewed in a scanning electron 
microscope. The technique permits high-
resolution study of biological specimens in 
something close to their natural state. 
Some striking new mathematical results 
were obtained for calculations used to 
analyze and filter digital signals. The new ap-
proach permits a five-fold reduction in 
the number of multiplications required in 
many such calculations, as compared with 
the "fast Fourier transform" introduced 
several years ago. 
In technology, encouraging progress 
was made in developing circuits and mem-
ory arrays using a new type of switch called 
a Josephson junction. These devices, which 
operate at low temperature, have great 
long-range promise because of their ex-
tremely high speed and low heat generation. 
Using advanced fabrication techniques 
including electron beam lithography. 
Research technologists fabricated an 8,192-
bit field-effect transistor memory array hav-
ing a storage density of 5 million bits per 
square inch-seven to ten times the density 
previously reported (see cover photo). In 
addition to the high density of the array, it 
also operates faster and uses less power 
than previous FET memory arrays. 
An experimental computer language 
called Query by Example permits people with 
little or no knowledge of computers to 
extract information from large computerized 
data bases. It permits a user to locate in-
formation by giving examples of what he is 
looking for, rather than specifying the 
steps the computer should take in its search, 
as is necessary in conventional systems.

Toward 
a More Productive 
World 
The equation is timeless: our standard 
of living depends upon the bounty of nature, 
multiplied by our ability to enhance it with 
muscle, machines and brainpower. 
Productivity is a measure of how well we 
do that. It is a basic yardstick of economic 
progress. 
Worldwide, there is a gap between the 
desire for higher standards of living and 
the productivity levels that will make them 
possible. Productivity breakthroughs are 
important to any closing of that gap, to the 
efforts of the developing nations to solve 
the problems of poverty and hunger, and to 
the battle against inflation and recession. 
Historically, increased productivity has 
come not just from working harder, but from 
working smarter-the wheel, the lever, 
movable type and the steam engine are land-
mark examples. 
Modern technology has been respon-
sible for many of our productivity increases 
in recent years, especially in the manu-
facturing of goods and in the mining and 
petroleum industries. In the service indus-
tries-government, finance, health, educa-
tion, wholesale and retail trade and personal 
services-productivity increases through 
technology are more difficult to achieve. 
These areas are growing rapidly in the more 
advanced nations, however, and the op-
portunity for productivity gains is great. 
The computer is one of the basic tools of 
our age. It can speed the answers to diffi-
cult problems, and make complex situations 
more manageable. The same can be said 
of office products which simplify the man-
agement and production of paperwork. 
IBM's customers, through their creative 
application of such devices, are demonstra-
ting that every day. Computers are helping 
design engineers test concepts without 
building expensive prototypes. They are 
helping farmers raise more abundant crops 
by providing information on the best land-
use patterns. They are helping manu-
facturers increase production yields and 
improve product quality, while conserving 
raw materials and energy. They are help-
ing hospitals improve medical care by 
relieving doctors and nurses of many ad-
ministrative duties. 
Advances in technology have made the 
computer itself more productive. In 1952 
it cost $1.26 to do 100,000 multiplications on 
an IBM computer. Today they can be done 
for a penny on a modern large-scale com-
puter system. 
A dozen years ago even the least expen-
sive IBM computer was beyond the fi-
nancial reach of many smaller firms. Now, 
models with comparable capacity are 
available for a fraction of the cost and are 
proving useful for a great variety of tasks. 
IBM is applying the productivity of com-
puters and office products to its own 
operations, developing word processing 
and management information systems and 
applying them in areas ranging from the 
branch office to the production floor. 
Above all, IBM is dedicated to helping 
its customers make their own productiv-
ity breakthroughs. Their achievements have 
been great, and great potential remains 
for further gains. 
Productivity has been described as 
the world's biggest undeveloped resource. 
At IBM, our goal is to help customers 
develop it to its maximum. 
On the next five pages are pictures illus-
trating the sorts of tasks to which custom-
ers are applying IBM equipment to increase 
productivity. For example, a lumber com-
pany in the Pacific Northwest: here, an IBM 
sensor-based computer sorts fresh-cut 
boards according to grade, width and thick-
ness. It does so with an accuracy rate of 
99.5 percent-10 to 15 percent higher than 
possible before. In addition, the computer 
generates daily production tallies that can 
be examined by management to alter cut- > 
ting patterns in response to the demands of 
the marketplace. 
Other illustrations from around the 
world: the production of oil and grain; the 
manufacture of glass; banking; the test-
ing of intricate products; city planning; edu-
cation; police protection; the guidance 
of intercontinental tankers. These diverse 
activities are grouped here because of 
one common factor: all represent custom-
ers using IBM technologies to perform 
their work more effectively than they were 
able to perform it before.

The government continues to seek di-

competing organizations and that IBM be

olistic practices.

Gross income from sales, rentals and 
services increased 13.9% in 1975 and 15.3% 
in 1974, reflecting continued growth in all 
major areas of the business. 
Gross income from sales increased 
6.2% and 27.0% in 1975 and 1974, respec-
tively, and gross income from rentals and 
services increased 17.8% in 1975 and 10.1% 
in 1974. In 1974, gross income from sales 
increased at a higher rate than from rentals 
and services due to the significant in-
crease in the level of outright purchases of 
data processing equipment. However, in 
1975 such purchases only slightly exceeded 
the 1974 level. 
Gross income from non-U. S. operations 
exceeded that of the U.S. operations for 
the first time, accounting for slightly over 
50% of the worldwide total. 
Total cost of sales, rentals and services 
increased 12.5% in 1975 and 13.3% in 
1974. Cost of sales increased 14.3% in 1975 
and 14.9% in 1974. The 1975 rate of in-
crease of cost of sales was greater than the 
comparable increase in gross income due 
to increased costs resulting from infla-
tion as well as changes in the mix of prod-
ucts which have different levels of cost. 
The cost of rentals and services increased 
11.8% in 1975 and 12.7% in 1974. The 
1975 rate of cost increase was lower than 
the comparable increase in gross income, 
whereas the 1974 increase was greater as a 
result of inflation and the changes in the 
mix of products and services which have 
different levels of cost. 
Selling, development and engineering, 
and general and administrative expenses 
increased 19.0% in 1975 as against 18.2% 
in 1974. The increased expenses resulted 
primarily from the growth in the business 
and from the continued effects of worldwide 
inflation. 
Interest on debt decreased by 9.4% 
in 1975 as a result of continued reduction of 
debt. The 28.8% reduction in interest ex-
pense in 1974 resulted primarily from the 
$300 million repayment of debt incurred to 
comply with regulations of the Office 
of Foreign Direct Investment of the U.S. De-
partment of Commerce. These regula-
tions expired in early 1974. 
Other income, principally interest from 
marketable securities, increased 5.8% 
over 1974 due to a larger average portfolio 
and the effect of revaluing certain securi-
ties at the lower of cost or market, which 
were partially offset by the effect of gener-
ally declining interest rates. Other income 
in 1974 exceeded 1973 by 26.2% principally 
as a result of increased interest earned 
due to higher interest rates and a larger 
average portfolio.

Questions and Answers 
There is a growing interest on the part of 
stockholders and the public in issues affect-
ing the corporation. Here are IBM's an-
swers to some frequently asked questions. 
Does IBM make political contributions? 
No. IBM has a worldwide policy against 
making political contributions, even in those 
countries where contributions are legal 
and customary. 
Why doesn't IBM pay higher cash dividends 
on its stock? 
To invest in the future growth of the 
business requires substantial amounts of 
capital, which is financed out of earnings 
retained in the business. In spite of this, 
IBM's dividend payments for the past 25 
years reflect an increase in the payout 
per share each year, after adjustments for 
stock dividends and splits-a total in-
crease of 69 percent in the past five years. 
Why doesn't IBM fight all lawsuits against 
it through the courts, instead of agreeing to 
settlements? 
We have to consider not only the often 
time-consuming uncertainties but the costs 
of litigation. Most settlements have cost us 
less than litigation through the courts would 
have cost us, even though we had won. 
Additionally, settlements may work in IBM's 
favor in other ways, as in the case of agree-
ment on the future use of patents. 
If a case cannot be settled on a basis we 
believe to be in the best interests of the 
company, then we do carry it through the 
courts to a final decision. 
What is IBM doing to help the quality of the 
environment? 
We are committed to conservation of 
materials, especially through recovery and 
reuse of treated water wherever possible. 
The company will invest more than $150 
million through 1979 to meet or exceed 
all applicable government environmental 
regulations, as well as its own stringent 
standards, at all locations. 
Is IBM doing anything about the energy 
problem? 
IBM is deeply committed to energy con-
servation, worldwide. In the United States 
for example, comparing 1975 usage with 
levels before our conservation program be-
gan, our estimated savings for the major 
plant, laboratory and headquarters loca-
tions were 35 percent for fuel and 27 percent 
for electricity. The energy savings since 
the start of the program in November, 1973, 
represent over $34,000,000. 
For new buildings, our objective is to 
reduce energy use by 30 to 40 percent in 
comparison with earlier buildings. This 
is being achieved by revising design criteria 
and using computer analysis and simula-
tion techniques to evaluate alternative 
architectural and building systems. 
We are installing System/7 computers 
to monitor and control energy at a num-
ber of major IBM locations worldwide. 
What steps has IBM taken to make sure 
that information about employees is not 
misused? 
Four basic practices governing the use 
of personal information about IBM em-
ployees were announced in the U.S. last 
October. 
The practices are: to collect, use and 
retain only personal information that is 
required for business or legal reasons; to 
provide employees with a means of ensur-
ing that their personal information in IBM 
personnel records is correct; to limit the 
internal availability of personal information 
about others to those with a business need 
to know; to release personal information 
outside IBM only with employee approval, 
except to verify employment or to satisfy 
legitimate investigatory or legal needs. 
Similar practices and procedures are in 
effect or under review in the other coun-
tries of the world where IBM does business. 
What is the representation of minorities and 
women in IBM in the United States? 
The company now has approximately 
17,200 employees from minority groups and 
26,850 women, representing 10.9 percent 
and 16.9 percent respectively of the total 
IBM population in the U.S. 
Did the number of minority representatives 
and women in management positions 
increase in 1975? 
In 1975, the number of minorities in man-
agement in the U.S. grew from 1,100 to 
more than 1,250, while women managers 
increased from 900 to 1,050. 
What is IBM doing to improve employment 
opportunities for handicapped people? 
IBM's long-standing efforts to provide 
appropriate job environments and special 
training for handicapped people have been 
formalized in the United States in the past 
year in accordance with new federal regula-
tions. Worldwide, current efforts include 
attention to employment and career devel-
opment and a continuing review of physical 
facilities at IBM locations to conform with 
local laws and policy of the IBM company. 
In the United States today, approximately 
5 percent of IBM's work force can be classi-
fied as handicapped. 
How is IBM helping to mark the 200th 
anniversary of the United States? 
An IBM grant helped make possible 
an exhibition, "The World of Franklin and 
Jefferson," designed for the American 
Revolution Bicentennial Administration by 
the Office of Charles and Ray Eames 
with the cooperation of the Metropolitan 
Museum of Art in New York. It was shown 
in Paris, Warsaw and London in 1975 and 
will appear in New York, Chicago and 
Los Angeles in 1976. In addition, IBM spon-
sored another exhibition, "America on 
Stage: 200 years of performing arts," which 
will be at the John F. Kennedy Center for 
the Performing Arts in Washington, D.C. 
throughout 1976. Other Bicentennial activi-
ties have included grants to the cities of 
Boston and Philadelphia; funding for the Na-
tional Committee for the Bicentennial Era 
and the New York Philharmonic's 1975 Euro-
pean tour; and support for "Operation 
Sail," an international regatta of sailing ships 
which will sail into New York harbor on 
July 4, 1976.
