Fresh, Pure Water...
One of Italy's largest petrochemical plants
desalts millions of cubic meters of seawater per
year. The process, which is controlled by an
IBM computer system, provides water for plant
use, without drawing on the scarce reserves
of Sardinia.

Mine Safety...
Right: In the depths of a silver mine, an instru-
ment for detecting rock movement alerts an
above-ground computer to potential trouble.
With sufficient warning, mining engineers can
analyze dangerous areas to design safer mining
methods.
Electric Power...
For the New York Power Pool, an IBM computer
system furnishes on-line intelligence on the
status of electric power. The eight-member Pool,
representing 99% of the electrical energy
generated in New York State, coordinates the
operation of electricity production and trans-
mission within the State, and coordinates power
exchanges between Power Pool members
and neighboring utilities in the U.S. and Canada.

Now, the Metric System...
In Atlanta, the seventh grade uses the 1974
SRA Mathematics Learning System, devel-

of users. Under the program, math students
will learn the metric system along with the cus-
tomary U.S. system of weights and measures.

Nobel Prizewinner:
Dr. Leo Esaki of IBM's Thomas J. Watson
Research Center is one of three 1973 recipients
of the Nobel Prize in Physics. Dr. Esaki's award
was based on Ph.D. studies he conducted in Japan
during the late 1950's while a physicist at the
Sony Corporation. His investigation of the elec-
tron tunneling phenomenon in semiconduc-
tors enabled him to show for the first time the
conditions under which tunneling could be
controlled.
Dr. Esaki shares half of this year's Nobel
Prize in Physics with Dr. Ivar Giaever of Gen-
eral Electric. The other half of the prize was
given to Dr. Brian Josephson of Cambridge
University. The work of all three men rec-
ognized by the Nobel Committee was in the
field of electron tunneling. Whereas Dr. Esaki's
work was in the area of semiconductors, that
of Drs. Giaever and Josephson, which followed
shortly after, was in the area of superconduc-
tors—materials that exhibit no resistance to elec-
tron flow at extremely low temperatures. Soon
after Drs. Josephson and Giaever reported their
results, an IBM researcher realized that their
findings could form the basis of a new type of
computer switch. The first device was built
at IBM Research in 1965 and work on the new
switches, now called Josephson junctions,
continues. Their present switching speed is con-

Products: As long anticipated, computer devices have

New Retail System...
At the retail store, a salesperson passes
a hand-held "wand" across a magnetically
encoded price tag to record a sale. A point-of-
sale terminal records information describing
the item and displays its price on a lighted panel.
To complete the purchase, it computes the
amount due and change to be returned, prints a
customer receipt and a journal tape for the
store. The system includes a unit that prints and
encodes data on price tags. By capturing this
information at the point of sale, a firm can de-
velop other applications such as inventory
control and credit authorization.
