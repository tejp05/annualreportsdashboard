To the Stockholders:
The past year was an eventful one for the IBM
Company.
Significant progress was made in the estab-
lished areas of our business, and in those new
areas that will help to determine IBM'S progress
in the years ahead.
The Company's gross income from its domestic
operations last year amounted to $1,694,295,547,
an increase of $258,242,462 over i960.
Earnings were $207,227,597, a $39,046,717
increase over the prior year.

IBM's business comes primarily from the rental
of its data processing machines. During 1961,
however, stockholders were informed of a sub-
stantial rise in the outright sale of this equipment
to our customers. Outright sales have the effect
of causing income to be realized immediately,
which on a rental basis would be realized gradu-
ally over future months and years. These sales,
therefore, have the effect of increasing current
earnings and decreasing future earnings. Stock-
holders' attention is again directed to the fact that
the higher proportion of outright sales rather
than rentals during the year does not represent
an accelerated long-term growth rate for the
Corporation.

Data Processmg Division:
The capabilities of IBM's most advanced comput-
ing systems, together with the knowledge of how
to put these systems to maximum use in solving
customer problems, is ushering in a new era of
data processing applications.
This era demands a higher degree of profes-
sionalism than ever before among the sales rep-
resentatives who initiate and develop customer
interest, the systems engineers who help our cus-
tomers study, define and develop solutions for
their problems, and the customer engineers who
install and maintain equipment at peak efficiency.
During a symposium in New York City last
year, IBM systems engineers from around the
world delivered seventy papers detailing new ap-
proaches to solving complex problems in indus-
try, government and science. The proceedings of
this—and other meetings to follow—point the way
to new uses for computers and to new markets.
Using the systems engineering approach, the
division expanded its position in the promising
process control field last year with the introduc-
tion of the IBM 1710 control system. The 1710
is designed to improve the efficiency of continu-
ous industrial operations such as those in the pe-
troleum, chemical, metal, pulp and paper, cement
and utilities fields.
Specially-developed IBM process control sys-
tems are regulating a 140,000-barrels-per-day
crude oil distillation still in Indiana, and a 40,000-
barrels-per-day fluid catalytic cracker in Califor-
nia. Another system is monitoring the production
of a paper-making machine in Idaho. And a steel
company is studying the applicability of a 1710
to blast furnace and strip milling processes.

Electric Typewriter Division:
The Electric Typewriter Division last year intro-
duced the revolutionary IBM SELECTRIC type-
writer that has met with unprecedented customer
approval. This new writing instrument repre-
sents the first major technological breakthrough
in the typewriter industry since IBM introduced
proportional spacing in 1940.
The SELECTRIC typewriter replaces the con-
ventional typewriter's metal type bars and mov-
ing paper carriage with a single sphere-shaped
element covered with 88 raised characters. The
element types by skimming across the paper in
the same way your hand does when you write.
This single element permits a typist to change
type styles in a few seconds by simply replacing
one typing sphere with another. The ribbon can
be changed in an instant by removing a cartridge
located in front of the typing sphere and snap-
ping a fresh one into place.
The new machine also has a mechanical stor-
age system that prevents the running together of
characters that occurs on regular typewriters when
two keys are struck almost simultaneously. If this
happens on a SELECTRIC typewriter, the system
allows the character struck first to print, while
automatically storing the other for a split-second
before typing it.

physics and implications in the development of
extremely fast computing devices.

mathematics and programming continue to dem-
onstrate the feasibility of expanding the versa-
tility and applicability of computers. One out-
standing effort in these areas resulted in simpli-

it to work on several different programs at the

ically the results of intermediate calculations to
various storage locations. Another significant
achievement utilized operations research tech-
niques to evolve larger—and hence more realistic
—models of business and military logistic systems
