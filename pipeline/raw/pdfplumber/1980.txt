The expansion of IBM's worldwide business
took many forms in 1980. Among the most visible
were the 8 million square feet of plant and
laboratory space under construction in 8 coun-
tries at the end of the year. During 1980, some
4 million square feet were completed.
As illustrated on the following pages,
the growth of IBM's operations in 1980 was also
reflected in:
The broader use of IBM products and services
by growing numbers of customers in the U.S.
and other countries.
Announcement of new IBM computing systems
and office products that offer improved price/per-
formance for customers of all sizes.
Expansion into higher-volume, lower-cost market-
ing channels.
A continuing flow of innovative contributions
by IBM men and women in research, engineering,
manufacturing, marketing and customer service.
Advanced new IBM technologies incorporated
into new products or under research for potential
future use.
Expansion to Meet Demand
Spurring IBM's physical growth is rising
product demand and the consequent need for
higher manufacturing output. Computer terminals,
for example, are now being produced at an
overall rate of hundreds of thousands a year at
IBM plants in Canada, Italy, Japan, Sweden, the
United Kingdom and the United States.
Semiconductors, the basic components of
advanced circuits in today's computers and office
products, are now being produced by the
millions each year in IBM plants in Burlington, Vt.;
East Fishkill, N.Y.; Manassas, Va.; Essonnes,
France; and Sindelfingen, West Germany. A semicon-
ductor production line is also under construction
in Yasu, Japan.
To serve growing customer needs, IBM increased
its worldwide marketing space in 1980 by more
than I million square feet through leasing and new
construction. An additional 3 million square feet
for marketing were under construction at
year-end.
New 1.7 million-square-foot IBM plant/laboratory
complex in Tucson, Ariz., for computer storage products
and non-impact printers is largest new site in IBM's
worldwide physical expansion program.

IBM support costs.

small computers were opened in Baltimore,

a cost-effective way to attract new business.
Business Computer Centers, where customers
can see demonstrations of small systems and

resolution characters and pictures.

Space and Defense Programs
The U.S. Navy's new ship/helicopter system
for defense against submarines and detection of
surface vessels was tested successfully in initial sea
trials off the New England coast in 1980. IBM
is the prime contractor for the system and is also
supplying signal processing equipment for both
the helicopters and ships.
IBM has been selected by the U. S. Air Force to
develop the computer command and control for
an extremely precise global navigation system.
An 18-satellite radio network will enable Air Force,
Navy, Army and other units to pinpoint positions
on the earth within 50 feet-much more accu-
rately than is possible today.
The satellites will circle the earth every 12
hours in a global pattern. Aircraft and surface units
equipped with special receiving and computing
devices will process satellite signals automatically.
IBM also received a separate contract to
modernize the U.S. Air Force's facility at Sunny-
vale, Calif., for control of a variety of satellite
missions. The facility can presently provide
command/control and support for about 40
satellites in orbit. That capability will be increased
significantly with the addition of new equipment,
software and procedures.
A new IBM laboratory in Manassas, Va., is
applying the cost and performance improvements
made in mass-produced commercial computer
circuits to the specialized needs of U.S. space and
defense agencies.
