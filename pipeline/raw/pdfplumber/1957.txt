To the Stockholders:
In 1957, its most successful year to date, IBM's gross income in the U. S. exceeded
the bilhon dollar level for the first time. Gross income amounted to $1,000,431,597,
an increase of $266,091,817 over the previous year.
Earnings were $89,291,589, a $20,507,079 increase over 1956.
Cash dividends of $25,407,174 were paid during the year, an increase of
$5,470,016 over the previous year.
Earnings per share were $7.73, based on the 11,552,460 shares outstanding after
both the stock split effected May 7, 1957, and the 1,050,223 shares sold through
the rights offering which ended June 10. In 1956, earnings were $6.55 per share on
the 10,502,237 shares outstanding December 31, 1956, as adjusted for the
May 7, 1957 stock split.
IBM World Trade Corporation, the wholly-owned subsidiary which handles
IBM's business outside the U. S., also had its most successful year. The World Trade
Corporation and its foreign subsidiaries attained gross income of $202,162,053
for the year, a $44,491,210 increase over 1956. Earnings were $27,205,855,
a $4,745,954 increase over the previous year. Operations outside the U. S. are not
included with the parent company's except to the extent of cash dividends received
from IBM World Trade Corporation which amounted to $6,000,000 in 1957,
an increase of $2,000,000 over 1956.
The Corporation's public offering of 1,050,223 additional shares of capital stock
last spring was well received. As a result, over $226,000,000 was made available
to help finance the Company's continued growth.
In recognition of the competitive nature of our industry—and looking to IBM's
future—the Corporation continued to expand its research and development functions.
In addition to specific product development work, increasing emphasis was
placed on basic research activities concerned with advanced new principles and
materials for incorporation into business and scientific machines of the future.
During the year the Company continued to progress in its program of
decentralization—the placing of each IBM product family in a separate operating
division, assisted and advised in its operations by a Corporate Staff. As a further part
of the decentralization program, three regional headquarters were established to
strengthen IBM sales and service operations throughout the country.

Data Processing Division:
Punched card and electronic data processing machine systems are widely employed in
business for cost accounting, production and inventory control, budgetary control,
billing and sales analysis. There are many other applications of IBM systems in such
major industries as banking, insurance, communications, chain stores, petroleum,
public utilities, transportation and manufacturing.
In local, state, and federal government operations, IBM data processing systems are
used for such functions as census-taking, motor vehicle registration, unemployment
compensation, conservation, police work, as well as general accounting tasks.
In science, IBM systems are helping in the solution of a wide range of problems
including nuclear physics, medicine, ballistic missiles, weather forecasting and aircraft
design.
Deliveries of the first production models of the IBM 305 RAMAC commenced in the
latter part of the year. This machine processes business transactions as they flow through
an office. Conventional equipment requires that records of business transactions be
gathered up in quantity and sorted before processing. Another, more powerful RAMAC
system—the IBM 650 RAMAC—is being put into volume production. The 650 RAMAC
combines the vast memory capacity of RAMAC data storage units with the powerful
calculating ability of the IBM 650 computer.
Among the major new products introduced in 1957 is the IBM 709 Electronic
Data Processing System. The IBM 709 is a large-scale digital computer which
handles both business and scientific problems of greatly increased size and com-
plexity. The versatility of this system will permit a more scientific approach by
businessmen to problems ranging from sales forecasting to optimum production
and shipping schedules.
Another advanced product is the IBM 705 III Electronic Data Processing System,
which is designed specifically to handle large quantities of business data. Applications
include inventory control, life insurance premium recording, and freight car record

The research activities of the division continue to emphasize the field of solid

The IBM program of providing leading colleges and universities with computers

Electric Typewriter Division:
Acceptance and use of the IBM Electric Typewriter, noted for dependability and ease
of operation, continued to grow during the year. There is a Standard model IBM
Electric Typewriter, and an Executive model with proportional spacing that produces
distinctive copy resembling printing.
In addition there are special purpose IBM typewriters such as the Input-Output
Typewriter, which automatically types results of work done by various machines and
recording devices in plants and laboratories.
IBM typewriter manufacturing operations were transferred to the new plant in
Lexington, Kentucky, during the year. A modern engineering laboratory devoted
exclusively to electric and electronic typewriter systems was also established at
that location.
Supplies Division:
Materials and supplies used in the operation of various types of IBM equipment are
handled by the Supplies Division. These supplies include primarily the punched cards
and magnetic tapes used in conjunction with IBM data processing systems.
The IBM punched card is involved in the everyday activities of most Americans.
The cards are widely used for such vital documents as birth certificates, gas and
electric bills. Government "E" bonds, postal money orders, motor vehicle registra-
tions, and checks.

Military Products Division:
IBM continued to play an important role during the year in the production of elec-
tronic defense equipment for the United States Government.
Shipment of special purpose electronic computers by the Military Products Division
for the SAGE continental air defense system of the Air Force is proceeding on schedule
from the Kingston, New York plant.
Another important IBM contribution to the U. S. defense establishment is the
development and construction of the electronic bombing and navigation system
known as BRANE for intercontinental bombers. Production of the BRANE system is
carried on in a new plant located at Owego, New York.
In addition to these major production contracts, the company is actively engaged
in research and development activities in connection with the defense effort.
Time Equipment Division:
Products of the Time Equipment Division are performing essential time recording
and control functions in a wide variety of locations such as offices, plants, stores,
commercial broadcasting stations, military training centers, schools, hospitals, and
other institutions. These products include clocks, time recorders, program signaling
equipment, and central control systems.
A new product introduced during the year was the IBM 8200 Time Punch, which
punches employee payroll and other labor cost data into IBM cards at the source of the
operation—thereby permitting subsequent recording and accounting functions to be
performed automatically. In contrast, conventional time recorders print payroll and
labor cost data on the cards, requiring manual computation, key punching and
verification.
Another new product announced during the year was the IBM 610 Auto-Point
Computer, a desk-side computer which handles a wide variety of engineering and
commercial problems. Examples are bridge and highway design; sales forecasting;
stress, flutter and vibration analyses encountered in jet aircraft design; matrix arith-
metic; actuarial computations; cam design; heat transfer calculations. An outstanding
feature of the 610 is simplicity of operation.
Special Engineering Products Division:
Special machines and systems which extend the use of regular IBM products to fulfill
special commercial and scientific requirements of customers are developed and pro-
duced by the Special Engineering Products Division, which during 1957 had its first
full year of operation.

The Service Bureau Corporation:
Data processing services are furnished on a contract basis to industry, science and
government by The Service Bureau Corporation, a wholly-owned but independently
operated subsidiary formed on January i, 1957. These activities previously were
furnished by the parent company. There are 82 branch office locations in principal
U. S. cities, each operating punched card accounting machine systems. Seventeen
of these service bureaus are also equipped with electronic data processing systems.
This Corporation performs a variety of data processing services for its customers,
including such applications as payroll, sales accounting, inventory control, cost dis-
tribution, statistical analysis and scientific computing.
Capital Expenditures:
During the year 1957, the Corporation's increased volume of business and replace-
ment of obsolete equipment required an investment of $326,336,537 in rental
machines and parts, factory and office equipment, land and buildings. Rental machines
sold, previously under lease to customers, and dismantled and obsolete equipment
amounting to $40,038,204 were written off and charged against cost of sales or
reserves provided out of prior and current years' earnings.
A plant and laboratory were completed at the Corporation's military products
facility at Owego, New York, providing 410,000 square feet of floor space and, as
a result of additional Air Force research and development contracts, further expan-
sion is under way which will add 158,000 square feet for engineering.and adminis-
tration. The Owego facilities are constructed under necessity certificates granted by
the U. S. Government.
Construction was completed during the year at San Jose, California, of manufac-
turing, laboratory and educational facilities comprising 456,000 square feet, utilized
primarily for the manufacture of data processing equipment.
A new plant comprising 568,000 square feet, which will include engineering
and educational facilities, is nearing completion at Rochester, Minnesota, with data
processing machines now being produced at that location.
Also nearing completion are school buildings at Endicott and Poughkeepsie, New
York, comprising 113,000 and 124,000 square feet respectively.
At Sherman, Texas, a 56,000 square foot plant has been erected for the manufacture
of supply items for our equipment. Plans were announced for construction of a similar
plant of 125,000 square feet at Dayton, New Jersey.
The Corporation has leased a 72,000 square foot plant near Burlington, Vermont,
for the manufacture of data processing machine components.
Electric typewriter manufacturing and engineering were transferred from Kingston,
New York, to Lexington, Kentucky, and a 727,000 square foot plant and engineering
laboratory is nearing completion at that location.

Common Stock Offering:
In the Corporation's first public offering of securities since 1925, 1,050,223 shares of
capital stock were offered to shareholders at a subscription price of $220 for each
new share, at the rate of one additional share for each ten shares held at the close of
business May 21, 1957. The subscription offer expired on June 10, 1957, and sub-
scriptions through the exercise of rights totaled 99.8% of the offering. The offering
was underwritten by a group of investment firms.
The additional equity financing, required for anticipated capital expenditures and
as working capital, resulted in net proceeds to the Corporation of approximately
$226,000,000 after payment of underwriting commissions and other expenses.

IBM World Trade Corporation:
All IBM business outside the United States, its territories and possessions is carried on
by the IBM World Trade Corporation and its foreign subsidiaries. The IBM World
Trade Corporation is a wholly-owned, independently-operated subsidiary of IBM. Sales
and service organizations are maintained in 84 countries. Manufacturing or assembly
plants are located in Argentina, Australia, Belgium, Brazil, Canada, France, West
Germany, Italy, Japan, The Netherlands, Norway, Sweden, Switzerland and the
United Kingdom. Construction began on a new plant in The Netherlands, and existing
plants in France, West Germany and Italy were enlarged during the year.
The Corporation's investment in and advances to IBM World Trade Corporation
increased during the year 1957 from $27,100,625 to 127,593,181 as a result of the
year's transactions. The total equity in IBM World Trade Corporation at December
31, 1957, including retained earnings, aggregated $137,909,059 compared with
$116,210,647 at December 31, 1956.
