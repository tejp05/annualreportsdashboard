IBM's Operations:
IBM's operations, with very minor exceptions, are in the field of information-handling
systems, equipment and services to solve the increasingly complex problems of business,
government, science, space exploration, defense, education, medicine, and many
other areas of human activity IBM's products include data processing machines and systems,
information processors, electric and electronic typewriters, copiers, dictation equipment,
educational and testing materials, and related supplies and services. Most products are both
leased and sold through IBM's worldwide marl<eting organizations.

Innovation. It is the key to growth in even/ part of IBM's business-from typewriters to advanced infornnation
systenns. It has dramatically lowered the cost of infornnation processing. And this lower cost not only attracts new
users of information products, it also produces greater use by present customers and stimulates the develop-
ment of entirely new applications. All of which increases the demand for IBM products.
Inflation in the United States has more than doubled most prices in the past 25 years. In spite of this inflation,
innovation has driven down the cost per computation by 180 times over the same period. But the pace
of innovation in information technology is not measured in quarter-centun/ segments. Innovation has cut the price
of main memoP/' in IBM's newest computers today to just one-sixth of what it was only two years ago.
Technological advance like this has resulted in significant productivity increases for our customers in business,
science and government.
The ultimate beneficiaries of innovation, of course, are people-in their health care, education, financial services,
energy conservation, shopping convenience and many other aspects of their daily lives.
Innovation must continue. IBM is part of an industn/ of thousands of companies in the U.S. and overseas-each one
competing hard to best meet the demands of a growing marketplace.
IBM has invested $8 billion in technical innovation over the past decade. That investment has produced
hundreds of new or improved products-typewriters, copiers, terminals, processors and programming support.
Behind each product has been the individual imagination and creativity of IBM men and women.Their efforts have
produced not only a steady flow of better products and better service to our customers but also improvements
in the operation of IBM itself-in manufacturing, in marketing and in administration.

Enhancements of large IBM computers give

Terminals for Many Needs
New and improved terminals broaden the
versatility of IBM computers.
Two new point-of-sale systems that operate
as electronic cash registers in retail stores
were announced by IBM in Januar/, 1979.
Both systems speed sales transactions, provide
a wide range of reports and can be linked to
computers. The IBM 3680 programmable store
system is designed for the experienced data
processing user with many small stores. The
IBM 5260 retail system is for the user with fewer
stores or for the first-time data processing user
The new IBM 3630 plant communication
system is a family of rugged terminals able to
operate in most industrial environments. The
system gathers up-to-the-minute data on work
in progress and distributes that information
wherever it is needed in an organization.
New IBM Electrocardiograph
The new IBM 5880 electrocardiograph
system is a low-cost diagnostic device that can
help physicians quickly detect many of the
abnormalities associated with heart disease.
Containing a small computer, the system can be
wheeled to a patient's bedside. Within minutes,
it can record and interpret an electrocardiogram
(ECG)-a graph of the electrical impulses that
cause the heart to beat. The system's report,
when reviewed by the physician, can help in diag-
nostic decisions. The IBM 5880 can be used
in hospitals, outpatient clinics and businesses New diskette storage unit for IBM computers

1 Equal Opportunity Activity
IBM's worldwide Equal Opportunity
Programs continued their emphasis in 1978 on
providing equal opportunities in employment,
training and advancement. Of the more than
16,000 employees hired in the U.S. during the
year, 38% were women and 20% were minori-
ties. At the end of 1978, IBM had 1,973 minority
managers and 1,780 women managers in the U.S
At present, IBM employees are on loan as
f: teachers at 21 predominantly minority colleges.
IBM also expanded its support of outside
programs for job training of minorities and handi-
capped persons in 1978. The company is now
aiding more than 300 such programs with loaned
equipment or personnel.
In addition, more than 50 employees are on
social service leaves, working with minority and
community organizations.
In cities across the country, IBM buys many
products and services from minority entrepre-
neurs-black, Asian, Hispanic and American
Indian. The number of minority-owned suppliers
to IBM has grown from 7 in 1968 to more
than 420 companies doing more than $35
million worth of business with IBM in 1978.

Additional accommodations for handi-
capped employees were installed at many IBM
locations in 1978. The new Santa Teresa,

signs, lowered telephones and water fountains,
and other forms of assistance. The design
of all future IBM buildings will have the aim of
reducing barriers for the handicapped.
