During 1992, the changes that are fundamentally reshaping the
worldwide computer industry accelerated. Customers showed a growing
preference for smaller computers and open systems, and placed
growing value on software, services, and integration skills. The increas-
ing pace of our participation in these opportunities was not enough
to offset generally weakened business conditions around the
world and severe competitive pressure on revenue, price, and profit
across our hardware businesses, especially large systems and
personal computers.
As a result, revenues declined and earnings from operations were down
sharply. And, in December, I reported to you that we took additional
restructuring charges to account for further reductions in our work force
and manufacturing capacity.
Those are the facts surrounding the financial results in this report.
About 1992, we are mindful of one fact above all: IBM's financial perform-
ance was not acceptable to us or to our stockholders.
To improve IBM's competitiveness and profitability, we are taking the
aggressive action required by the rapidly changing marketplace,
competitive, and financial realities we face - reallocating resources to
growth businesses, reducing costs, increasing the autonomy of our
business units.

During 1992, revenue from sources other than hardware accounted for
48 percent of IBM's total, up from 43 percent the year before. Services
revenue grew 32 percent year-to-year, outpacing industry growth.
IBM's services business is close to becoming the largest in the world,
and we did become the largest professional services company in
Europe in 1992.
The investment decisions we are making in our Manufacturing and
Development businesses reflect the marketplace shift from larger
to smaller computers, and the reality that industry-standard building
blocks are increasingly becoming the norm.
We are reallocating resources from maturing businesses, and increasing
our investments in the growth areas of client/server computing, net-
working, RISC technology and multimedia, together with the essential
software that underpins all of these areas. While overall spending is
being reallocated and reduced, our 1993 investments in research, devel-
opment, and engineering will maintain IBM's leadership. We expect
our time-to-market and returns will benefit from the synergies of common
building blocks and sharper focus.
Reducing costs Advances in technology are placing more function, power, and memory
in ever-smaller chips and devices. At the same time, customers have
established world-class quality at competitive prices as the starting
requirement to earn their business; and providing world-class value is
essential for winning the race. The combination means unrelenting
pressure on costs and on capacities.

There is no easy way through this period of wrenching transition
for IBM. Prompt, decisive action is essential to improve the profitability
of each of our businesses and the company as a whole. The long-

That is the case with the decision by the Board of Directors to reduce the

The IBM Corporation in the '90s
Unique businesses, combined strengths
Many companies are computer makers. Others are software developers,
systems integrators, networking specialists, or service companies.
IBM is each of these, and more - a federation of businesses dedicated to
helping customers work smarter, faster, and better. We do this with
technology and talent - advanced products and services, matched with a
unique understanding of how they can help, even inspire people to
think, innovate, educate, or entertain.
Our new business structure, now in its second year, has given IBM
greater flexibility for change and growth. Our principal businesses -
marketing and services organizations and an array of manufacturing and
development units - represent the industry's broadest spectrum of
expertise and technologies, brought together for customers in more than
140 countries. In the past year, each business has become more focused
and more responsive to its customers. The corporations challenge is
to orchestrate this federation, to balance our businesses' greater auton-
omy with the advantages of their combined strengths. This includes
a continuing examination of alternative forms of ownership.
These businesses, in turn, are responsible to the corporation for meet-
ing specific financial goals, measuring up in areas such as quality and
customer satisfaction, and maintaining the high business standards
that have made IBM a respected name among customers, investors, and
employees for decades. Individually, each of our businesses brings
distinct advantages to the marketplace. Together, they bring to customers
I an unmatched combination of technology and talent. Unique busi-
« nesses, combined strengths - that is today's IBM, qualified as no other

Alliances and Partnerships
Partners
The rush of new technology, the expense of staying on the leading edge,
the demands of customers, and worldwide competition have required
IBM and other high-tech companies to form a wide range of alliances
and partnerships.
The costs of developing the 256-million-bit memory chip (in a
three-way pact with Siemens and Toshiba) may reach a billion dollars.
With these stakes, sharing costs, risk, and knowledge is essential.
In a decade, IBM has moved from a do-it-all-yourself, inwardly directed
company to an enterprise that reaches out for new ideas and approaches
that mesh with its own talents and strengths. Today, IBM has more
than 20,000 business partnerships worldwide and more than 500 equity
alliances with agents, dealers, distributors, software firms, services
companies, and manufacturers.
The quest to satisfy customers has blurred traditional competitive
boundaries. Apple Computer is IBM's partner inTaligent to develop
object-oriented software, and in Kaleida to create multimedia standards.
Hewlett-Packard and IBM are developing and manufacturing high-
speed fiber-optic communications components. Wang and Mitsubishi
sell IBM systems under their logos, and Digital Equipment is a partner
in offering business recovery services.
Alliances not only divide high development and production costs, but
also reduce critical time-to-market, pool scarce human skills, pro-

Organization
Operations IBM's primary operating units include geographic units
that provide marketing, services, and support to their
customers, and manufacturing and development businesses
that have worldwide responsibility for product develop-
ment, manufacturing, and delivery to geographic market-
ing units and to other equipment manufacturers.
Geographic Units The geographic marketing and services units include
IBM Asia Pacific, IBM Europe/Middle East/Africa,
IBM Latin America, and IBM North America.
Business Units The business units and their responsibilities are:
Application Business Systems—develops and manufactures
processors and related software for small- and medium-
sized businesses, and departments of large companies.
Application Solutions—develops solutions that consist of
hardware, software, and services for a broad range of
industries, including manufacturing, finance, and the
public sector.
Enterprise Systems—provides enterprise-wide solutions
involving the development and manufacture of I BM's
largest general-purpose processors, operating systems,
systems software, and supercomputing offerings.
Networking Syslems—provides products and services for
operating and managing networks, including client/server
systems, that deliver information in any format—data,
voice, text, and image—to users.
I^ennant Systems Company—develops and manu-
factures I BM's printers and printing solutions, includ-
ing advanced-function printers, software, and
printing services.
Personal Systems—develops and manufactures personal
computers and high-performance workstations and
their operating systems, including client/server systems,
as well as multimedia, graphics, and display systems.
I^rogramming Systems—develops software for data
management, office systems decision support, and appli-
cation development, and the architecture that pro-
vides a consistent structure for software across all
our businesses. Manufactures and distributes software.
ADS TAR—develops and manufactures a range of
direct access storage devices, tape drives, and optical
storage devices, and their associated software support.
Technology Products—develops and manufactures
logic and memory chips and electronic circuit packaging
used in IBM products.
Research Research Division—advances fundamental science, devel-
ops expertise in strategic technologies, and works
with IBM businesses to leverage IBM's technical strengths.
IBM Credit Corporation IHM Credit Corporation—provides customer, dealer, and
other remarketer financing for IBM products and services
in the United States, as well as investment options such
as money market accounts to U.S. stockholders and money
market and mutual fund accounts to U.S. employees
and retirees.
