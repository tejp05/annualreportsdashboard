In Memoriam
T H O M AS J. W A T S ON
1874-1956
On June 19th, 1956, the Chairman of the IBM Board of Directors, Thomas
J. Watson, passed away at Roosevelt Hospital in New York. His leadership
and guidance were an inspiration and a comfort to everyone employed in
the business, regardless of position, since his coming to IBM in 1914. The
interest that he took in building the rather crude equipment comprising the
Company's line in 1914 to the very capable office automation machines so
widely used today was overshadowed by his deep interest in the men and
women in IBM. To build a company, Mr. Watson felt that one first had to
build men and women, unite them into a loyal, hard-driving team, and then
with quality products and service, a successful enterprise could be built.
We will always remember him for his pioneering in the development of
the Company's large and varied line of office machines and service, but
most of all we will remember him for the warm personality and friendship
which he showed to everyone with whom he worked, and for his deep and
abiding interest in the human relations of the Company. Everyone of us in
IBM feels fortunate in having had his leadership for so many years. We
face the future confident that the basic policies of business management
which built IBM to its present success can continue with appropriate
adjustments to carry us toward the new goals we see ahead.
THOMAS J. WATSON, JR.

To the Stockholders
The Corporation's gross income during the year amounted to $734,339,780, an increase
of $170,790,988 over the previous year. Earnings amounted to $13.10 per share,
compared with $10.64 in 1955, computed on the same number of shares. Cash
dividends amounting to $19,937,158 were paid during 1956, compared with $16,386,489
in 1955.
The Corporation produced and sold more of its products in 1956 than in any previous
year, and despite production increases, unfilled orders at the end of the year remained at
a high level.
The Corporation's new electronic products are finding increasing acceptance and use.
The 500th IBM 650 Magnetic Drum Data Processing Machine, an intermediate-sized
electronic computing system, and the 100th 700 series Electronic Data Processing
Machine, a large-scale system, were delivered to customers during the year. Among the
dramatic tasks planned for IBM electronic computing systems is the calculation and pre-
diction of the orbit of the earth satellite to be launched by the U. S. Navy in connection
with its Project VANGUARD. This work will be done on an IBM 704, which is capable
of making 40,000 calculations a second.
During 1956 the Corporation announced the development of electronic data process-
ing systems called RAMAC, which process business transactions as they occur rather
than accumulating them for later group processing. Also announced was an electric type-
writer with electronic tabulation; and an automatic production recording system which
gathers and records data from production lines, providing a needed link between the
manufacturing floor and management.
In addition to commercial product activities, the Corporation's military products
include construction of very large-scale computers for the vast electronic air warning sys-
tem known as SAGE, and production of advanced bombing and navigational systems
known as BRANE for the U. S. Air Force's Strategic Air Command.
The Corporation continued during the year to realign its organizational structure
and decentralize its operations by placing each product family in autonomous operating
divisions. The Data Processing Division, the Supplies Division, the Time Equipment
Division, and the Special Engineering Products Division were made autonomous operat-
ing divisions within the Corporation. This was a continuation of the process begun in
1955 with the establishment of the Electric Typewriter Division and Military Products
Division. Each division is responsible for the development, manufacture, sale or lease and
service of its products.
Further strength was given the organization during the year with the creation of a
Corporate Staff which, being separate from the operating organization, devotes its

===== PAGE 8 =====
efforts to planning and other non-operating matters and assists the operating executives
in solving any special problems in its various specialized areas.
As previously reported to stockholders, the Corporation entered into a consent decree
with the Department of Justice on January 25, 1956. During the remainder of the year,
administrative procedures required to carry out the terms of the decree were completed
by the Corporation. Accordingly, the Corporation has 1) offered to sell as well as lease its
data processing machines, 2) offered its tabulating machine and electronic data process-
ing machine patents on a reasonable royalty basis, 3) offered the required number of its
card presses for sale, and 4) commenced operation effective January 1,1957, of a wholly
owned subsidiary corporation known as The Service Bureau Corporation.
To maintain its leadership in civilian and military electronic systems, the Corporation
has continued to expand its research and development activities. In addition to the sev-
eral IBM laboratories presently in operation or under construction throughout the coun-
try, land has been purchased for the construction of a basic research laboratory at York-
town, New York. A laboratory devoted to scientific research established at Zurich,
Switzerland during the year, wholly staffed by European scientists, will enable the Cor-
poration to take advantage of scientific inventions abroad and extend scientific teamwork
on an international plane.
The retirement of John George Phillips as Vice Chairman after 38 years of distin-
guished service was announced in December, 1956. His loyal and extensive service to
this Corporation has been tremendously helpful to everyone in bringing IBM to its pres-
ent position. The Corporation is gratified at his willingness to continue as a member of
the Board of Directors and as Chairman of its Executive and Finance Committee.
A corporation is no better than the team of employees which makes up its personnel,
and this is particularly true in the IBM Company because of the nature of our products
and services. We record with gratitude the efforts, skill and loyalty of every member of
the IBM team on whom so much of our continued growth has depended.
The future markets for data processing machines, computing systems and office auto-
mation equipment of all types are indeed challenging. The office machine is increasingly
becoming a tool to free people from the monotony of repetitive tasks for more interesting
and creative work, and to extend the reach of the human mind to problems that can be
handled only by its ability to imagine and to create. We in the IBM Company believe that
this "releasing" of the human mind, on an ever increasing scale, adds dignity to our indi-
vidual jobs and to the role of our Corporation in the free enterprise system of the
United States.
By order of the Board of Directors,
January 29,1957 President

Gross and Net Income IBM Corporation gross income for the year 1956 from sales, service and
rentals in the United States amounted to $734,339,780, compared with

actively for rental machines produced since January 1, 1956, the Corpora-
tion changed its method of computing depreciation on such assets from
straight line to sum of the years-digits method. This change, which provides
greater depreciation in the earlier years of an asset's life and lower deprecia-
tion in later years, resulted in a reduction of net income after taxes amount-
ing to $4,264,688 for the year 1956.

Employees' Retirement An amendment to the Company's retirement plan was approved by stock-
Plan holders at the last annual stockholders' meeting, and the amended plan
has been approved by the Internal Revenue Service.
For the information of new stockholders, an employee retired after ten
or more years of service is provided a retirement income which is computed
in two ways—exclusively upon length of service, and based upon both length
of service and earnings — with the retiring employee receiving whichever
retirement income is greater.
Employees retiring at age 65 receive a retirement income, based on
length of service only, of from $110 for ten years of continuous service to
$275 a month for forty-five years of continuous service, including Social
Security. Based on earnings as well as length of service, an employee with
forty-five years of service whose average earnings have been $6,000 a year
would receive $293 a month including Social Security; $7,000 a year,
$341; with increased retirement income depending on remuneration, up to
a ceiling of $25,000 a year, including Social Security.

Litigation As stockholders were previously advised, during 1956 Sperry Rand Corpo-
ration withdrew its antitrust complaint filed December 27, 1955, and IBM
withdrew its counterclaim, charging patent infringement, filed June 6, 1956.
The Corporation and Sperry Rand on August 21,1956 entered into a non-
exclusive licensing agreement to exchange licenses to manufacture punched
card accounting machines and electronic data processing machines under
their respective patents and patent applications in existence as of October
1,1956. Based on IBM's greater production of these machines, IBM will pay
to Sperry Rand a fixed annual royalty of $1,250,000 for eight years as a
credit against production royalties, after which time no further royalty
payments will be due.
The two companies also agreed upon a procedure for settling patent
interferences and exchanging technical information with respect to punched
card accounting and electronic data processing machines announced or
released to production prior to October 1, 1956.

The 650 Data Processing Systems, which are intermediate-sized com-

scale electronic systems, are being employed at a continually increasing rate
by industry, government and science for general business and complex
scientific purposes.

Electric Typewriter The IBM Electric Typewriter, known throughout the world for its ease of
Division operation, appearance, dependable performance, and distinctive printing, is
available in an increased variety of models to meet the many typing require-
ments of customers. In addition to the widely used Standard model, there is
the Executive model with proportional spacing that produces letters resem-
bling fine book printing.
During the year the Electric Typewriter Division announced an electronic
"reading" device, available on the Standard model, which frees typists from
setting tabulating stops while typing the variety of business forms used in
offices. Special forms, preprinted with vertical lines of electrically conductive
ink, are sensed by the electronic device in the typewriter, and the carriage
is automatically positioned. The new device marks the first commercial
application of electronics to typewriters.
Also, a new model typewriter was introduced which automatically types
the results of work done by computers, scales, meters, and other measuring
and recording devices. Electronic signals emitted by the recording devices
are sensed within the typewriter, which operates at the rate of 120 words per
minute. The new typewriter may also be used to read information into data
processing systems.
Supplies Division The Supplies Division was made an autonomous operating unit within the
Corporation in March, 1956, to facilitate the growth of this important seg-
ment of the Corporation's operations. Supplies handled by this division are
those used in connection with the Company's various types of equipment.
Military Products Division The Military Products Division of IBM continues to perform a substantial
amount of defense work under direct contracts with the U. S. Government.
Among the major defense projects is the construction of complex elec-
tronic computers which play an integral part in the vast electronic air warn-
ing system known as SAGE. These computers automatically track invading
aircraft and calculate information concerning the most effective employment

===== PAGE 19 =====
of such defense weapons as guided missiles, anti-aircraft batteries and jet
interceptors. The Corporation continues to perform this vital work in coop-
eration with the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.
Another important phase of IBM defense work is the engineering and
production of the advanced electronic bombing and navigational system
known as BRANE. This equipment is in production and being delivered for
installation in the Boeing B-52 Stratofortress, which is the largest and fastest
bomber presently employed by the Strategic Air Command.
Time Equipment Division Time systems, which include clocks, time recorders, program signaling
equipment, and central control systems are developed, manufactured and
marketed by the Time Equipment Division. The Time Equipment Division
was established as an autonomous operating unit within the Corporation in
October, 1956. The products of the division are performing essential time
recording and control functions in a wide variety of institutional, govern-
mental, commercial and industrial establishments.
The Automatic Production Recording System was a significant new
product announced by the Time Equipment Division during the year. The
Production Recording System is capable of getting variable production line
information such as length, count, weight, pressure and temperature,
together with information identifying the product and process. The system
then automatically correlates, stores and records the information through
electric typewriters, punched tapes, or punched cards. The recorded data
may be entered directly into IBM Data Processing Systems for the prepara-
tion of management reports.
