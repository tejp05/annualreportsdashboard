To the Stockholders:
During 1965, the Company conducted the most intensive
engineering, manufacturing, and marketing effort in its
history to launch and expand the IBM System/360. This
new product family, spanning the field of data processing,
has met with unprecedented demand around the world.
• i if
All of IBM's divisions and subsidiaries made excellent
progress and contributed significantly to making 1965 an-
other record year. Consolidated gross income from world-
wide operations amounted to $3,572,824,719, an increase
of $333,465,138 over 1964.
Earnings were $476,902,490, a $45,742,724 increase
over the prior year.
Earnings per share were $13.54, based on the 35,224,914
shares outstanding at the end of the year. This compares
with earnings of $12.30 per share on the 35,048,259 shares
outstanding December 31,1964.
Foreign operations, which are included in consolidated
results, showed gross income of $1,085,505,751, an in-
crease of $152,105,432 over 1964. This is the first year
Chairman Thoinas J.Watson, Jr. IBM World Trade Corporation's gross income exceeded
the billion dollar mark. Earnings from foreign operations

over the year before.
Our industry continues to be dynamic and highly com-
petitive. Our business is the creation of machines and
methods to help find solutions to the increasingly complex
problems of business, government, science, space explora-
tion, education, medicine, and nearly every other area of
human endeavor. The expansion of System/360's capabili-
ties during 1965, together with other new products, serv-
ices, and applications, greatly increases our ability to help
solve customers' problems. Some of our new developments
are described in this report.
Meeting the challenge of System/360 and the increased
complexity of our business environment has made far-
reaching demands on all areas of IBM's operations. To meet
these increased requirements, more than 22,000 new em-
ployees joined the IBM organization during 1965, bring-
ing worldwide employment to more than 172,000. To in-
crease productive capacity, the Company undertook the
construction of more than three million square feet of
manufacturing space at IBM sites throughout the world.
Many of these facilities are already being utilized and the
rest will be ready for use this year.

Time-sharing is just one of the ways customers can use
communication terminals to enter and retrieve data from
computers. Another method is Tele-processing in which
terminals form a communications network to exchange
information with each other, or with a data processing
center located nearby or a great distance away. IBM offers
many different kinds of communication units so that cus-
tomers can select the ones best suited to their particular
needs. Last year this line of equipment was broadened still
further by the introduction of three new terminals.
The new 2740 and 2741 terminals are low-cost type-
writer units which enable anyone who can type to utilize
the capability of System/360. The 2740 is a general-pur-
pose inquiry station which enables a person to query a
computer for business data stored inside a system and ob-
tain an immediate response. The 2741, on the other hand,
is intended primarily for scientific applications and per-
mits persons at remote points to utilize the problem-solv-
ing capability of a computer on a time-sharing basis.
The third new terminal, the 2260 display station, en-
ables a person to retrieve data stored inside a computer
and rapidly display it on a television-like screen where he
can review it. Through the unit's typewriter-like keyboard,
the user also can change the data or enter new material
into the system.
Customers in many different fields are using IBM com-
munication systems to speed data between their comput-
ing centers and outlying locations. In the airlines industry,
for example, Pan American World Airways and Delta Air
Lines last year joined American Airlines in using IBM
computer-based reservation systems to serve customers
across the country and around the world. The systems auto-
matically compile and update such data as passengers'
names, telephone numbers, flight schedules, special meal
requests and baggage weights. IBM also has received orders
for System/360 reservation networks from Continental
Airlines and Frontier Airlines.

Advances in Research and Development
During 1965, IBM's research and development facilities
continued to create advanced information-handling tech-
niques of value to customers in many different fields, as
well as investigate new technologies which may be im-
portant in the creation of future products and services.
In the field of medicine, for example, a number of insti-
tutions are implementing new methods in medical re-
search and hospital patient care through the use of ad-
vanced data processing techniques. Mayo Clinic physicians
used a specially designed IBM monitoring system to detect
swift and subtle physiological changes in the condition of
more than 250 patients who underwent neurosurgery dur-
ing the year. Using data collected from sensing devices
attached to the patient, the system displays on a television
screen in the operating room changes in such things as
blood pressure, heartbeat and breathing rates, and body
temperature. IBM is working with several other leading
medical institutions on the development of computer-
based patient monitoring systems.
Last year, the National Cancer Institute and IBM an-
nounced the development of an experimental blood cell
separator for use in leukemia research. This device accepts
blood from a donor, breaks it down into its major compo-
nents, collects the white cells, and returns the plasma and
red cells to the donor. In another project, the Company
and Columbia University researchers created an advanced
mathematical model which enables a computer to simu-
late the performance of a human lung. This model will
enable doctors to simulate how patients being treated for
pulmonary diseases would react to various concentrations
of oxygen and other gases.
In the field of education, IBM began an experimental
program utilizing computer-assisted instruction methods
to train the customer engineers who service the Company's
data processing equipment. An IBM system at Poughkeep-
sie, New York, provided instruction to customer engineers
through remote terminals located at selected branch offices
across the country. This experimental program is the first
