To the Stockholders:
During the year 1959, IBM's gross income from domestic operations amounted
to $1,309,788,037, an increase of $137,999,838 over the previous year.
Earnings were $145,633,212, a $19,441,354 increase over 1958.
Earnings per share were $7.97, based on the 18,268,943 shares outstanding at the
end of the year. Earnings in 1958 were $6.93 per share on the 18,217,721 shares
outstanding December 31, 1958, adjusted for the 2V2% stock dividend paid
January 28, 1959, and the 50% stock spht-up effected May 5, 1959.
Cash dividends of $37,073,971 were paid during the year, an increase of
$6,308,979 over the previous year.
The IBM World Trade Corporation, a wholly-owned subsidiary which carries
on IBM's business outside the United States, had a gross income of $296,898,501 in
1959, an increase of $51,177,480 over 1958. Net income was $40,202,103,
a $6,187,364 increase over 1958. The IBM World Trade Corporation's financial
results are not included with the parent company's except to the extent of
the cash dividends and interest received. During 1959, the IBM Company received
$10,368,750 from this source, an increase of $2,368,750 over 1958.
IBM continues to meet strong competition from both new and established
competitors in the United States and abroad. The Company's success in moving
ahead in the face of its growing competition is directly attributable to the efforts and
abilities of its more than 94,000 employees throughout the world.
During 1959, IBM announced a number of new products created in its
development and engineering laboratories. One of the most important was the
medium-sized IBM 1401 electronic data processing system which features a new
high-speed printer and bridges the gap between standard punched card equipment
and larger computers. Another new product was the IBM 1620 scientific
computer which meets the need for a small machine that can swiftly perform
complex engineering and scientific computations.

Organization:
IBM's operations were further decentralized during 1959 to strengthen them for
growth in the years ahead. The Company's present organizational structure consists
of a centralized Corporate Staff which provides over-all planning and assists operating
executives in various specialized areas, and the following operating divisions and sub-
sidiary corporations:
• The Data Processing Division to market and service IBM punched card accounting
machines and electronic data processing equipment.
• The General Products Division to develop and manufacture intermediate and small
electronic data processing equipment, and punched card accounting machines.
• The Data Systems Division to develop and manufacture large-scale electronic data
processing equipment.
• The Advanced Systems Development Division to create systems that will pioneer
IBM's entry into new markets for information collection and processing equipment.
• The Federal Systems Division to be responsible for IBM's service to the U. S. Gov-
ernment in the field of specially developed electronic systems, and for coordinating our
service in the area of commercial data processing products.
• The Electric Typewriter Division to manufacture and market a full line of electric
typewriters, electronic typing calculators, and related supplies.
• The Supplies Division to provide punched cards, magnetic tapes and other products
for use with data processing machines.
• The Service Bureau Corporation which offers data processing services to customers
on a contract basis.
• The IBM World Trade Corporation which conducts IBM'S business outside the
United States.
Data Processing Division:
The Data Processing Division leases, sells and services IBM punched card accounting
machines and electronic data processing equipment to domestic customers. This equip-
ment is produced by the General Products Division and the Data Systems Division.
During i960, the Data Processing Division will be installing IBM'S new family of
data handling systems. All newly developed IBM computers employ transistors and
related solid state devices in line with the Company's policy of utilizing the most
advanced solid state technologies. These new systems will further broaden the spectrum
of IBM data processing equipment which customers are employing in an increasing
variety of applications.
A major manufacturer of electrical products, for example, uses an IBM 705 com-
puter to design giant power transformers which step up electrical voltages so they can
be transmitted over long distances. When specifications for a new transformer are fed
into the 705 via punched cards or tape, the computer swiftly selects the best design,

The U. S. Coast Guard uses an IBM RAMAC 305 to speed maritime search and rescue
operations. Each day the computer traces the course of every merchant vessel in an
area stretching from New England to the Gulf of Mexico. When a ship is in distress,
its latitude and longitude are immediately fed into the com.puter. The RAMAC then
determines which vessels are at that moment nearest the distressed ship so the Coast
Guard can advise them to participate in rescue operations.
During i960, this division will extend the Company's marketing program by open-
ing a series of Datacenters where IBM computers will be made available to businesses
on an hourly basis. Datacenter customers will supply their own programmers (people
who develop instructions for computers) and their own operators.
General Products Division:
The General Products Division develops and manufactures IBM's punched card
accounting machines, and its small and intermediate data processing systems.
This equipment includes IBM's standard punched card units, the IBM RAMAC 305,
which stores data from tens of thousands of records and permits users to randomly
select and revise any record almost instantly, the medium-sized 650 computer, and the
1200 series banking machines. Last year, the division introduced the 1401 data proc-
essing system, the 1620 scientific computer, and the 357 data collection system.
The new 1401 will bring the advantages of electronic data processing within the
range of customers who could not previously justify its use. Using punched cards,
magnetic tape, or both, the 1401 can perform many complex jobs and print results
at high speed using IBM's new chain printer. Smaller electric and gas utilities, insur-
ance companies, and manufacturing firms, for example, can use the versatile 1401
for accounting. Banks can use a 1401, linked with an IBM 1210 that sorts and reads
deposit slips and card or paper checks imprinted with magnetic ink, as a high-speed
check processing system. Users of large-scale IBM computers can also employ the
smaller 1401 to speed the preparation of information for these machines, and the
printing of their output in the form of reports and other documents.

Data Systems Division:
The development and manufacture of IBM'S large-scale electronic data processing
equipment is the responsibility of the Data Systems Division.
Data Systems builds the widely used IBM 700 series computers. The division has
also started producing the completely transistorized IBM 7090 and 7070 computers.
The first 7090s, which are the fastest scientific computers IBM has yet marketed com-
mercially, were delivered late last year for inclusion in the Air Force's Ballistic Missile
Early Warning System. Deliveries of the intermediately priced 7070 computers will
begin in mid-1960.
Several weeks ago, the division announced the development of the IBM 7080, an all-
transistorized computer designed for business use which can produce management
reports and process payrolls, inventory control, billing and similar accounting jobs
up to six times faster than the IBM 705 computer.
In i960. Data Systems will deliver the world's most powerful computing system to
the Atomic Energy Commission's Los Alamos Scientific Laboratory. Atomic scientists
working at this laboratory are continually examining new ways of building nuclear
equipment such as atomic power plants. To test whether any one particular design
will work efficiently may require 100 billion arithmetical computations, IBM'S new
computer, known by the name STRETCH, will be able to solve such massive problems
in just 20 hours.
One reason for the increased speed of STRETCH and the 7000 series computers,
is IBM's new electronic memory unit. This unit can hold tens of thousands of numbers,
and deliver any one of them to another part of the computer for processing in just over
two millionths of a second. These memory units take up only one-third the space of
their bulkier predecessors, yet are almost six times faster.

Federal Systems Division:
The Federal Systems Division is responsible for IBM's service to the U. S. Government

The division's technical skills are becoming increasingly important in the develop-

defense system.
Analysis of the technical and economic factors involved in the joint use 0 f air de fense and

Long-range study of an integrated control system for submarines. This system will improve

Electric Typewriter Division:
The introduction of the newly engineered and dramatically restyled IBM electric type-
writer in early 1959 helped raise this division's sales to a record high in 1959.
In the latter part of last year, the division took steps to further diversify its product
line by acquiring the rights to the development of dictation systems of the Peirce Wire
Recorder Corporation of Chicago, Illinois. The division expects to enter the dictating
systems field in about a year.
The IBM 632 electronic typing calculator, which was introduced in 1958, continued
to gain new customers last year. This machine, which a typist can operate after only
a very short period of instruction, combines electronic calculating with an IBM electric
typewriter in a single unit. The 632 speeds and simplifies the preparation of such
important business documents as shipping invoices and purchase orders.
In preparing a shipping invoice, for example, the IBM 632 operator types on the
document such variable information as the kind, quantity and unit price of the goods
being shipped. The 632 then multiplies the number of each item ordered by its price,
subtracts any discounts, adds any taxes, and automatically types the total on the invoice.
Detail and summary information contained in shipping invoices and other business
documents can be automatically punched into IBM cards for further processing by
combining the 632 with an IBM card punch.
IBM's typing calculators and the electric typewriters are produced at the division's
manufacturing plant and development laboratory in Lexington, Kentucky.
Supplies Division:
The Supplies Division provides customers with the punched cards, magnetic tapes,
and other materials used with IBM data processing equipment.
Many of these supplies form a familiar part of everyday life. Punched cards, for
example, are used as airline tickets, telegraph money orders, income tax withholding
forms, hunting and fishing licenses, checks and utility bills.
The division added several new items to its product line last year including a
""carbonless" paper which is used in sets of punched cards and eliminates the need
for carbon paper inserts, and "Durexcel" magnetic computer tape which combines out-
standing durability and reliability, and is engineered to meet the exacting requirements
of data processing equipment.

The Service Bureau Corporation:
The Service Bureau Corporation is a wholly-owned, but independently operated,
subsidiary of IBM that supplies both large and small companies with data processing
services on a contract basis.
Through its coast-to-coast network of 83 branch offices, SBC is able to put a wide
variety of data processing equipment and specialists to work assisting customers.
All SBC offices, for example, have complete punched card accounting installations,
and 16 of them have electronic data processing equipment as well. SBC Data Processing
Centers in Boston, New York and Los Angeles have available large-scale IBM com-
puters. SBC also has experienced specialists available to aid customers in finding solu-
tions to such problems as aircraft vibration, oil refinery scheduling, and inventory
control.
In 1959, SBC developed two new programs for meeting the specialized needs of
bankers and brokers. Branch offices in 16 cities began offering an automation plan for
performing the demand deposit accounting requirements of banks.
A brokerage operations center was opened in New York City's financial district to
help relieve medium and smaller brokerage houses of many paperwork chores such
as the confirmation of customer "buy" and "sell" orders, recording of daily transactions,
and preparation of customer statements.
Research:
At IBM research laboratories, company scientists, engineers and supporting personnel
are working on advanced technologies that will be needed to create the information
systems of tomorrow.
Today's most powerful computers utilize thousands of pea-sized transistors that
operate at far faster speeds than the many times larger vacuum tubes they replace.
IBM scientists are working on a number of new techniques that may dramatically
decrease the size of even these all-transistorized computers, while considerably improv-
ing their performance.
One of the devices being evaluated for possible use in these computers is the Esaki
diode. IBM scientists are studying these extremely small electronic devices to deter-
mine if they can be used to increase the speed and simplicity of computer circuits in
years ahead.

Long-Term Indebtedness:
Under an agreement entered into with The Prudential Insurance Company of America
on July 31, 1956, IBM was scheduled to borrow $75 million in the latter part of 1959
against promissory notes bearing a 3%% interest rate. Since its current working
capital position was considered adequate to finance the continued growth of the busi-
ness, the Corporation did not wish to further increase its long-term indebtedness.
Therefore, it was agreed with Prudential that IBM would not borrow the money.
This meant that the amount of IBM's long-term indebtedness on December 31,1959,
all of which is held by Prudential, was unchanged from a year earlier. It totaled $425
million and carried an average interest rate of 3.64%.
On January 4, i960, Prudential exercised its option to convert its I115 million
issue of 3%% notes due January i, 2052, into 25-year notes bearing a 3V^% interest
rate. IBM will retire these notes by a series of 20 equal annual payments to Prudential
beginning in January, 1966.

Stock Option Plan:
Under the terms of the Stock Option Plan approved by stockholders in 1956, options
may be granted to officers and other key employees to purchase the Corporation's stock
at 100% of the market price on the date of grant. Options may not be exercised until
two years after the date of grant and then become exercisable in installments over the
next eight years. In accordance with the Plan, options are adjusted to reflect any stock
dividends and split-ups subsequent to the grant.
During 1959, options were granted for 17,267 shares and terminated for 1,412
shares. A total of 11,784 shares were purchased during the year, for which the Corpo-
ration received 11,979,156. This amount was credited to the capital stock account.
At December 31, 1959, sixty-one officers and other key employees held options for
111,473 shares, of which 9,241 shares had become purchasable. Option prices, after
adjustment for stock dividends and split-ups, ranged from $137.70 to I448.00 per
share. A balance of 26,096 shares was available for future option grants.
Stock Purchase Plan for IBM Employees:
The IBM Employees 1958 Stock Purchase Plan, adopted by the stockholders in 1958,
was extended by the Board of Directors to be effective for the second plan year start-
ing July I, 1959.
All regular employees not under the Stock Option Plan who have completed one
year's continuous service are eligible to purchase IBM stock through payroll deductions
not exceeding 10% of their regular rate of pay. Employees can apply these deductions
toward the purchase of IBM shares at a price 15% below the market price at the time
the stock is bought.
On December 31, 1959, 456,398 shares of unissued capital stock were reserved for
sale under this Plan. During 1959, 33,419 shares were issued, for which $12,597,271
was paid to the Corporation and credited to the capital stock account.

IBM World Trade Corporation:
IBM's expanding business outside the United States, its territories and possessions, is
conducted by a wholly-owned subsidiary, IBM World Trade Corporation.
IBM World Trade employs over 29,000 people in 22 manufacturing plants, 4 labo-
ratories and 262 sales offices located in 87 countries.
Some highlights of IBM World Trade's growth last year were:
Initial production abroad of the IBM RAM AC 30^ accounting machine and the large-
scale IBM 70J computer.
Dedication of the European Educational Center in Blaricum, the Netherlands, where
courses in machine accounting and data processing conducted in English, French and
German are given to customers, employees and special professional groups.
Completion of a new headquarters building for IBM Belgium, and two new manufac-
turing plants that add to existing facilities in the Netherlands and Japan.
The Corporation's investment in and advances to the IBM World Trade Corporation
rose to 157,255,603 in 1959 from $28,605,480 the year before. The increase was
principally caused by a I28 million loan to IBM World Trade for the purpose of buy-
ing the minority stock interest in IBM United Kingdom Limited, a World Trade sub-
sidiary. The loan is for 20 years and carries a 5% interest rate.
The Company's total equity in IBM World Trade Corporation on December 31,
1959, including retained earnings, aggregated $223,588,323 compared with
$164,936,097 on December 31, 1958.
